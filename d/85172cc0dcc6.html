Abstract written by a model

This unit is Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang), translated by James Legge, and consists of Mencius’s counsel to rulers on benevolence, righteousness, and royal government. It begins with King Hui of Liang seeking profit, but Mencius insists that a kingdom must be guided by benevolence and righteousness rather than competition for gain. Through discussions of royal possessions, famine, taxes, warfare, and defeat, he argues that rulers who neglect hungry people are responsible for their suffering, while light burdens, secure livelihoods, education, and moral cultivation can win popular support and make a ruler invincible. Mencius similarly tells King Xiang of Liang that unity under one sway will follow a ruler who does not delight in killing. The central extended exchange is with King Xuan of Qi. Mencius identifies the king’s compassion for an ox as evidence that he can protect his people if he extends that feeling beyond animals. He repeatedly teaches that a ruler’s music, parks, wealth, beauty, tours, hunting, and joy must be shared with the people rather than purchased through their distress. He defines royal government as protecting livelihoods and the destitute, educating people in filial and fraternal duties, appointing and dismissing officials responsibly, and acting with the people’s judgment in serious cases. He rejects war as the means to Qi’s ambitions and distinguishes a true sovereign from a robber or ruffian who violates benevolence and righteousness. When Qi conquers Yan, Mencius makes possession conditional on Yan’s people’s approval and urges restoration, consultation, and withdrawal when Qi’s conduct provokes opposition. The later chapters extend these principles to Duke Mu, Duke Wen of Teng, and Duke Ping of Lu. Mencius explains that people will not defend officers who ignored their earlier misery, advises small Teng to prepare its defenses while pursuing goodness, and presents King Tai’s migration as an example of responding to necessity. The work ends with Meng Ke learning that Zang Cang prevented Duke Ping from visiting him; he accepts that Heaven determines whether a ruler will trust and employ him.

Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang) body

The unit identifies the work as Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang), translated by James Legge. It contains Part I of the longer document. No further events or teachings are provided.

Chapter 1 body

Mencius visits king Hui of Liang, who asks whether he has counsels to profit the kingdom. Mencius rejects profit as the guiding concern and presents benevolence and righteousness as his only topics. He warns that prioritizing profit would cause superiors and inferiors to compete for gain, endangering the kingdom. He teaches that benevolence includes not neglecting parents and righteousness includes not treating the sovereign as an afterthought.

Chapter 2 body

Mencius tells King Hui of Liang that wise and good princes can enjoy towers, ponds, animals, and fish because their people enjoy them too. He cites King Wen, whose people willingly built his marvellous tower and pond and rejoiced in them. Mencius contrasts this with Jie, whose people wished for his death, making it impossible for him to enjoy his possessions alone.

Chapter 3 body

King Hui of Liang describes his efforts to aid people during bad years and asks why his people do not increase while those of neighboring kingdoms do not decrease. Mencius compares his policies to soldiers who run away and then criticise those who run farther, saying that limited efforts do not distinguish him from other rulers. Mencius explains that proper management of agriculture, fisheries, forests, livestock, land, and education would allow people to live well and establish royal government. He then condemns King Hui of Liang for allowing animals to consume human food and leaving people to die from famine, urging him to stop blaming the year.

Chapter 4 body

King Hui of Liang asks Mencius for instruction about whether killing with a weapon differs from killing through government. Mencius argues that there is no difference and criticizes the king because his kitchen and stables are well supplied while his people are hungry and others die of famine. He compares this rule to leading beasts to devour men and invokes Zhong Ni's condemnation of using wooden images in burials to emphasize the cruelty of causing people to die of hunger.

Chapter 5 body

King Hui of Liang tells Mencius that Jin has suffered defeat by Qi, the death of his eldest son, territorial loss to Qin, and disgrace at the hands of Chu. He says these events have brought shame on his departed predecessors and asks how to wipe it away. Mencius advises benevolent government, light taxes and levies, limited punishments and fines, careful farming, and the cultivation of moral qualities. He says this could produce a people able to oppose Qin and Chu and allow King Hui to attain royal dignity, because the benevolent has no enemy.

Chapter 6 body

Mencius visited king Xiang of Liang and judged that the king did not appear sovereign or venerable. The king asked how the kingdom could be settled, and Mencius said it would be settled by being united under one sway. Mencius explained that a ruler who takes no pleasure in killing men could unite the kingdom, because all the people of the nation would support him. He compared this popular support to grain reviving after rain and water rushing downhill.

Chapter 7 body

Chapter 7 concerns Mencius advising King Xuan of Qi about royal government and the royal sway. Mencius argues that the king’s compassion in the incident of the ox and the bell shows that he is capable of love and protection of the people, but must extend that kindness from animals to the people. He teaches that benevolent government requires securing the livelihood of the people, treating elders and young people properly, and providing education in filial and fraternal duties. Mencius warns that Qi cannot achieve its desires through war and urges the king to establish benevolent government instead.

Chapter 1 body

Zhuang Bao tells Mencius that the king of Qi loves music, and Mencius says that a great love of music could bring Qi near to good government. The king explains that he prefers music suited to the present age, but Mencius says ancient and contemporary music have the same effect. Mencius shows that the people suffer when the king enjoys music and hunting while they are distressed, but rejoice when his pleasures are shared with them. He concludes that making pleasure common to the people and the king would bring royal sway.

Chapter 2 body

King Xuan of Qi asks Mencius about the size of king Wen's park and contrasts it with his own park. Mencius explains that king Wen's park was shared with grass-cutters, fuel-gatherers, and hunters, so the people considered it small. King Xuan's park is smaller, but strict prohibitions make it seem large to the people. Mencius says the park is like a pitfall in the middle of the kingdom because killing a deer there is treated as killing a person.

Chapter 3 body

King Xuan of Qi asks Mencius how to regulate relations with neighbouring kingdoms and admits that he loves valour. Mencius explains that a great state should serve a small one and a small state should serve a great one, according to Heaven’s will. He distinguishes common valour from the greater valour of king Wen and king Wu, whose anger brought repose to the people of the kingdom. Mencius urges king Xuan of Qi to use his valour to give repose to all the people.

Chapter 4 body

King Xuan of Qi asks Mencius whether men of talent and worth enjoy the same pleasures as rulers. Mencius says rulers should share their joy and sorrow with the people, because this sympathy can lead to royal dignity. He recounts how duke Jing of Qi asked minister Yan about making a tour of inspection like those of the ancient sovereigns. Minister Yan explained the proper purposes of such tours and criticized rulers who oppressed the people through extravagant travel, hunting, or drinking. Duke Jing accepted the advice, opened his granaries, and commissioned music celebrating a prince and minister who restrain and love each other.

Chapter 5 body

King Xuan of Qi asks Mencius whether to remove the Hall of Distinction and what true royal government involves. Mencius explains that true royal government protects the people, especially the four classes of destitute people, and describes king Wen's government of Qi. When the king admits that he is fond of wealth and beauty, Mencius argues that he can attain royal sway by enabling the people to satisfy those same desires. He cites examples involving Gong Liu, king Tai, and passages from the Book of Poetry.

Chapter 6 body

Mencius questions the king Xuan of Qi about how to deal with officials and friends who fail in their responsibilities. He presents a minister whose friend lets the minister’s wife and children suffer from cold and hunger, and the king says the friend should be cast off. He then asks about a chief criminal judge who cannot regulate the officers under him, and the king says the judge should be dismissed. When Mencius asks what should be done if the kingdom lacks good government, the king avoids answering by looking around and speaking of other matters.

Chapter 7 body

Mencius tells king Xuan of Qi that an ancient kingdom is defined by ministers from distinguished families, not lofty trees. He warns that the king lacks intimate ministers and does not recognize when those he advanced have departed. Mencius explains that appointments, dismissals, and executions should follow the judgment of the people only after the ruler investigates the case. The ruler must act this way to be the parent of the people.

Chapter 8 body

King Xuan of Qi asks Mencius whether Tang banished Jie and king Wu smote Zhou. Mencius confirms that this is recorded. The king then asks whether a minister may put his sovereign to death. Mencius explains that one who outrages benevolence is a robber and one who outrages righteousness is a ruffian, and says he has heard of the cutting off of the fellow Zhou, not of putting a sovereign to death.

Chapter 9 body

Mencius addresses king Xuan of Qi about how he evaluates useful materials and people. He compares selecting large trees for a mansion with employing a lapidary to cut and polish a valuable gem. Mencius argues that a man who has studied the principles of right government should likewise be allowed to apply them. He questions why the king tells such a man to set aside what he has learned when governing the State.

Chapter 10 body

The people of Qi attacked Yan and conquered it. King Xuan asked Mencius whether he should take possession of Yan, fearing calamities from Heaven if he did not. Mencius said that king Xuan should take possession only if the people of Yan would be pleased, and otherwise should not. He warned that if king Xuan made their situation more oppressive, the people of Yan would revolt again.

Chapter 11 body

Qi conquered Yan, after which the princes of the various States resolved to deliver Yan from Qi’s power. King Xuan asked Mencius how to prepare for their threatened attack, and Mencius explained that Qi’s treatment of Yan had turned the people and other states against it. Mencius contrasted this conduct with Tang’s benevolent conquest and advised King Xuan to restore the captives, stop removing Yan’s treasures, appoint a ruler chosen with the people’s consultation, and withdraw.

Chapter 12 body

The unit concerns a brush between Zou and Lu and a dispute brought by duke Mu to Mencius. Duke Mu says that thirty-three officers were killed and that none of the people died defending them, creating a dilemma about punishment. Mencius explains that the people had previously suffered while the state's granaries, treasuries, and arsenals remained full, and that the officers failed to report their distress. He advises duke Mu to practice benevolent government so that the people will love their superiors and die for their officers.

Chapter 13 body

Duke Wen of Teng asks Mencius whether Teng should serve Qi or Chu. Mencius says the proposed plan is beyond him and advises Duke Wen to dig deeper moats, build higher walls, and guard them well. He further advises preparing to die in defense and ensuring that the people will not leave Teng during an attack.

Chapter 14 body

Duke Wen of Teng asks Mencius how to respond because the people of Qi are going to fortify Xue. Mencius recounts that king Tai left Bin because of repeated incursions by northern barbarians and settled at the foot of mount Qi out of necessity. He tells the duke that if he does good, one of his descendants may attain royal dignity, while Heaven determines the accomplishment of the great result. Mencius says the duke should be strong to do good, because that is his business.

Chapter 15 body

Duke Wen of Teng asks Mencius how Teng can avoid suffering from the large kingdoms on either side of it. Mencius recounts how king Tai faced repeated incursions by the barbarians of the north despite serving them with goods and animals. King Tai left Bin, crossed mountain Liang, built a town at the foot of mount Qi, and dwelt there. The people of Pin followed him, while Mencius asks duke Wen to choose between leaving and remaining prepared to die for the kingdom.

Chapter 16 body

Duke Ping of Lu plans to visit Meng Ke but is stopped by his favourite, Zang Cang, who claims that Meng Ke’s observances during his second mourning exceeded those of the first. Yue Zheng corrects the duke, explaining that the differences reflected the distinction between poverty and wealth, not improper excess. Yue Zheng tells Meng Ke that Zang Cang prevented the visit, and Meng Ke says that Heaven determines whether a ruler will trust and employ him.
