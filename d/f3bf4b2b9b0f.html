Abstract written by a model

This record summarizes Book VI of The Analects of Confucius, a sequence of sayings and conversations centered on virtue, learning, capable disciples, government, and the superior man. Confucius repeatedly commends particular followers: Yung may be fit to be a prince; Yen Hui uniquely loves learning, avoids transferring anger and repeating faults, sustains virtue and joy amid poverty, and maintains perfect virtue longer than others; Chung-yu, Ts'ze, and Ch'iu can serve in government. He praises humility in Mang Chih-fan, integrity in Tan-t'ai Mieh-ming, and the balance of solid qualities with accomplishments that produces a virtuous person. Encounters with disciples explore practical judgment: Chung-kung distinguishes proper disregard of minor matters from careless ease; Yen Ch'iu is challenged for limiting himself; Tsze-hsia is urged to model the superior rather than mean man; and Tsai Wo learns that a superior man cannot be fooled into folly. The Master also responds to illness and death with grief for Po-niu and remembrance of Hui. His teachings define uprightness, wisdom, perfect virtue, learning, propriety, the Constant Mean, and differing capacities for receiving high subjects. Wisdom means earnestly fulfilling duties toward people while keeping distance from spiritual beings; perfect virtue prioritizes difficulty over success and, at its fullest, establishes and enlarges others as oneself. The book closes with Tsze-kung’s question about one who benefits everyone, leading Confucius to portray such a person as approaching sagehood.

The Analects of Confucius, Book VI (Yung Yey) / CHAP. I. 1. The Master said, 'There is Yung!-- He might occupy body

Confucius says that Yung might occupy the place of a prince. Chung-kung asks Confucius about Tsze-sang Po-tsze, whom Confucius says may pass because he does not mind small matters. Chung-kung argues that overlooking small matters is acceptable only when joined with reverential attention to business, but excessive when practiced as ease. Confucius agrees that Chung-kung’s words are right.

CHAP. II. The Duke Ai asked which of the disciples loved to body

Duke Ai asks Confucius which of the disciples loved to learn. Confucius identifies Yen Hui as someone who loved to learn. Yen Hui did not transfer his anger or repeat a fault. Confucius says Yen Hui’s appointed time was short, he died, and no one else has been heard of who loved to learn as he did.

CHAP. III. 1. Tsze-hwa being employed on a mission to Ch'i, body

Tsze-hwa is employed on a mission to Ch'i, and Zan requests grain for his mother. The Master first assigns her a fu, then an yu after Yen requests more, and Yen gives her five ping. The Master criticizes adding to the wealth of the rich and gives Yuan Sze nine hundred measures of grain, which he declines before the Master tells him to distribute them.

CHAP. IV. The Master, speaking of Chung-kung, said, 'If the body

The Master speaks about Chung-kung. He compares a red and horned calf of a brindled cow to something that may be rejected by people despite its qualities. He asks whether the spirits of the mountains and rivers would also put such a calf aside.

CHAP. V. The Master said, 'Such was Hui that for three months body

The Master says that Hui maintained a mind free from anything contrary to perfect virtue for three months. The Master contrasts Hui with others, who may attain this state for some days or some months, but not for as long. The unit concerns Hui, the Master, and perfect virtue.

CHAP. VI. Chi K'ang asked about Chung-yu, whether he was fit body

Chi K'ang asks the Master whether Chung-yu, Ts'ze, and Ch'iu are fit to be employed as officers of government. The Master says Chung-yu is a man of decision, Ts'ze a man of intelligence, and Ch'iu a man of various ability. He concludes that each would have no difficulty serving as an officer of government.

CHAP. VII. The chief of the Chi family sent to ask Min Tsze- body

The chief of the Chi family sent to ask Min Tsze-ch'ien to be governor of Pi. Min Tsze-ch'ien said the offer should be declined politely. He added that if someone came again with a second invitation, he would have to go and live on the banks of the Wan.

CHAP. VIII. Po-niu being ill, the Master went to ask for him. body

Po-niu is ill, and the Master goes to ask for him. The Master takes hold of Po-niu's hand through the window. He says that the sickness is killing Po-niu and calls it the appointment of Heaven. The Master laments that such a man should have such a sickness.

CHAP. IX. The Master said, 'Admirable indeed was the body

The Master praises Hui for his admirable virtue. Hui lives in a mean narrow lane with only a single bamboo dish of rice and a single gourd dish of drink. Although others could not have endured such distress, Hui does not allow it to affect his joy. The Master repeats that Hui’s virtue is admirable.

CHAP. X. Yen Ch'iu said, 'It is not that I do not delight in your body

Yen Ch'iu says that he delights in the doctrines but lacks sufficient strength. The Master responds that those whose strength is insufficient give over in the middle of the way. He says that Yen Ch'iu is now limiting himself.

CHAP. XI. The Master said to Tsze-hsia, 'Do you be a scholar body

The Master speaks to Tsze-hsia. He tells Tsze-hsia to be a scholar after the style of the superior man. He advises Tsze-hsia not to be a scholar after the style of the mean man.

CHAP. XII. Tsze-yu being governor of Wu-ch'ang, the Master body

Tsze-yu is governor of Wu-ch'ang. The Master asks him whether he has good men there. Tsze-yu identifies Tan-t'ai Mieh-ming as a good man. Tan-t'ai Mieh-ming never takes a short cut while walking and comes to Tsze-yu's office only on public business.

CHAP. XIII. The Master said, 'Mang Chih-fan does not boast of body

The Master says that Mang Chih-fan does not boast of his merit. During an occasion of flight, Mang Chih-fan was in the rear as they approached the gate. He whipped up his horse and claimed that he was not daring to be last, but that his horse would not advance.

CHAP. XIV. The Master said, 'Without the specious speech of body

The Master says that two qualities make someone difficult to avoid in the present age: the specious speech of the litanist T'o and the beauty of the prince Chao of Sung. He names the litanist T'o and the prince Chao of Sung as examples of these qualities. The statement concerns the difficulty of escape in the present age.

CHAP. XV. The Master said, 'Who can go out but by the door? body

The Master asks who can go out except through the door. He questions why men will not walk according to these ways.

CHAP. XVI. The Master said, 'Where the solid qualities are in body

The Master explains how solid qualities and accomplishments relate to different kinds of people. When solid qualities exceed accomplishments, the result is rusticity; when accomplishments exceed solid qualities, the result is the manners of a clerk. When the two are equally blended, the result is the man of virtue.

CHAP. XVII. The Master said, 'Man is born for uprightness. If body

The Master teaches that man is born for uprightness. He says that if a man loses his uprightness and continues to live, his escape from death is due only to good fortune.

CHAP. XVIII. The Master said, 'They who know the truth are body

The Master states that knowing the truth, loving the truth, and delighting in the truth are distinct levels of engagement. Those who know the truth are not equal to those who love it. Those who love the truth are not equal to those who delight in it.

CHAP. XIX. The Master said, 'To those whose talents are above body

The Master states that people whose talents are above mediocrity may be told the highest subjects. He states that people below mediocrity may not be told the highest subjects. The unit concerns how the Master distinguishes access to the highest subjects based on talent.

CHAP. XX. Fan Ch'ih asked what constituted wisdom. The body

Fan Ch'ih asks the Master what constitutes wisdom and perfect virtue. The Master says that wisdom consists in earnestly performing duties toward people while keeping aloof from spiritual beings. He says that perfect virtue consists in making the difficulty to be overcome one's first concern and treating success as secondary.

CHAP. XXI. The Master said, 'The wise find pleasure in water; body

The Master describes the wise and the virtuous. The wise find pleasure in water, are active, and are joyful. The virtuous find pleasure in hills, are tranquil, and are long-lived.

CHAP. XXII. The Master said, 'Ch'i, by one change, would come body

The Master describes the effects of one change on Ch'i and Lu. Ch'i, by one change, would come to the State of Lu. Lu, by one change, would come to a State where true principles predominated. The statement concerns the transformative potential of change in these states.

CHAP. XXIII. The Master said, 'A cornered vessel without body

The Master comments on a cornered vessel without corners. He calls it a strange cornered vessel twice.

CHAP. XXIV. Tsai Wo asked, saying, 'A benevolent man, body

Tsai Wo asks Confucius whether a benevolent man would go into a well after being told that someone was in it. Confucius questions why the man should do so. He explains that a superior man may be induced to go to the well but cannot be made to descend into it. A superior man may be imposed upon, but he cannot be fooled.

CHAP. XXV. The Master said, 'The superior man, extensively body

The Master explains how the superior man should conduct himself. The superior man studies all learning extensively and keeps himself under the restraint of the rules of propriety. Through this discipline, he may avoid overstepping what is right.

CHAP. XXVI. The Master having visited Nan-tsze, Tsze-lu was body

The Master visited Nan-tsze. Tsze-lu was displeased by the visit. The Master swore that if he had done improperly, Heaven should reject him.

CHAP. XXVII. The Master said, 'Perfect is the virtue which is body

The Master says that virtue is perfect when it follows the Constant Mean. He remarks that this virtue has rarely been practiced among the people for a long time. The unit concerns the rarity of practicing virtue according to the Constant Mean.

CHAP. XXVIII. 1. Tsze-kung said, 'Suppose the case of a man body

Tsze-kung asks the Master about a man who benefits the people extensively and assists everyone. The Master says such a man would have the qualities of a sage, noting that even Yao and Shun were concerned with this. The Master explains that perfect virtue involves establishing and enlarging others as one establishes and enlarges oneself, and judging others by what is near in oneself.
