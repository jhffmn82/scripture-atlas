The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach, or Ecclesiasticus

author unknown · 51 sections
The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach, or Ecclesiasticus, from the World English Bible, Catholic Edition, dedicated to the public domain by eBible.org. Shown here as plain text, with facts drawn from it. https://ebible.org/eng-web-c/

Abstract written by a model

This is the Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), a 51-chapter wisdom book of instruction, mostly addressed to 'my son'. Its central theme is that all wisdom comes from the Lord and that the fear of the Lord is its beginning, fullness, crown and root. Chapter 1 sets this out, and later chapters return to it repeatedly, including the claim that nothing surpasses fear of the Lord. The first and largest part is a collection of maxims and counsels. They urge patience, trust and endurance in testing, since gold is tried in fire. They cover honoring parents, humility, almsgiving and care for the poor, and strict discipline of children and slaves. They treat friendship, warning against false friends and self-interested advisers and praising the faithful friend, and they deal with prudent speech, with warnings against the double tongue, gossip, whispering and betrayed secrets. They also address caution toward the powerful and rich, lending and surety, moderation at table and with wine, health and sorrow, women and wives (the good wife praised, the wicked or unfaithful one condemned), the contrast of the wise man and the fool, and warnings against pride, sin and presuming on God's mercy. Chapters 15 to 17 stress that the Lord left man free to choose between life and death and that he rewards deeds yet accepts repentance. Chapter 24 is a high point in which Wisdom praises herself, comes to dwell in Israel and Jerusalem, and is identified with the law of Moses. Later chapters include a prayer for Israel and against foreign nations (36), honor for the physician and the scribe over the craftsman (38), reflections on the toil of all humanity and on death (40 and 41), and a hymn to creation's wonders (43). The final section, chapters 44 to 50, is a praise of famous ancestors. It names Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, Moses, Aaron, Phinehas, Joshua, Caleb, the judges, Samuel, Nathan, David, Solomon (whose bowing to women led to a divided kingdom), Elijah, Elisha, Hezekiah, Isaiah, Josiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, the twelve prophets, Zerubbabel, Jesus son of Josedek, Nehemiah, and Adam. It culminates in the high priest Simon son of Onias and his temple liturgy. The author then closes with a prayer for Israel and signs as Jesus, son of Sirach Eleazar, of Jerusalem, blessing whoever lays the book up in his heart. Chapter 51 ends with thanksgiving for rescue from death, slander and enemies, and with the author's account of seeking wisdom from youth. He invites the uneducated to her house of instruction, to take her yoke without money, and to do their work before the Lord gives their reward.

The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach, or Ecclesiasticus / Chapter 1 body

Chapter 1 of Sirach, the Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach, declares that all wisdom comes from the Lord and is with him forever, and that no one can count the sand, rain, or days of eternity or search out wisdom. The Lord, sitting on his throne, created, measured, and poured out wisdom upon all his works, giving her freely to those who love him. The chapter repeatedly identifies the fear of the Lord as the beginning, fullness, crown, and root of wisdom, bringing glory, gladness, length of days, peace, and a blessed death. It counsels patience, keeping the commandments, faith, humility, and an undivided heart. It warns against unjust wrath, hypocrisy, self-exaltation, and deceit, for the Lord will reveal the secrets of the proud and cast them down in the congregation.

Chapter 2 body

Chapter 2 addresses "My son" who comes to serve the Lord, warning him to prepare his soul for temptation, to endure, and to be patient in humiliation, since gold is tried in fire and acceptable men in the furnace of humiliation. It repeatedly urges all who fear the Lord to trust him, wait for his mercy, and hope for good things, pointing to past generations as proof that no one who trusted or feared him was ashamed or forsaken. The Lord is described as full of compassion and mercy, forgiving sins and saving in affliction. Woes are pronounced on fearful and faint hearts, on the sinner who goes two ways, and on those who have lost patience. The chapter closes by describing what those who fear and love the Lord do, and by saying it is better to fall into the Lord's hands than into the hands of men, because his mercy matches his majesty.

Chapter 3 body

Chapter 3 is a father's instruction to his children, urging them to honor their father and mother. Honoring parents is said to bring atonement for sins, joy in one's own children, length of days, and blessing, while forsaking or provoking them brings a curse from the Lord. The addressed son is told to help his father in old age and be patient if his understanding fails. The chapter then turns to humility, telling the son to stay humble, avoid seeking things too hard or secret, and warning that conceit, stubbornness, and pride lead to ruin. It closes with proverbs on wisdom, almsgiving that atones for sins, and repaying good turns.

Chapter 4 body

Chapter 4 addresses a 'son' with instructions on treating the poor and needy: he must not withhold living, delay giving, or turn away from those who ask, since the Maker hears the curse of an embittered poor man. He is told to rescue the wronged, give fair judgment, and be like a father to the fatherless and a husband to their mother, so that he will be as a son of the Most High. The chapter then praises Wisdom, who exalts her sons, blesses and gives glory to those who love and hold her, and who first tests them with discipline and fear before revealing her secrets, but forsakes those who stray. Finally, the son is given counsel on honest conduct: avoid sinful shame, show no partiality, speak when speech brings safety, confess sins, strive for the truth, be neither hasty in speech nor slack in deeds, and give as readily as he receives.

Chapter 5 body

Chapter 5 is a series of prohibitions and exhortations addressed to the reader about self-reliance, sin, and speech. It warns against trusting in one's goods or unrighteous gains, following one's own desires, and presuming on the Lord's patience, compassion, or atonement to keep sinning. It urges the reader not to delay turning to the Lord, since his wrath may come suddenly and bring destruction in the time of vengeance. The second half concerns speech: the reader is to be steadfast in understanding, consistent in speech, swift to hear, and patient in answering. It condemns the double-tongued sinner, the whisperer, and the one who lies in wait with the tongue, and it closes by telling the reader not to be ignorant in any matter, great or small.

Chapter 6 body

Chapter 6 warns against becoming an enemy through a double tongue and against self-exaltation, which leaves a person like a dry tree and a laughingstock. It then turns to friendship, advising the reader to test a friend before trusting him, since some friends last only for an occasion, turn into enemies, or abandon a person in affliction. A faithful friend is described as a strong defense, a treasure, and a life-saving medicine found by those who fear the Lord. The second half urges 'my son' to pursue wisdom from youth, accepting her fetters and bonds, which become a robe of glory and a crown of rejoicing. It closes by advising him to attach himself to wise elders, seek out men of understanding, and meditate on the Lord's commandments, after which the Lord will establish his heart and grant his desire for wisdom.

Chapter 7 body

Chapter 7 is a series of maxims addressed to "my son" on avoiding wrongdoing and living rightly. It warns against sowing unrighteousness, seeking honor or judgeship, lying, repeating sins, and presuming that many gifts will win God's acceptance. It urges humility, hard labor, loyalty to friends and a wise wife, and fair treatment of faithful servants, along with care for cattle, children and daughters. It commands honoring one's father and mother, fearing the Lord, revering priests and giving them their prescribed portions. It closes by calling for generosity to the poor, mourning with those who weep, visiting the sick, and remembering eternity in all one's words.

Chapter 8 body

Chapter 8 is a series of prohibitions offering advice on conduct toward others. It warns against contending with mighty, rich, loudmouthed, rude, wrathful, reckless, or foolish men, and against going to law with a judge. It urges respect for people who turn from sin, for the elderly, and for the dead, reminding readers that all will age, die, and deserve punishment. It encourages attending to the discourse of the wise and aged for discipline, understanding, and how to serve great men. It also counsels caution with lending, giving surety, and sharing secrets or one's heart with strangers or fools.

Chapter 9 body

Chapter 9 gives a man a series of warnings about women, friends, and the company he keeps. He is told not to be jealous of his wife, not to surrender himself to women, and to avoid prostitutes, singers, virgins, and married women, since beauty kindles passion and leads to destruction. He is also told not to abandon an old friend, since a new friend is like new wine that gains value with age, and not to envy sinners or delight in the ungodly, who will not go unpunished. The chapter advises keeping away from men with power to kill, getting to know neighbors, and taking counsel with the wise. It ends by urging conversation with men of understanding and righteous table companions, and notes that a ruler is judged by his speech while a loudmouthed man is hated.

Chapter 10 body

Chapter 10 teaches that the character of a ruler shapes his people: a wise judge instructs them and an undisciplined king destroys them. The Lord holds the government of the earth and a man's prosperity, and raises up the right person at the right time. Pride, which begins when a man departs from the Lord, is hateful to the Lord and to men, and the Lord has overthrown the thrones and lands of nations, putting the lowly in their place. Human frailty is stressed, since the king of today dies tomorrow and a dead man inherits maggots, vermin, and worms. True honor belongs to those who fear the Lord, whether rich or poor, and the chapter urges humility, honest labor, and honor according to one's worthiness rather than boasting.

Chapter 11 body

Chapter 11 offers a series of counsels addressed to "my son" about humility, judgment, labor, and the Lord's control over fortune. It teaches that the lowly can be raised, as the bee is small yet produces the best confection, and that appearances and clothing should not be trusted or boasted of. It warns against blaming before investigating, answering before listening, interrupting, and meddling in many matters. It says that good and bad, life and death, poverty and riches come from the Lord, who can swiftly make a poor man rich, and that a man should stay steadfast in his work and not judge anyone happy before death. It closes by warning against admitting every man into one's house, because the proud and deceitful lie in wait like a decoy partridge, a spy, or a spark that kindles coals.

Chapter 12 body

Chapter 12 advises readers on whom to benefit and whom to distrust. The speaker urges giving to the godly or lowly man and withholding help and bread from the sinner and ungodly man, since the Most High hates sinners and will repay them with vengeance. The chapter then contrasts friends and enemies: a friend is tested in adversity, when he may leave, while an enemy's hostility is hidden in prosperity. The reader is warned never to trust an enemy, never to seat him close, and not to associate with a sinner, since no one will pity such a person. The chapter closes by describing the enemy's deceit, with sweet words, tears, feigned help, and gestures, while he plots to trip the reader and seek blood.

Chapter 13 body

Chapter 13 warns against associating with the proud, the mighty, and the rich. It argues that a powerful man exploits those who are useful to him, deceives them with smiles and kind words, drains their resources, and then abandons and mocks them. The text advises reserve and caution around such men, and care in keeping secrets. It contrasts how the rich and the poor are treated: the rich are supported and praised when they fall or speak, while the poor are pushed away, rebuked, and ignored. It closes by saying riches are good if free of sin, that poverty is evil only in the opinion of the ungodly, and that a person's heart shows in their countenance.

Chapter 14 body

Chapter 14 contrasts the miser with the person who enjoys what he has. The miser is described as stingy, evil, and grudging even to himself; he gathers wealth for others, cannot enjoy his possessions, and begrudges bread at his table. The speaker then addresses 'my son', urging him to treat himself well, bring offerings to the Lord, and do good to friends, because death will not wait and there is no seeking of luxury in Hades. Flesh is compared to a garment and to leaves that fall and grow again, as generations come and go. The chapter ends by blessing the man who meditates on wisdom, pursues her like a hunter, lodges near her house, and finds shelter and glory under her branches.

Chapter 15 body

Chapter 15 describes how the one who fears the Lord and holds the law will obtain wisdom, who is personified as a mother and a wife and who nourishes, steadies, and exalts him, giving him joy and an everlasting name. Foolish men, sinners, and liars will not obtain or remember her, and praise is unfitting in a sinner's mouth because it was not sent to him from the Lord. The text rejects blaming the Lord for one's falling away or error, since he hates abomination and has no need of a sinful man. It teaches that the Lord made man from the beginning and left him to his own counsel, setting fire and water, life and death, before him to choose. The Lord is great in wisdom and power, sees all things, watches those who fear him, and has commanded no one to be ungodly or licensed anyone to sin.

Chapter 16 body

Chapter 16 warns against desiring many unprofitable or ungodly children, saying one child with understanding is better than a thousand and that dying childless beats having ungodly offspring. It recalls God's past judgments on the giants of old, Lot's neighbors, the people of perdition, and the six hundred thousand footmen, showing that he does not spare the proud and stiff-necked. It teaches that his mercy and wrath are both great and that he judges each man according to his works, so no one can hide from him among the multitude. The speaker then calls his son to listen and describes how the Lord ordered his works from the beginning, fixing their boundaries and generations. The chapter ends with the Lord filling the earth with blessings and with living things covering it and returning into it.

Chapter 17 body

Chapter 17 describes how the Lord created mankind from earth, gave them limited days, authority over beasts and birds, strength, understanding, and a law of life, and made an everlasting covenant with them. It says that all human ways and works are always before the Lord, that every nation was given a ruler while Israel is the Lord's portion, and that no iniquity is hidden from him. The Lord is said to keep a man's alms and kindness dear and to repay people for their deeds, yet he grants a return to those who repent and comforts those losing hope. The text urges people to return to the Lord, forsake sins, and hate what is abominable, noting that the dead in Hades cannot give thanks but the living can praise him. It closes by stressing the Lord's great mercy and human frailty: humans are not immortal, flesh and blood devise evil, and all men are earth and ashes.

Chapter 18 body

Chapter 18 praises the Lord, who lives forever, created the universe, and is alone just, whose works no one can fully trace, measure, or explore. It reflects on mankind's brief lifespan of at most a hundred years, which is like a drop of water or a pebble set against eternity, and on how the Lord is patient and multiplies forgiveness and mercy toward all flesh, reproving and teaching as a shepherd does his flock. The speaker then addresses 'my son' with advice: do not mix reproach with giving, since a word is better than a gift, and learn, examine yourself, repent, and pay vows promptly before judgment or sickness comes. It urges remembering hunger in days of plenty and the coming wrath, and commends the wise and understanding who are cautious and speak apt proverbs. It closes by warning against following lusts and appetites and against luxury and banqueting on borrowed money, which would make one a beggar and a laughing stock to enemies.

Chapter 19 body

Chapter 19 gives warnings about folly, gossip, and deceit. It begins with drunkenness, despising small things, and consorting with wine, women, and prostitutes, which lead to ruin. It then urges people not to repeat what they hear, since a fool suffers over a secret like a woman in labor, and it advises questioning a friend or neighbor before believing slander, reproving before threatening. Finally it equates wisdom with fear of the Lord and doing the law, ranks a God-fearing person of little understanding above a clever lawbreaker, and describes the deceitful man who feigns mourning while plotting mischief and whose character shows in his appearance, laughter, and gait.

Chapter 20 body

Chapter 20 is a series of proverbs contrasting the wise man and the fool, especially in how they use speech, silence, and gifts. It says a wise man stays silent until the right time, while the braggart and fool miss their time and are hated for much talk. It describes the fool's gifts as unprofitable because he expects many times the return, and his habit of lending today and demanding repayment tomorrow. It condemns the liar, calling a lie an ugly blot and saying a thief is better than a habitual liar, though both inherit destruction. It closes by warning that gifts blind the wise and that hidden wisdom is useless, so a man who hides his folly is better than one who hides his wisdom.

Chapter 21 body

Chapter 21 is addressed by a speaker to his son and urges him to stop sinning and seek forgiveness for past sins. It compares sin to a snake and a two-edged sword, and says that violence and arrogance bring ruin, that the poor man's prayer reaches God, and that the way of sinners ends in the pit of Hades. The larger part of the chapter contrasts the fool with the wise or prudent man, covering speech, response to instruction, laughter, conduct at another's house, and where each keeps the heart and mouth. It ends by saying that the ungodly who curse an adversary curse their own souls, and that a whisperer defiles his own soul and is hated wherever he travels.

Chapter 22 body

Chapter 22 uses comparisons to describe the slothful man, the undisciplined or shameful child, and especially the fool. The slothful man is likened to a defiled stone and a dunghill, and a foolish or arrogant daughter brings shame on her father and husband. Teaching a fool is compared to gluing potsherds or waking a sleeper, and the fool is said to be heavier than lead, sand, salt, or iron, deserving lifelong mourning and avoidance. The chapter contrasts a heart settled on wise counsel, which stands firm, with the fearful heart of a fool. It ends by discussing friendship: insulting a friend dissolves the bond, but reconciliation is possible except after insult, arrogance, secret disclosure, or a treacherous blow, and the speaker pledges loyalty to a friend and asks for a guard on his own mouth.

Chapter 23 body

Chapter 23 opens with the speaker praying to the Lord, addressed as Father and Master of his life, not to be abandoned to the counsel of his adversaries and asking for discipline over his thoughts, haughty eyes, evil desire, gluttony, and lust. He then instructs his children in the discipline of the mouth, warning against habitual oaths, naming the Holy One, gross rudeness, and abusive language, which bring sin, calamity, and shame. The chapter condemns the fornicator and the adulterous man, who falsely believes darkness and walls hide him, though the Lord's eyes see all things, and he will be punished and seized unexpectedly. It likewise condemns the wife who leaves her husband and bears an heir by another man, citing three offenses, and says she will be brought before the congregation, her children will not take root, and her memory will be a curse. Those left behind learn that nothing is better than the fear of the Lord or sweeter than heeding his commandments.

Chapter 24 body

Chapter 24 is a speech in which Wisdom praises herself among the people and in the congregation of the Most High. She says she came from the mouth of the Most High, roamed heaven, the abyss, and every nation seeking rest, and was then commanded by the Creator to dwell in Jacob and Israel. She ministered in the tabernacle, was established in Zion, and had her domain in Jerusalem, where she grew like cedars, palms, olives, and fragrant spices. She invites those who desire her to eat and drink of her fruits, promising they will not be ashamed or sin. The text identifies all this with the book of the covenant and the law of Moses, compares her abundance to great rivers, and closes with Wisdom saying her stream has become a sea and that she labors for all who seek wisdom.

Chapter 25 body

Chapter 25 is a wisdom sermon listing things the speaker finds beautiful, hateful, and happy. He enjoys harmony among kindred, neighbors, and spouses, and hates the arrogant poor man, the lying rich man, and the adulterous old fool. He praises the judgment, wisdom, and experience of the aged, and says the fear of the Lord surpasses everything, including wisdom. The second half turns to a long denunciation of the wicked woman, whom he calls worse than any wound or enemy, and who he says darkens her husband's life and began sin and death. He closes by advising a husband to deny such a woman freedom of speech and to cut her away if she does not obey.

Chapter 26 body

Chapter 26 praises the good wife and warns about the wicked or unrestrained woman. A good wife brings her husband joy, peace, doubled days, and delight, and her beauty, modesty, and self-control are compared to the sun, a lampstand, and golden pillars on silver. The speaker names three things his heart fears: slander of a city, a mob assembly, and false accusation, all worse than death. Women who are jealous, drunken, or sexually unfaithful are condemned, and a headstrong daughter is to be watched closely. The text also lists grievous sights, including a warrior in poverty, wise men treated as garbage, and one who turns from righteousness to sin, and notes that merchants and retailers struggle to avoid sin.

Chapter 27 body

Chapter 27 warns that profit-seeking invites sin and that a household without the fear of the Lord is quickly overthrown. It teaches that a person's thoughts and speech reveal his character, comparing them to a sieve, a furnace testing a potter's vessels, and a tree's fruit. It contrasts the wise discourse of the godly with the offensive, swearing, quarrelsome talk of fools and the proud. It strongly condemns the one who reveals a friend's secrets, since trust and friendship are lost and cannot be recovered. It closes by describing the deceitful schemer and evildoers, whose harm rebounds on them, and says the Lord hates the schemer and that wrath and anger belong to the sinner.

Chapter 28 body

Chapter 28 teaches that those who take vengeance will meet vengeance from the Lord, while those who forgive their neighbor's hurt will have their own sins pardoned when they pray. It urges the reader to remember death, the commandments, and the covenant of the Highest in order to stop enmity and anger. It warns that passionate and sinful men kindle strife and discord, comparing quarrels to a fire that spreads according to its fuel, and to a spark that can be blown or quenched by the same mouth. It then condemns the whisperer and slanderer, whose tongue destroys peace, scatters peoples, topples cities, and breaks bones more deadly than the sword, with a yoke of iron and bands of brass. It closes by advising the reader to guard words with a balance, a door, and a bar, as one guards silver and gold with thorns, lest he slip and fall.

Chapter 29 body

Chapter 29 instructs readers on lending, almsgiving, and suretyship, and warns about dependent living. It urges merciful people to lend to a neighbor in need and to repay on time, while describing borrowers who flatter before receiving a loan and then make excuses, repay half, or curse their creditor. It tells readers to be patient with a poor man, to give alms for the commandment's sake, and to store up almsgiving as protection greater than a shield or spear. It praises the guarantor who stands surety for a neighbor, condemns the thankless sinner who ruins him, and warns that suretyship has ruined prosperous people and driven them abroad. It ends by naming life's essentials, preferring a modest home to sumptuous fare in another man's house, and depicting the humiliation of a sojourner who is made to serve a host and then ordered out.

Chapter 30 body

Chapter 30 first urges a father to discipline his son strictly, promising that the father will gain joy, pride before friends, honor against enemies, and a legacy in a son like himself. It warns that pampering, playing with, or giving liberty to a child leads to fear, grief, stubbornness, and sorrow, comparing an unrestrained son to an unbroken horse. The chapter then turns to health, saying a healthy poor man is better than a sick rich one, and that health of body and joy of heart surpass gold and wealth. It says death is better than bitter life and compares good things given to the afflicted to offerings laid on a grave or before an idol. It closes by urging the reader not to give the soul to sorrow, since gladness and cheerfulness lengthen life while envy, wrath, and anxiety shorten it.

Chapter 31 body

Chapter 31 warns first against the sleeplessness and ruin that come from pursuing riches, noting that the rich man toils to gather money while the poor man toils in lack, and that many have been destroyed for gold. It praises the rich person who is found blameless and does not go after gold, whose prosperity will be made sure and whose alms the congregation will proclaim. The middle of the chapter instructs a guest at a great table to avoid greed, to consider his neighbor's feelings, to eat like a human being, to stop first, and to take moderate portions, since moderate eating brings healthy sleep while insatiable eating brings colic and wakefulness. It notes that people bless the man liberal with food and murmur against the stingy one. The chapter ends with advice on wine: do not show valor in it, drink it in season and moderation for joy, avoid excess that brings bitterness, rage and wounds, and do not rebuke or distress a neighbor at a wine banquet.

Chapter 32 body

Chapter 32 gives instruction on proper conduct at a feast and in life generally. A person made ruler of a feast should stay humble, serve the guests first, and then sit down to receive a wreath. Older speakers may talk but with sound knowledge and without interrupting the music, while a young man should speak only if obliged and asked, no more than twice, and briefly. Music at a wine banquet is compared to a ruby or emerald signet set in gold, and the reader is told to enjoy himself, avoid proud speech, and bless his Maker. The second half contrasts those who fear the Lord, who accept discipline and seek the law, with sinners and hypocrites who shun reproof, and urges counsel, caution, and guarding one's soul by keeping the commandments.

Chapter 33 body

Chapter 33 opens by saying that the one who fears the Lord will be delivered in trials, and that a wise man trusts the law while a hypocrite is like a boat in a storm. It contrasts the fool, likened to a cartwheel, and a mocking friend, likened to a stallion. It asks why one day excels another and answers that the Lord, in his knowledge, distinguished days, seasons and feasts, and made men different from Adam's dust, blessing some and cursing others like clay in a potter's hand. It teaches that the Most High's works come in pairs, one against another, and the speaker says he labored for all who seek instruction. The chapter then advises rulers and others not to give power or goods to family or friends while alive, and ends with instructions on keeping slaves at work, with discipline, yet treating them fairly like oneself.

Chapter 34 body

Chapter 34 warns that hopes placed in dreams, divinations, and soothsayings are vain and have led many astray, unless the Most High sends them in a visitation. The speaker then praises the wisdom gained by experience and travel, recounting that he was often in danger of death yet was preserved. He declares that those who fear the Lord will live, be unafraid, and be protected and supported by the Lord's watchful eyes. The chapter then condemns offerings made from wrongfully gotten goods or from the possessions of the poor, equating depriving the needy of their living or a hireling of his wages with murder. It closes with illustrations of futile effort: building while another pulls down, praying while another curses, washing after touching a corpse and touching it again, and fasting for sins then repeating them.

Chapter 35 body

Chapter 35 teaches that keeping the law, heeding the commandments, showing kindness, giving alms, and departing from wickedness are themselves equivalent to offerings and sacrifices to the Lord. It urges generous, cheerful giving of first fruits and tithes, promising that the Lord repays sevenfold, while warning that he cannot be bribed with gifts or unrighteous sacrifices because he shows no partiality. The Lord is described as hearing the prayers of the wronged poor, the fatherless, the widow, and the humble, whose supplication pierces the clouds. The chapter closes by promising that the Lord will act without delay to crush the unmerciful, the heathen, and the arrogant, repay every man according to his deeds, and bring joy to his people through his mercy, which is as welcome as rain in drought.

Chapter 36 body

Chapter 36 opens with a prayer asking the Lord, the God of all, to have mercy on his people and to send fear on the foreign nations so that they will know there is no God but him. The petitioners ask him to show new signs, destroy the adversary and enemy, crush the rulers who say there is no one but themselves, gather the tribes of Jacob, and have compassion on Israel and Jerusalem, filling Zion with his glory and fulfilling the prophecies spoken in his name. The prayer ends by appealing to the blessing of Aaron so that all on earth will know the Lord is the eternal God. The chapter then turns to proverbs about discernment: the belly accepts any food but some is better, and an understanding heart detects false speech just as the mouth tastes game. Finally it praises a good wife as a man's richest treasure, help, and pillar of support, and warns that a man without a wife is like an unfenced property or a wanderer with no home.

Chapter 37 body

Chapter 37 warns about false friends and self-interested advisers, then urges careful choice of counsel. Some companions are friends only in name, turning to enemies in affliction or battle, and some counselors advise for their own gain and watch to see what befalls the person they advised. The reader is told not to consult people whose position biases them, such as a coward about war or a merchant about business, but to stay with a godly man who keeps the commandments, to trust the counsel of his own heart, and to ask the Most High to direct his way. The chapter then contrasts the clever who profit no one with the wise man who instructs his people, is blessed, and has a lasting name. It closes by urging the reader to test his own soul and avoid gluttony, which brings disease, nausea, and death.

Chapter 38 body

Chapter 38 first urges honoring the physician, whom the Lord created, and the medicines the Lord brought out of the earth. It advises a sick son to pray, put away wrongdoing, make an offering, and then give place to the physician, who also asks the Lord to prosper his healing. It then turns to the dead, instructing the son to weep and lament and bury the body with honor, but to limit mourning to a day or two and not give his heart to sorrow, since the same end awaits him. Finally it contrasts the scribe, whose wisdom comes from leisure, with the plowman, craftsman, smith, and potter, who are absorbed in their labors and trust their hands. These workers sustain the city and the fabric of the age, though they are not sought in council, judgment, or assembly.

Chapter 39 body

Chapter 39 describes the wise man who devotes his soul to the law of the Most High, studying ancient wisdom, prophecies, proverbs and parables, serving great men, praying for pardon, and being filled with understanding if the Lord wills. His name will endure through generations and nations will praise his wisdom. The speaker then calls the holy children to bloom like a rose and lily and to bless the Lord. He declares that all the Lord's works are good and fulfilled in their time, so no one should question them. He explains that good and evil things were created for the good and for sinners respectively, listing life's necessities, which turn to evil for sinners, and instruments of vengeance such as winds, fire, hail, famine, death, beasts and the sword. The chapter closes with a summons to sing praises and bless the Lord's name.

Chapter 40 body

Chapter 40 describes the heavy travail laid on all the sons of Adam from birth to burial, afflicting everyone from the king on his throne to the man in burlap with fear of death, strife, and troubled sleep. Sinners suffer sevenfold more, with death, bloodshed, sword, famine, and plague created for the wicked, and the flood came because of them. The text says bribery and injustice will be blotted out while good faith stands forever, and the goods and children of the ungodly will dry up like a river or like unhealthy roots on a rock. It then lists good things such as wine, music, friends, gold, and strength, each said to be surpassed by something better, with the fear of the Lord named as the best of all. It closes by warning a son not to lead a beggar's life, since it is better to die than to beg.

Chapter 41 body

Chapter 41 reflects on death, which is bitter to the prosperous and at-peace man but acceptable to the needy, aged and weary, and urges readers not to fear it since it is the Lord's sentence on all flesh and the Most High's good pleasure. It then turns to sinners and the ungodly who have forsaken the law of the Most High, saying their children will suffer disgrace, their inheritance will perish, and they are cursed in birth and death. The text contrasts the mourning over bodies with the blotting out of sinners' evil name, and says a good name outlasts a good life and a thousand treasures of gold. It closes with a list of things to be ashamed of, including sexual immorality, lying, theft, betraying secrets, and rude behavior toward friends, so that one is ashamed of the right things and finds favor with everyone.

Chapter 42 body

Chapter 42 first lists matters about which one should not be ashamed or sin to save face, including the law of the Most High, fair reckoning with partners and travellers, accurate scales and weights, and honest dealing with merchants, along with discipline of children and slaves. It advises securing and recording what is handed over by number, weight and writing, and instructing the unwise and the very old. It then describes a father's anxious care for his daughter at each stage of her life, urges strict watch over a headstrong daughter, and warns against gazing at beautiful bodies or sitting among women, claiming wickedness comes from a woman as a moth from garments. The second half turns to the works of the Lord, which he declares from what the speaker has seen, and which the Lord, who knows all things past and future and needs no counselor, has ordered in wisdom. The chapter ends by praising his works as desirable, obedient, paired and perfect, asking who could ever see enough of his glory.

Chapter 43 body

Chapter 43 praises the Lord through the wonders of creation. It describes the sun's burning heat, the moon marking seasons and feast days, the stars standing in order at the word of the Holy One, the rainbow, and the snow, hail, lightning, thunder, frost, ice and winds. It then turns to the sea, with its dangers, islands and huge creatures. The writer concludes that words cannot say enough, that the Lord is greater than all his works, and that people should glorify him with full strength, since only a few of his works have been seen and he made all things and gave wisdom to the godly.

Chapter 44 body

Chapter 44 opens a praise of famous ancestors, whom the Lord endowed with great glory. It describes their roles as rulers, prophets, counselors, and composers of music and verse, noting that some left a name while others are forgotten. The righteous men of mercy are promised lasting inheritance for their offspring within the covenant and enduring fame. The chapter then names Enoch, Noah, and Abraham, recounting how Enoch was taken up, how Noah kept a remnant alive through the flood, and how Abraham was faithful in testing and received an oath of blessing. The covenant and blessing pass through Isaac to Jacob, whose inheritance was divided among twelve tribes.

Chapter 45 body

Chapter 45 praises Moses as a man of mercy loved by God and men, whom God glorified before kings, spoke to face to face, and gave the law to teach Jacob and Israel. It then turns to Aaron, his brother of the tribe of Levi, whom God exalted with an everlasting covenant, the priesthood, and glorious garments including the ephod, Urim and Thummim, engraved stones, and a gold crown inscribed "HOLINESS". Moses consecrated and anointed Aaron, and Dathan, Abiram, and Korah's congregation, who conspired against him in the wilderness, were destroyed by the Lord with flaming fire. Aaron and his offspring receive first fruits and sacrifices but no land inheritance, since the Lord is their portion. The chapter ends with Phinehas son of Eleazar, who was zealous and made atonement, receiving a covenant of peace and the priesthood forever, a mention of the covenant with David, and a closing prayer for wisdom to judge the people righteously.

Chapter 46 body

Chapter 46 praises Joshua the son of Nun as a valiant warrior and Moses' successor, who fought enemies, saw the sun go back, and gave Israel its inheritance. It recounts how he and Caleb the son of Jephunneh alone, of six hundred thousand on foot, were preserved to enter the promised land, and how Caleb kept his strength into old age. It then blesses the judges, whose hearts did not turn from the Lord, and wishes their memory and names to endure. Finally it honors Samuel, the Lord's prophet, who established a kingdom, judged by the law, called on the Lord against enemies, declared his integrity before his death, and prophesied even after he fell asleep.

Chapter 47 body

Chapter 47 begins with Nathan prophesying in David's days and then praises David, who as a youth killed Goliath with a sling stone, defeated the Philistines, gave thanks to the Most High, set singers before the altar, and ordered the feasts and seasons. The Lord took away David's sins and gave him a covenant of kings and a glorious throne. His wise son Solomon reigned in peace, built a house for God's name, and was famed across the earth and islands for his songs, proverbs, parables, and riches. But Solomon bowed to women and was brought into subjection, which blemished his honor, defiled his offspring, and led to the kingdom being divided with a disobedient rule out of Ephraim. Though the Lord would not forsake his mercy and left a remnant to Jacob and a root to David, Solomon's offspring Rehoboam and Jeroboam son of Nebat led the people into revolt and sin, until their sins multiplied and they were removed from their land.

Chapter 48 body

Chapter 48 praises the prophet Elijah, whose word burned like a torch, who shut up the heavens, brought down fire three times, raised a dead man, anointed kings and prophets, and was taken up in a chariot of fiery horses. His successor Elisha, filled with Elijah's spirit, feared no ruler, and his body prophesied even after burial. The people did not repent despite these wonders and were carried away as plunder and scattered, leaving few of them under a ruler from David's house. The chapter then turns to Hezekiah, who fortified his city, brought water into it, and was strong in the ways of David. When Sennacherib invaded, sent Rabshakeh, and boasted against Zion, the people called on the Lord, who delivered them by the hand of Isaiah and destroyed the Assyrian camp through his angel. Isaiah, great and faithful in his vision, saw the future, made the sun go backward, prolonged the king's life, and comforted those who mourned in Zion.

Chapter 49 body

Chapter 49 praises Josiah, whose memory is likened to sweet incense and who reformed the people, removed abominations, and made godliness prevail in lawless days. It states that apart from David, Hezekiah, and Josiah, the kings of Judah were wicked and abandoned the law, so their power and glory passed to others and the city was set on fire, as Jeremiah wrote. It then recalls Jeremiah, who was mistreated though sanctified in the womb as a prophet, and Ezekiel, who saw the vision of glory on the chariot of the cherubim. The chapter also honors the twelve prophets, Zerubbabel, Jesus the son of Josedek, and Nehemiah for comforting and rebuilding the people, the house, and the walls. It closes by naming Enoch, Joseph, Shem, Seth, and above all Adam as honored figures.

Chapter 50 body

Chapter 50 praises Simon, the son of Onias, the high priest, who repaired and strengthened the temple, had a water cistern dug, and fortified the city against siege. It describes his glory as he came out of the house of the veil, comparing him to the morning star, the full moon, the sun, and flourishing plants and trees. It then narrates the liturgy at the altar, where Simon poured out the libation, the sons of Aaron sounded trumpets, the people fell down in worship, the singers praised, and Simon blessed the congregation. The author closes with a prayer for joy and peace in Israel, a lament over two nations and a third that is no nation, and a signature by Jesus, the son of Sirach Eleazar, of Jerusalem, who says he wrote the instruction in this book and blesses whoever lays it up in his heart.

Chapter 51 body

In Chapter 51, a speaker gives thanks to the Lord, who is called King and Savior, for rescuing him from death, slander before the king, surrounding enemies, and fire he did not kindle. He recalls that when no human help was available he remembered the Lord's mercy, prayed to the Lord and to the Father of his Lord, and was heard. He then recounts seeking wisdom from his youth, praying for her before the temple, practicing her, and finding her in purity, and he praises the Lord for giving him a tongue as his reward. He invites the uneducated to come to the house of instruction, take on her yoke, and get her without money. He closes by urging them to rejoice in the Lord's mercy and to do their work before the time comes, when the Lord will give their reward.
