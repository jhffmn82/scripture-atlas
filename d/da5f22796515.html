Abstract written by a model

This document summarizes Book II of Mencius, centered on Mencius’s account of benevolent rule, moral cultivation, and his conduct while serving and leaving Qi. Gong Sun Chou first asks whether Mencius could equal Guan Zhong and Yan in governing Qi. Mencius argues that Qi could readily attain royal dignity: its people suffer, its resources are sufficient, and benevolent government would secure genuine support. He distinguishes submission compelled by force from willing submission won by virtue, and repeatedly presents benevolence, righteousness, worthy appointments, sound policies, and care for the people as the basis of lasting rule. He identifies compassion, shame, modesty, and moral discernment as innate principles that must be developed into benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and knowledge. He also praises learning from others, self-examination, and the superior man’s ability to help others practise virtue, while criticizing both narrow purity and deficient self-respect. The later chapters turn to Mencius’s actions in Qi and the objections they provoke. He maintains that offering benevolence and righteousness to a ruler is the highest form of respect, and that rulers seeking great achievements should learn from virtuous ministers. He defends accepting or refusing gifts according to their purpose, rebukes Kong Ju Xin for failing the people of Ping Lu, and explains the different responsibilities of officeholders and those without office. Incidents involving his mission to Teng, his mother’s coffin, Yan’s destruction, and the rebellion in Yan illustrate his concern for proper authority, duty, and correction of errors. Finally, Mencius leaves Qi, declining the king’s proposed house and support because it would compromise his consistency and lack of desire for riches. He explains that he had hoped to serve and reform the king, but could not remain without a proper recall or suitable treatment. His dissatisfaction comes from believing the time was ripe for tranquillity and true sovereignty, though Heaven had not yet granted the kingdom order.

Mencius, Book II (Gong Sun Chou) / Chapter 1 body

Gong Sun Chou asks Mencius whether he could equal the achievements of Guan Zhong and Yan if he governed Qi. Mencius says that bringing Qi to royal dignity would be easy because its territory and population are sufficient, the people suffer greatly, and benevolent government would win their support. He explains that king Wen faced greater obstacles than a ruler of Qi would face, while Wu Ding and Zhou benefited from established influence and capable assistance. Mencius concludes that the present time is especially favorable for a true sovereign to arise.

Chapter 2 body

Gong Sun Chou questions Mencius about the unperturbed mind, valour, the will, and the passion-nature. Mencius explains that the will leads the passion-nature, and that the vast, flowing passion-nature is nourished by accumulated righteous deeds without forcing its growth. He says he understands words by discerning the condition of the speaker's mind and argues that these conditions affect government. Mencius compares Bo Yi, Yi Yin, and Confucius, concluding that no one has been like Confucius, though Mencius wishes to learn to be like him.

Chapter 3 body

Mencius explains the difference between ruling through force and ruling through virtue. He says that force can make a person the leader of the princes, but people submit only because they lack the strength to resist. Virtue and benevolence make people sincerely pleased to submit, allowing a prince to become sovereign without a large kingdom. Tang, king Wen, Confucius, the seventy disciples, and the Book of Poetry are cited as illustrations.

Chapter 4 body

Mencius teaches that benevolence brings glory to a prince, while acting without it brings disgrace. He says a prince should esteem virtue, honor virtuous scholars, appoint worthy and able people, and use peaceful periods to clarify government and its legal sanctions. Confucius affirms that the Book of Poetry illustrates sound governing through preparation and protection from insult. The passage concludes that calamity and happiness result from people's own actions, while Heaven-sent calamities may still be escaped and self-caused calamities cannot.

Chapter 5 body

Mencius explains that a ruler can gain the support of scholars, traders, travellers, husbandmen, and all the people by practising five specific policies. These policies include honouring talented and virtuous men, limiting market and frontier charges, requiring mutual aid on public fields without extra taxes, and avoiding certain levies on shop occupiers. If a ruler truly practises these five things, neighbouring kingdoms will regard him as a parent, and he will have no enemy in the kingdom. Such a ruler is called the minister of Heaven and will attain the royal dignity.

Chapter 6 body

Mencius explains that all men possess a mind that cannot bear to see others suffer, illustrated by their alarm when a child is about to fall into a well. He says the ancient kings possessed this commiserating mind and practiced commiserating government, making ruling the kingdom easy. He identifies four essential human feelings as principles of benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and knowledge. He urges men to develop these four principles fully, since their development enables them to love and protect all within the four seas, while their neglect leaves them unable even to serve their parents.

Chapter 7 body

Mencius compares the maker of arrows with the maker of armour, noting that each fears the harm associated with his work. He says choosing a profession and a residence requires caution, citing Confucius on the importance of virtuous manners in a neighbourhood. Mencius teaches that benevolence is the honourable dignity conferred by Heaven and the quiet home in which man should dwell. He compares the benevolent person to an archer who seeks the cause of failure in himself rather than blaming others.

Chapter 8 body

Mencius says that Zi Lu rejoiced when told of his faults, while Yu bowed when he heard good words. Shun delighted in what was good, regarded virtue as common to himself and others, and gave up his own way to follow others. From his early work as a farmer, potter, and fisherman until he became emperor, Shun continually learned from others. Mencius teaches that taking example from others to practise virtue helps them practise it, and that helping men practise virtue is the greatest attribute of the superior man.

Chapter 9 body

Mencius compares Bo Yi and Hui of Liu Xia in their responses to impure princes, bad men, office, poverty, and personal principles. Bo Yi refuses service, association, and gifts connected with people he disapproves of, valuing purity above employment. Hui of Liu Xia serves an impure prince without concealing his virtue, remains steady when neglected or poor, and does not believe proximity to others can defile him. Mencius says Bo Yi was narrow-minded and Hui of Liu Xia lacked self-respect, while the superior man shows neither trait.

Chapter 1 body

Mencius explains that opportunities of time vouchsafed by Heaven are inferior to advantages of situation afforded by the Earth, which are in turn inferior to the union arising from the accord of Men. He illustrates this with cities that fail to resist attack despite favorable timing or strong defenses. He teaches that a people, State, or kingdom is secured by proper conduct and human support rather than by boundaries, natural defenses, or weapons. He concludes that a true ruler avoids fighting, but must overcome if he fights.

Chapter 2 body

Mencius declines the king of Qi’s request to attend court, saying he is unwell, but visits a condolence gathering the next day. When questioned by Gong Sun Chou and Jing Chou, Mencius defends his conduct and says that presenting benevolence and righteousness to the king is the greatest respect he can show him. He argues that virtue, age, and nobility are the three universally acknowledged honors, and that rulers who seek great achievements should learn from virtuous ministers rather than command them. Tang’s treatment of Yi Yin and the duke Huan’s treatment of Guan Zhong are given as examples of rulers who first learned from those ministers and then employed them.

Chapter 3 body

Chen Zhen questions Mencius about accepting or refusing presents sent to him in Qi, Song, and Xue. Mencius says he did right in all three cases because the presents served different purposes. In Song, the present was for travelling expenses, and in Xue, it was to help procure arms for protection. In Qi, he had no need for money, so accepting the present would have been accepting a bribe.

Chapter 4 body

Mencius confronts the governor of Ping Lu, Kong Ju Xin, about repeatedly failing to protect the people under his charge. He compares the governor’s duty to that of a herder responsible for finding pasture for cattle and sheep. Kong Ju Xin admits his guilt, and Mencius later tells the king that Kong Ju Xin is the only governor who knows his faults. The king accepts responsibility and says that he is guilty in this matter.

Chapter 5 body

Mencius questions Qi Wa about declining the governorship of Ling Qiu and becoming chief criminal judge so he could speak his views. After several months, Qi Wa remonstrates with the king, resigns when his counsel is ignored, and leaves. The people of Qi approve Mencius’s guidance to Qi Wa but question Qi Wa’s own conduct. Mencius explains that he is free to advance or retire because he holds no office and bears no duty to give his opinion.

Chapter 6 body

Mencius, a high dignitary in Qi, went to Teng on a mission of condolence. The king sent Wang Huan, the governor of Gai, as assistant-commissioner. Wang Huan attended Mencius morning and evening, but Mencius did not discuss the mission's business with him during the journey there and back. Mencius explained that proper officers handled those matters.

Chapter 7 body

Mencius traveled from Qi to Lu to bury his mother and stopped at Ying on his return to Qi. Chong Yu questioned Mencius about the quality of the coffin used for his mother, saying that the wood seemed too good. Mencius explained that traditional coffin thickness applied to everyone and fulfilled natural feelings, including the desire to protect the bodies of the dead from the earth. He concluded that a superior man is not niggardly toward his parents.

Chapter 8 body

Shen Tong asked Mencius whether Yan might be smitten. Mencius said that it might because Zi Kuai had no right to give Yan away and Zi Zhi had no right to receive it. The people of Qi then smote Yan, and Mencius denied that he had advised Qi to do so. He explained that he would have said only that the minister of Heaven could smite Yan, not that Qi could do it.

Chapter 9 body

The people of Yan rebelled, causing the king of Qi to feel ashamed when he thought of Mencius. Chen Jia compared the king with Zhou Gong and argued that even Zhou Gong had lacked benevolence or knowledge if he knowingly or unknowingly appointed Guan Shu, who later rebelled with the Yin State. Mencius explained that Zhou Gong did not know Guan Shu would rebel and that his appointment of his elder brother was understandable. Mencius said that superior men of old reformed their errors, while those of the present persist in errors and apologize for them.

Chapter 10 body

Mencius gave up his office and arranged to return to his native State. The king visited him, expressed regret at his departure, and said he did not know whether he would see Mencius again. The king later proposed giving Mencius a house and supporting his disciples with an allowance of 10,000 zhong. Mencius declined, explaining that accepting the offer would appear inconsistent with his rejection of 100,000 zhong and his lack of desire for riches.

Chapter 11 body

Mencius, having left Qi, spent the night in Zhou. A visitor who wished to detain him on behalf of the king approached him, but Mencius gave no answer and slept. Mencius explained that the duke Mou had retained Zi Si through proper treatment and said that the visitor's plans did not treat him similarly.

Chapter 12 body

Yin Shi criticizes Mencius for leaving Qi after failing to find a suitable ruler and for delaying his departure from Zhou. Mencius explains that he wanted to serve the king, hoped the king would change, and felt constrained to leave when the king did not recall him. He says that using him would benefit the people of the whole kingdom, not only Qi, and contrasts his conduct with that of small-minded remonstrants. Yin Shi accepts the explanation and admits that he is a small man.

Chapter 13 body

Mencius left Qi, and Chong Yu questioned him about his dissatisfied appearance. Mencius explained that the expected time for a true royal sovereign and illustrious individuals had arrived, but Heaven did not yet wish the kingdom to have tranquillity and good order. He said that if Heaven wished for this, he was the one who could bring it about, which explained his dissatisfaction.

Chapter 14 body

Mencius left Qi and dwelt in Xiu. In response to Gong Sun Chou, Mencius explained that the ancients did not generally hold office without receiving salary. Mencius had intended to leave after seeing the king in Chong and therefore declined salary, but orders for the collection of troops made it improper to ask permission to leave, although remaining in Qi was not his purpose.
