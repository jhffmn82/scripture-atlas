Abstract written by a model

This unit from Book IX, Tsze Han, presents the Master through sayings, encounters, and the responses of his disciples. He seldom speaks of profitableness, Heaven’s appointments, and perfect virtue, yet he is portrayed as learned, adaptable, ceremonially principled, free of egoism and obstinacy, and confident that Heaven protects the cause of truth entrusted to him. Though praised for many abilities, he attributes them to his low early condition and lack of office, and he denies being inherently knowledgeable, saying instead that he fully explains questions put to him. He shows reverence toward mourners, the formally dressed, and blind people; reforms music after returning from Wei to Lu; and rejects a false display of ministerial attendants during illness. He would sell a beautiful gem only when its price is offered, considers living among eastern tribes, and reflects pessimistically that the FANG bird and river map no longer appear. The Master’s teaching is described as profound and demanding. Yen Yuan finds it ever higher, firmer, and elusive, though taught through orderly method, learning, and propriety. The Master compares learning to building a mound: progress or stopping belongs to the learner. He praises Hui and Yen Yuan for unceasing advancement, while observing that growth does not always reach flowering or fruit. He urges respect for youth, faithfulness and sincerity, abandoning faults, and turning admonition into reformed conduct and gentle advice into clarified purpose. The closing sayings stress inner character: a common person’s will cannot be taken, Yu’s freedom from shame and desire falls short of perfect excellence, pine and cypress endure cold, and the wise, virtuous, and bold are free respectively from perplexity, anxiety, and fear. Companionship in study does not guarantee shared principle, establishment, or judgment. The final image of fluttering aspen-plum flowers concludes that distance comes from want of thought, not from the remoteness of a house.

The Analects of Confucius / CHAP. I. The subjects of which the Master seldom spoke body

This unit concerns The Analects of Confucius, specifically Book IX, Tsze Han. It records that Confucius seldom spoke about profitableness, the appointments of Heaven, and perfect virtue. These three subjects are identified as matters the Master rarely discussed.

CHAP. II. 1. A man of the village of Ta-hsiang said, 'Great body

A man from the village of Ta-hsiang praises the philosopher K'ung as great and extensively learned. He says K'ung does not make his name famous through any particular achievement. K'ung responds by asking whether he should practise charioteering or archery, and chooses charioteering.

CHAP. III. 1. The Master said, 'The linen cap is that prescribed body

The Master discusses the linen cap, the silk cap, and practices governed by the rules of ceremony. He says the linen cap is prescribed, but he follows the common practice of wearing the more economical silk cap. He also says the rules of ceremony prescribe bowing below the hall, while common practice is to bow after ascending it, which he calls arrogant; he continues to bow below the hall.

CHAP. IV. There were four things from which the Master was body

The unit concerns the Master and four things from which he was entirely free. He had no foregone conclusions or arbitrary predeterminations. He also had no obstinacy and no egoism.

CHAP. V. 1. The Master was put in fear in K'wang. body

The Master was put in fear in K'wang. He says that after the death of King Wan, the cause of truth was lodged in him. He believes that if Heaven does not let the cause of truth perish, the people of K'wang cannot harm him.

CHAP. VI. 1. A high officer asked Tsze-kung, saying, 'May we body

A high officer asks Tsze-kung whether the Master may be called a sage, noting his many abilities. Tsze-kung says Heaven endowed the Master abundantly and that he is nearly a sage with varied ability. The Master explains that his low condition when young led him to acquire many abilities in mean matters, but says a superior man does not need such variety. Lao reports that the Master said he acquired many arts because he had no official employment.

CHAP. VII. The Master said, 'Am I indeed possessed of body

The Master says that he is not truly knowledgeable. He explains that when a seemingly empty and mean person asks him something, he presents the subject fully from beginning to end. He claims to exhaust the matter in his explanation.

CHAP. VIII. The Master said, 'The FANG bird does not come; body

The Master says that the FANG bird does not come and that the river sends forth no map. He concludes that it is all over with him.

CHAP. IX. When the Master saw a person in a mourning dress, body

The Master observes people approaching him. When he sees a person in a mourning dress, a person in full dress, or a blind person, he rises even if they are younger than himself. If he must pass by them, he does so hastily.

CHAP. X. 1. Yen Yuan, in admiration of the Master's doctrines, body

Yen Yuan admires the Master's doctrines and reflects on their depth and elusiveness. He finds that the doctrines seem higher when he looks up to them, firmer when he tries to penetrate them, and suddenly behind him when he looks before him. The Master leads people on through orderly method, learning, and the restraints of propriety. Yen Yuan cannot give up studying the doctrines, but despite exerting all his ability, he finds no way to follow and grasp them.

CHAP. XI. 1. The Master being very ill, Tsze-lu wished the body

The unit concerns the Master, who is very ill, and Tsze-lu's response to his condition. Tsze-lu wishes the disciples to act as ministers to the Master, but the Master condemns this as deceitful because he has no ministers. During a remission, the Master says he would rather die in the hands of his disciples than in the hands of ministers, and accepts dying without a great burial or on the road.

CHAP. XII. Tsze-kung said, 'There is a beautiful gem here. body

Tsze-kung asks the Master what to do with a beautiful gem. He considers storing it in a case or seeking a good price and selling it. The Master says to sell it, but to wait for someone to offer the price.

CHAP. XIII. 1. The Master was wishing to go and live among body

The Master wishes to go and live among the nine wild tribes of the east. Someone questions how he could do this because they are rude. The Master replies that if a superior man lived among them, there would be no rudeness.

CHAP. XIV. The Master said, 'I returned from Wei to Lu, and body

The Master said that he returned from Wei to Lu. After his return, the music was reformed. The pieces in the Royal songs and Praise songs all found their proper places.

CHAP. XV. The Master said, 'Abroad, to serve the high body

The Master asks which of four duties he has attained. These duties are serving high ministers and nobles abroad, serving one's father and elder brothers at home, exerting oneself fully in all duties to the dead, and not being overcome by wine. The Master presents these duties as measures of his conduct.

CHAP. XVI. The Master standing by a stream, said, 'It passes body

The Master stands by a stream. He says that it passes on just like this. He adds that it does not cease day or night.

CHAP. XVII. The Master said, 'I have not seen one who loves body

The Master says that he has not seen anyone who loves virtue as much as he loves beauty. The statement concerns the Master, virtue, and beauty.

CHAP. XVIII. The Master said, 'The prosecution of learning body

The Master explains that the prosecution of learning may be compared to raising a mound. If only one basket of earth remains and a person stops, the stopping is their own work. Even when earth is thrown onto level ground one basketful at a time, advancing with it is their own going forward.

CHAP. XIX. The Master said, 'Never flagging when I set forth body

The Master praises Hui for never flagging when the Master sets forth anything to him. The statement concerns the Master's observation of Hui's attentive constancy. Hui is identified as the person who shows this quality.

CHAP. XX. The Master said of Yen Yuan, 'Alas! I saw his body

The Master speaks about Yen Yuan. He says he saw Yen Yuan constantly advance. He never saw Yen Yuan stop in his progress.

CHAP. XXI. The Master said, 'There are cases in which the body

The Master presents cases involving a blade, a plant, flowering, and fruit. In some cases, the blade springs but the plant does not go on to flower. In other cases, the plant flowers but subsequently produces no fruit.

CHAP. XXII. The Master said, 'A youth is to be regarded with body

The Master says that a youth should be regarded with respect. He explains that the youth’s future may become equal to the present of those judging him. If the youth reaches forty or fifty without making himself heard of, he will no longer be worth regarding with respect.

CHAP. XXV. The Master said, 'Can men refuse to assent to the body

The Master says that men may assent to words of strict admonition, but the valuable result is that their conduct is reformed because of them. He also says that men may be pleased with words of gentle advice, but the valuable result is that their aim is unfolded. A man who is pleased with words of gentle advice without unfolding their aim, or who assents to words of strict admonition without reforming his conduct, is someone with whom the Master can do nothing.

CHAP. XXIV. The Master said, 'Hold faithfulness and sincerity body

The Master teaches principles for living well. He says to hold faithfulness and sincerity as first principles. He advises having no friends who are not equal to oneself. He also says that when a person has faults, the person should not fear abandoning them.

CHAP. XXV. The Master said, 'The commander of the forces of body

The Master states that the commander of the forces of a large state may be carried off. He contrasts this with the will of even a common man, which cannot be taken from him. The unit concerns the vulnerability of military authority and the inviolability of personal will.

CHAP. XXVI. 1. The Master said, 'Dressed himself in a tattered body

The Master says that Yu can wear a tattered hemp-quilted robe among men dressed in furs without shame. He quotes that a person who dislikes none and covets nothing can do only what is good. Tsze-lu repeatedly recites these words from the ode. The Master says that such qualities are not sufficient to constitute perfect excellence.

CHAP. XXVII. The Master said, 'When the year becomes cold, body

The Master explains what is learned when the year becomes cold. Cold weather reveals that the pine and the cypress are the last trees to lose their leaves. The saying concerns the endurance of the pine and cypress during the cold year.

CHAP. XXVIII. The Master said, 'The wise are free from body

The Master states that wise people are free from perplexities. He states that virtuous people are free from anxiety. He also states that bold people are free from fear.

CHAP. XXIX. The Master said, 'There are some with whom we body

The Master says that some people can study together with others, but may be unable to proceed together to principles. Even if they proceed to principles together, they may be unable to become established in those principles together. If they become established together, they may still be unable to weigh occurring events together.

CHAP. XXX. 1. How the flowers of the aspen-plum flutter and body

The unit concerns the flowers of the aspen-plum, an addressee, the addressee’s distant house, and The Master. The flowers flutter and turn, prompting the speaker to think of the addressee. The Master says that distance results from a want of thought and asks how the house can be distant.
