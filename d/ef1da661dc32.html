Abstract written by a model

This record is Book XIII, “Tsze-Lu,” of The Analects: a sequence of teachings by the Master, often prompted by disciples and rulers, on government, character, learning, and leadership. Government begins with a leader’s example, diligence, correct personal conduct, competent officers, forgiveness of small faults, and promotion of virtuous, talented people. The Master stresses rectifying names so speech accords with reality and action can succeed; he also says that numerous people should be enriched and then taught. Good government makes nearby people happy and attracts distant people, while haste, pursuit of small advantages, and a ruler who tolerates no opposition endanger it. Personal correction is central: a prince or minister who is correct can govern effectively, but one unable to rectify himself cannot rectify others. The Master values practical learning over mere recitation, and predicts that sustained rule by good men can reform even the violently bad. He contrasts the superior man, proper, sincere, dignified, capable, affable without flattery, with the mean man, and identifies firmness, endurance, simplicity, modesty, constancy, and perfect virtue as essential qualities. He also discusses family concealment of wrongdoing as uprightness, ranks worthy officers and scholars, and warns that people must be taught before being used in war; otherwise, leading them into battle is throwing them away.

The Analects, Book XIII: Tsze-Lu / CHAP. I. 1. Tsze-lu asked about government. The Master said, body

Tsze-lu asks the Master about government. The Master teaches that a leader should go before the people by example and work diligently in their affairs. When Tsze-lu asks for further instruction, the Master tells him not to become weary in these duties.

CHAP. II. 1. Chung-kung, being chief minister to the Head of body

Chung-kung, chief minister to the Head of the Chi family, asks the Master about government. The Master advises employing various officers, pardoning small faults, and raising men of virtue and talents to office. When Chung-kung asks how to identify such men, the Master says to raise those he knows, because others will not neglect those he does not know.

CHAP. III. 1. Tsze-lu said, 'The ruler of Wei has been waiting body

Tsze-lu asks The Master what should be done first if he administers the government with the ruler of Wei. The Master says that names must first be rectified. He explains that incorrect names make language fail to accord with the truth of things, preventing affairs from succeeding and disrupting proprieties, music, and punishments. The superior man therefore uses names appropriately and ensures that his words can be carried out correctly.

CHAP. IV. 1. Fan Ch'ih requested to be taught husbandry. The body

Fan Ch'ih asks the Master to teach him husbandry and gardening. The Master says he is not as skilled in those subjects as an old husbandman or gardener. After Fan Ch'ih leaves, the Master calls him a small man. The Master teaches that a superior who loves propriety, righteousness, and good faith will inspire reverence, submission, and sincerity in the people, who will come to him with their children.

CHAP. V. The Master said, 'Though a man may be able to body

The Master discusses a man who can recite the three hundred odes. He says that if the man cannot act when entrusted with a governmental charge or reply unaided when sent on a mission, his learning has limited practical value. The unit concerns the relationship between learning and effective action.

CHAP. VI. The Master said, 'When a prince's personal conduct body

The Master explains how a prince's personal conduct affects his government. When the prince's personal conduct is correct, his government is effective without issuing orders. When his personal conduct is not correct, he may issue orders, but they will not be followed.

CHAP. VII. The Master said, 'The governments of Lu and Wei body

The Master states that the governments of Lu and Wei are brothers. The statement concerns the relationship between the governments of Lu and Wei.

CHAP. VIII. The Master said of Ching, a scion of the ducal body

The Master speaks about Ching, a scion of the ducal family of Wei. He says that Ching knew the economy of a family well. As Ching’s means increased, he described his possessions first as a collection, then as complete, and finally as admirable when he became rich.

CHAP. IX. 1. When the Master went to Wei, Zan Yu acted as body

The Master goes to Wei with Zan Yu acting as the driver of his carriage. He observes that the people are numerous. He says that the numerous people should first be enriched and then taught.

CHAP. X. The Master said, 'If there were (any of the princes) body

The Master says that if any of the princes employed him, he would accomplish something considerable within twelve months. He adds that within three years, the government would be perfected. The statement concerns the Master’s potential service to the princes and its effect on government.

CHAP. XI. The Master said, '"If good men were to govern a body

The Master says that if good men governed a country in succession for a hundred years, they could transform the violently bad. He adds that such governance would make capital punishments unnecessary. The statement is affirmed as true.

CHAP. XII. The Master said, 'If a truly royal ruler were to body

The Master says that if a truly royal ruler were to arise, the ruler’s influence would not prevail immediately. He says that a generation would still be required. After that generation, virtue would prevail.

CHAP. XIII. The Master said, 'If a minister make his own body

The Master teaches that a minister who makes his own conduct correct will have no difficulty assisting in government. He asks what a minister can do to rectify others if he cannot rectify himself. The unit concerns the minister’s conduct, government, and others who may need correction.

CHAP. XIV. The disciple Zan returning from the court, the body

Disciple Zan returns late from the court and tells the Master that he had government business. The Master suggests that Zan's lateness must have involved family affairs instead. He adds that if there had been government business, he would have been consulted despite no longer being in office.

CHAP. XV. 1. The Duke Ting asked whether there was a single body

Duke Ting asks Confucius whether a single sentence could make a country prosperous or ruin it. Confucius says that one sentence cannot be expected to have either effect, but discusses sayings about the difficulty of being a prince and the danger of a ruler whose words face no opposition. He explains that recognizing the difficulty of ruling may contribute to the prosperity of the country, while good words may properly face no opposition but bad words without opposition may lead to its ruin.

CHAP. XVI. 1. The Duke of Sheh asked about government. body

The Duke of Sheh asks the Master about government. The Master explains that good government obtains when those who are near are made happy. He adds that those who are far off are attracted under such government.

CHAP. XVII. Tsze-hsia, being governor of Chu-fu, asked about body

Tsze-hsia, serving as governor of Chu-fu, asks the Master about government. The Master advises against wanting things done quickly and against focusing on small advantages. He explains that haste prevents thorough completion, while attention to small advantages prevents great affairs from being accomplished.

CHAP. XVIII. 1. The Duke of Sheh informed Confucius, saying, body

The Duke of Sheh tells Confucius that people in his community considered upright will testify when their father has stolen a sheep. Confucius says that upright people in his community instead conceal a father’s misconduct from others and a son’s misconduct from others. He teaches that uprightness is found in this concealment between fathers and sons.

CHAP. XIX. Fan Ch'ih asked about perfect virtue. The Master body

Fan Ch'ih asks the Master about perfect virtue. The Master teaches that it consists of sedate gravity in retirement, reverent attentiveness in business, and strict sincerity in intercourse with others. He adds that these qualities should not be neglected even when a man goes among rude, uncultivated tribes.

CHAP. XX. 1. Tsze-kung asked, saying, 'What qualities must a body

Tsze-kung asks the Master what qualities qualify a man to be called an officer. The Master says an officer maintains a sense of shame in his conduct and does not disgrace his prince’s commission when sent abroad. The next ranks include those regarded as filial and fraternal, and those who are sincere in speech and action, though obstinate. The Master dismisses those of the present day who engage in government as insignificant and unworthy of consideration.

CHAP. XXI. The Master said, 'Since I cannot get men pursuing body

The Master says he cannot find men pursuing the due medium to whom he can communicate his instructions. He therefore seeks the ardent and the cautiously-decided. The ardent will advance and lay hold of truth, while the cautiously-decided will keep themselves from what is wrong.

CHAP. XXII. 1. The Master said, 'The people of the south have body

The Master discusses a saying among the people of the south about constancy. He says that a man without constancy cannot be either a wizard or a doctor. He adds that a person inconstant in virtue will be visited with disgrace, because he has not attended to the prognostication.

CHAP. XXIII. The Master said, 'The superior man is affable, body

The Master contrasts the superior man with the mean man. The superior man is affable but not adulatory. The mean man is adulatory but not affable.

CHAP. XXIV. Tsze-kung asked, saying, 'What do you say of a body

Tsze-kung asks the Master about a man loved by everyone in his neighborhood and about a man hated by everyone there. The Master says that universal love does not by itself justify approval, and universal hatred does not by itself prove that a man is bad. He says the better situation is for the good people in the neighborhood to love a man and the bad people to hate him.

CHAP. XXV. The Master said, 'The superior man is easy to body

The Master contrasts the superior man with the mean man in how each is served and pleased. The superior man is difficult to please when approached contrary to right, but he employs people according to their capacity. The mean man is easy to please even through improper means, but he expects everyone he employs to be equal to everything.

CHAP. XXVI. The Master said, 'The superior man has a body

The Master distinguishes the superior man from the mean man. The superior man has dignified ease without pride. The mean man has pride without a dignified ease.

CHAP. XXVII. The Master said, 'The firm, the enduring, the body

The Master states that four qualities are near to virtue. These qualities are firmness, endurance, simplicity, and modesty. The teaching concerns the relationship between these qualities and virtue.

CHAP. XXVIII. Tsze-lu asked, saying, 'What qualities must a body

Tsze-lu asks the Master what qualities qualify a man to be called a scholar. The Master says the man must be earnest, urgent, and bland. He should be earnest and urgent among his friends, and bland among his brethren.

CHAP. XXIX. The Master said, 'Let a good man teach the body

The Master says that a good man should teach the people for seven years. After that period, the people may likewise be employed in war. The statement concerns the Master, a good man, the people, and war.

CHAP. XXX. The Master said, 'To lead an uninstructed people body

The Master says that leading an uninstructed people to war is to throw them away. The statement concerns the danger of sending people without instruction into war.
