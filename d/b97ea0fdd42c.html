Esther (Greek)

author unknown · 115 to 47 BC · 10 sections
Esther (Greek), from the World English Bible, Catholic Edition, dedicated to the public domain by eBible.org. Shown here as plain text, with facts drawn from it. https://ebible.org/eng-web-c/

Abstract written by a model

This is the Greek version of the Book of Esther, told in ten chapters, about how a plot to destroy the Jews of King Ahasuerus's realm is reversed. The central figures are Mordecai, a Jew of the tribe of Benjamin serving at Susa; his cousin Esther, whom he raised and who becomes queen; King Ahasuerus; and Haman, the king's favored official and the villain. The text opens with Mordecai's vision of two serpents and conflicting nations, and his exposure of a plot by two chamberlains against the king. Ahasuerus holds great feasts, and Queen Vashti refuses his summons, so on Memucan's advice she is removed. Beautiful virgins are gathered, and Esther, who at Mordecai's command hides her family and country, is loved above all and crowned. Mordecai again uncovers a chamberlains' plot through Esther, and his goodwill is recorded in the royal library. Haman is promoted, and Mordecai alone refuses to bow to him. Enraged, Haman casts lots, which fall on the fourteenth of Adar, offers ten thousand talents of silver, and obtains a decree, sent to 127 provinces, ordering the Jews' destruction. Mordecai mourns and urges Esther to plead with the king, asking who knows whether she became queen for this occasion. She asks the Jews to fast three days, and both she and Mordecai pray to the Lord. Esther goes uncalled before the king, and God changes his anger to gentleness. She invites the king and Haman to two banquets, while Haman, boasting to his wife Zeresh and friends, prepares a fifty-cubit gallows for Mordecai. That night the king, sleepless, learns Mordecai was never rewarded and has Haman honor Mordecai publicly. At the second banquet Esther names Haman as the enemy of her people. The king, finding Haman fallen on her couch, takes this as an assault, and Haman is hanged on his own gallows. Mordecai receives the king's ring and Haman's holdings, and Esther obtains a reversing letter, dated the twenty-third of Nisan, permitting the Jews to defend themselves on the thirteenth of Adar. Many Gentiles become Jews out of fear. On that day the Jews overcome their adversaries, killing five hundred in Susa including Haman's ten sons, three hundred more the next day, and fifteen thousand elsewhere, taking no spoil. Mordecai establishes the fourteenth and fifteenth of Adar as Purim, named for the lots, a feast of joy and gifts to be kept forever. The final chapter notes Mordecai's high office, then gives his interpretation of the opening dream: the river is Esther, the serpents are Haman and himself, and God ordained two lots. It closes with a colophon saying Dositheus and his son Ptolemeus brought the Purim letter to Egypt, as interpreted by Lysimachus in Jerusalem.

Esther (Greek) / Chapter 1 body

Chapter 1 of Greek Esther opens with Mordecai, a Jew serving in the palace at Susa, who sees a vision of two serpents, conflict among nations, and the exaltation of the lowly. He then uncovers a plot by the king's chamberlains Gabatha and Tharrha against King Ahasuerus, and the king has them executed and rewards Mordecai. Haman, favored by the king, seeks to harm Mordecai and his people. In the third year of his reign Ahasuerus holds a lavish feast of one hundred eighty days and a six-day banquet, while Queen Vashti holds a banquet for the women. On the seventh day Vashti refuses the king's summons, and on Memucan's advice the king issues a decree to remove her royalty and to ensure that wives honor their husbands.

Chapter 2 body

After the king's anger over Vashti subsides, his servants propose gathering beautiful young virgins from all the provinces to Susa so that the one who pleases him can replace her as queen. Mordecai, a Jew of the tribe of Benjamin who was carried captive from Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar, has raised his cousin Esther, and she is brought to Hegai, keeper of the women, who favors her. At Mordecai's command Esther does not reveal her family or country, and she keeps her manner of life. After twelve months of purification she goes in to King Ahasuerus, who loves her above all the others, crowns her queen, and holds a seven-day banquet. When two chamberlains plot to kill the king, Mordecai discovers it and tells Esther, who informs the king; the chamberlains are hanged and Mordecai's goodwill is recorded in the royal library.

Chapter 3 body

King Ahasuerus promotes Haman the son of Hammedatha, the Bugaean, above all his friends, and everyone in the palace bows to him except Mordecai, who has told them he is a Jew. Enraged when palace officials report Mordecai's refusal, Haman plots to destroy all the Jews in Ahasuerus's realm and casts lots, which fall on the fourteenth day of Adar. Haman tells the king that a scattered nation with different laws disobeys the king and offers ten thousand talents of silver for its destruction. The king gives Haman his ring and tells him to keep the silver and treat the nation as he wills, so decrees in the king's name are sent by couriers to all 127 provinces ordering the Jews' destruction and plunder. The king and Haman drink while the city of Susa is confused.

Chapter 4 body

Mordecai learns of the decree against the Jews, tears his garments, puts on sackcloth and ashes, and cries out in the city, while mourning spreads through every province. Esther, troubled, sends clothes and then her chamberlain Hathach to learn the truth; Mordecai tells him of Haman's promise of ten thousand talents to the treasury and gives him a copy of the decree, urging Esther to plead with the king. Esther answers that approaching the king uncalled means death unless he extends his golden sceptre, but Mordecai warns that she and her father's house will perish if she stays silent and asks who knows whether she was made queen for this occasion. Esther agrees to go in contrary to the law and asks the Jews of Susa to fast three days, and Mordecai carries this out. The chapter closes with a long prayer by Mordecai and a long prayer by Esther, both appealing to the Lord God to save his people from destruction.

Chapter 5 body

On the third day, Esther stops praying, dresses in her glorious apparel, and goes before the king, who looks at her with anger until God changes his spirit to gentleness. The king comforts her after she faints, extends the golden sceptre, and offers her up to half his kingdom. Esther asks that he and Haman come to a feast she will prepare, and at the banquet she postpones her request by inviting them again for the next day. Haman leaves glad but is enraged at seeing Mordecai the Jew in the court, and he boasts of his wealth and honor to his friends and his wife Zeresh. At their advice he has a fifty cubit gallows prepared so that Mordecai can be hanged on it.

Chapter 6 body

The king cannot sleep and has the records read to him, discovering that Mordecai once exposed two chamberlains who plotted against Ahasuerus and that Mordecai was never rewarded. Haman arrives in the court intending to ask for Mordecai's hanging, but the king asks him what should be done for a man he wishes to honor. Assuming it is himself, Haman describes the king's robe and horse and a public proclamation, and the king orders him to do all this for Mordecai the Jew. Haman carries it out, then goes home mourning with his head covered, where his wife Zeresh and his friends warn that he will fall before Mordecai. The chamberlains then arrive to hurry Haman to Esther's banquet.

Chapter 7 body

At a second banquet, Queen Esther asks the king to spare her life and her people, who she says have been sold for destruction and genocide. When the king demands to know who is responsible, Esther names Haman as the enemy, and Haman is terrified. The king leaves for the garden, and on returning finds Haman fallen on Esther's couch begging her for mercy, which the king takes as an assault. The chamberlain Bugathan tells the king about the fifty-cubit gallows Haman prepared for Mordecai, and the king orders Haman hanged on it. Haman is hanged there, and the king's wrath subsides.

Chapter 8 body

King Ahasuerus gives Esther all that belonged to Haman, and Mordecai receives the king's ring and is appointed over Haman's former holdings. Esther pleads again, falling at the king's feet, and asks that Haman's letters against the Jews be reversed. The king tells her to write in his name whatever seems good and seal it with his ring. On the twenty-third day of Nisan the scribes write orders to the governors of 127 provinces from India to Ethiopia, permitting the Jews to use their own laws and defend themselves against attackers on the thirteenth day of Adar. The text of the letter denounces Haman, and Mordecai goes out in royal apparel while the Jews rejoice and many Gentiles are circumcised and become Jews for fear of them.

Chapter 9 body

On the thirteenth of Adar the king's letters arrived and the Jews' adversaries perished, since no one resisted out of fear of the Jews and of Mordecai. In Susa the Jews killed five hundred men, including the ten sons of Haman, and the king, hearing this, asked Esther what more she wanted. Esther requested a second day of the same action in Susa and the hanging of Haman's sons' bodies, which the king allowed; the Jews of Susa killed three hundred more on the fourteenth, while the Jews elsewhere destroyed fifteen thousand, taking no spoil. Mordecai then wrote to all the Jews of Ahasuerus's kingdom establishing the fourteenth and fifteenth of Adar as days of feasting, joy, and gifts to friends and the poor, named Purim after the lots Haman cast. Mordecai and Esther confirmed this observance by letter and command as a memorial to be kept forever.

Chapter 10 body

Chapter 10 opens with King Ahasuerus levying a tax on land and sea, and notes that his strength and glory are recorded in the book of the Persians and Medes. Mordecai is described as viceroy to the king, a great man honored by the Jews and loved by his nation. In a bracketed passage, Mordecai says these events came from God and interprets his earlier dream: the spring that became a river is Esther, the two serpents are Haman and himself, and the nations are those who sought to destroy the Jews. He says God ordained two lots, one for his people and one for the other nations, delivered Israel, and commanded that the days of the fourteenth and fifteenth of Adar be observed forever. The chapter closes with a note that Dositheus and his son Ptolemeus brought the letter of Purim to Egypt in the fourth year of Ptolemeus and Cleopatra, and that Lysimachus in Jerusalem had interpreted it.
