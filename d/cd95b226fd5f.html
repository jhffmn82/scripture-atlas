Abstract written by a model

The Second Book of the Maccabees opens with two letters from the Jews of Jerusalem and Judea to the Jews of Egypt, urging them to keep the feast marking the temple's purification. The letters recall the hidden altar fire, later found as a liquid called Nephthar, and Jeremiah's hiding of the tabernacle, ark and incense altar. An abridger then explains that he is condensing Jason of Cyrene's five books on Judas Maccabaeus, the temple's purification, and the wars against Antiochus Epiphanes and Eupator. The narrative begins in a peaceful Jerusalem under the godly high priest Onias. Simon's quarrel with Onias leads King Seleucus to send Heliodorus to seize the temple treasury, but heavenly figures scourge him to the ground. Under Antiochus Epiphanes the priesthood is bought by Jason and then by Menelaus, who has Onias murdered, and Greek customs spread. Antiochus invades Jerusalem, plunders the temple, and later forces the Jews to abandon their laws, renaming the sanctuary for Olympian Zeus. The narrator treats this as God's chastening. Eleazer, a ninety-year-old scribe, and seven brothers with their mother die rather than eat swine's flesh, declaring their hope in resurrection. Judas Maccabaeus then gathers faithful Jews and, with his brothers, defeats Nicanor, Timotheus and Bacchides. Antiochus, struck by an incurable illness, repents, vows to become a Jew, and dies in a strange land. Judas recovers and rededicates the temple on the twenty-fifth of Chislev, founding an eight-day festival. Under Antiochus Eupator, Lysias leads a vast army, but a heavenly horseman aids the Jews and Lysias sues for peace; letters from the king and Roman ambassadors confirm Jewish rights. Fighting continues with neighbouring peoples. After one battle Judas sends two thousand drachmas to Jerusalem for a sin offering, linked to belief in the resurrection. Eupator marches again with Lysias and Menelaus, who is put to death, and makes peace with the Jews. In the closing chapters Demetrius takes the country, and Alcimus accuses Judas. Demetrius sends Nicanor, who first befriends Judas, then turns hostile and threatens to level the temple. The elder Razis dies rather than be captured. Nicanor plans to attack on the Sabbath, but Judas, encouraged by a dream of Onias and Jeremiah, prays for a good angel and routs the army. Nicanor is found dead, and his head and arm are displayed in Jerusalem. The people decree the thirteenth of Adar a day of honor, and the author ends with remarks on his style.

The Second Book of the Maccabees / Chapter 1 body

The Jews of Jerusalem and Judea write a letter to the Jews of Egypt, praying for their welfare and urging them to keep the feast of tabernacles in the month Chislev in the 188th year. They recall their earlier letter from the time of distress after Jason and his company revolted, and thank God for rescuing them, including the death of Antiochus, who was cut down by the priests of Nanaea's temple in Persia. They explain that the feast marks the purification of the temple and remembers the altar fire in Nehemiah's time. The godly priests had hidden the fire in a dry well before the exile to Persia, and Nehemiah's men found only a thick liquid there, which, sprinkled on the sacrifice, kindled a great blaze when the sun shone. The Persian king enclosed and sanctified the place, and the liquid was called Nephthar, meaning Cleansing.

Chapter 2 body

Chapter 2 continues the letter to the Jews in Egypt, recounting records about Jeremiah the prophet, who charged the exiles not to forget the Lord's law amid idols of gold and silver. Jeremiah hid the tabernacle, the ark, and the altar of incense in a cave on the mountain where Moses had gone up, sealed it, and rebuked followers who could not find it, saying the place would stay unknown until God gathers the people and shows mercy. The letter also recalls Moses' and Solomon's sacrifices consumed by fire, Nehemiah's library, and Judas' gathering of scattered books, and invites the recipients to celebrate the purification days. Then the abridger introduces the work: the story of Judas Maccabaeus and his brothers, the temple's purification, and the wars against Antiochus Epiphanes and Eupator, drawn from Jason of Cyrene's five books. The abridger explains the aim of condensing them into one book, comparing the labor to a banquet host and a house decorator, and ends by saying a long prologue would be foolish.

Chapter 3 body

In Jerusalem, peace and the godly high priest Onias kept the laws, and King Seleucus even paid the sacrificial costs. Simon, guardian of the temple, quarreled with Onias over the city market and, failing to prevail, told Apollonius of Tarsus that the temple treasury held vast sums the king could seize. The king sent his chancellor Heliodorus, who learned from Onias that the treasury held deposits of widows, orphans, and Hyrcanus son of Tobias, totaling four hundred talents of silver and two hundred of gold, but insisted on confiscating them. As the city prayed in distress, a horse with a golden-armored rider and two radiant young men appeared and scourged Heliodorus to the ground; Onias then offered a sacrifice, the young men told Heliodorus he was spared for Onias's sake, and Heliodorus returned to the king testifying to God's power in that place.

Chapter 4 body

Chapter 4 recounts how Simon slandered the high priest Onias, who appealed to the king for peace. After Seleucus died and Antiochus Epiphanes became king, Onias's brother Jason bought the high priesthood with promised silver, built a gymnasium under the citadel, and pushed Jerusalem toward Greek customs, so that the priests neglected the altar. Menelaus, Simon's brother, then outbid Jason by three hundred talents, drove him into the country of the Ammonites, and stole temple vessels. When Onias reproved him, Menelaus induced Andronicus to kill Onias by treachery, and Antiochus, grieved, had Andronicus executed. Menelaus's brother Lysimachus committed sacrileges and was killed by the crowd, and Menelaus bribed Ptolemy the son of Dorymenes to win the king over, so that the three men who pleaded against him were sentenced to death while Menelaus kept his office.

Chapter 5 body

During Antiochus's second invasion of Egypt, signs of armed cavalry appeared in the sky over the city for almost forty days. On a false rumor of Antiochus's death, Jason attacked Jerusalem with a thousand men and slaughtered his own citizens, forcing Menelaus into the citadel, but he failed to take control and died in exile. Believing Judea in revolt, Antiochus took the city by force, ordered a massacre, and entered the temple with Menelaus as his guide, plundering it. The narrator explains that the Lord's anger over the people's sins permitted this, and that the place shares in the nation's fortunes. Antiochus left governors, including Philip, Andronicus and Menelaus, and sent Apollonius, who killed many on the Sabbath, while Judas Maccabaeus and about nine others withdrew to the mountains.

Chapter 6 body

The king sends an old man of Athens to force the Jews to abandon their ancestral laws and to pollute the sanctuary in Jerusalem, renaming it for Olympian Zeus. The Jews suffer a harsh persecution: they cannot keep the Sabbath or feasts, are forced to eat sacrifices and join Dionysian processions, and those who circumcise their children or keep the Sabbath in caves are killed. The narrator urges readers not to be discouraged, explaining that these calamities are God's chastening of his people rather than their destruction. The chapter then recounts Eleazar, a ninety-year-old scribe, who refuses to eat swine's flesh or even pretend to, and dies under the scourge as an example of nobility and virtue to the young and to the nation.

Chapter 7 body

Seven brothers and their mother are seized at the king's command and tortured to force them to eat swine's flesh. The spokesman brother is mutilated and fried alive, and the next five brothers each die after torture while declaring their hope in resurrection and warning the king of God's judgment. The mother is praised for her courage, exhorting her sons in the language of their fathers and teaching that the Creator will give back their spirit and life. King Antiochus offers the youngest son wealth, honor, and friendship, then urges the mother to persuade him; she instead urges him to accept death, and he defies the king and dies after being treated worse than the others. The mother dies last, and the chapter closes by noting the sacrificial feasts and extreme tortures.

Chapter 8 body

Judas Maccabaeus secretly gathers about six thousand faithful Jews, who pray for the Lord's mercy on the people, sanctuary and city, and he trains them and wins back positions through night raids. Philip alerts Ptolemy, who sends Nicanor and Gorgias with at least twenty thousand troops; Nicanor plans to pay the king's two thousand talent tribute to the Romans by selling Jewish captives. Some Jews flee, but Maccabaeus exhorts the rest to trust almighty God, recalls past divine help, and divides his army among himself and his brothers Simon, Joseph and Jonathan, with Eleazer leading the first band. They defeat Nicanor's army, keep the Sabbath, share spoils with the maimed, widows and orphans, and then defeat Timotheus and Bacchides and burn Callisthenes. Nicanor flees to Antioch and admits that the Jews have One who fights for them.

Chapter 9 body

Antiochus flees Persia after failing to rob a temple at Persepolis, and on hearing news of Nicanor and Timotheus's forces at Ecbatana he vows to make Jerusalem a graveyard for the Jews. God strikes him with an incurable pain of the bowels, and he falls from his speeding chariot, then suffers worms, falling flesh and a stench that repels his army. Broken in spirit, he acknowledges that mortals must be subject to God and vows to free Jerusalem, make the Jews equal to Athenian citizens, restore and adorn the sanctuary, and become a Jew. When his suffering does not cease, he writes the Jews a letter of supplication appointing his son Antiochus as king and asking for their continued goodwill. He dies in the mountains in a strange land, and Philip, his foster brother, takes the body home and then withdraws to Ptolemy Philometor in Egypt, fearing the son.

Chapter 10 body

Maccabaeus and his men, led by the Lord, recover the temple and city, tear down foreign altars, build a new altar, and rededicate the sanctuary on the twenty-fifth of Chislev, instituting an annual eight-day festival. Under Antiochus Eupator, who appoints Lysias as governor, Ptolemy Macron kills himself after accusations and Gorgias wages war on the Jews with the Idumaeans' help. Maccabaeus attacks the Idumaean strongholds, kills Simon and his colleagues' fellow officers for taking a bribe, and takes the two towers. When Timotheus invades with a large foreign army, the Jews pray and are aided in battle by heavenly horsemen, killing over twenty thousand. Timotheus flees to Gazara, which the Jews storm on the fifth day, killing Timotheus, Chaereas, and Apollophanes, and they bless the Lord.

Chapter 11 body

Lysias, the king's guardian and chancellor, gathers about eighty thousand infantry, all his cavalry, and eighty elephants to attack the Jews, planning to make Jerusalem a Greek home, tax the temple, and sell the high priesthood yearly. He besieges the stronghold of Bethsuron, and Maccabaeus and the people pray for a good angel; a white-clad horseman with golden weapons appears at their head, and the Jews rout the enemy, killing eleven thousand infantry and one thousand six hundred cavalry. Lysias flees, concludes that God fights for the Hebrews, and sues for terms, which Maccabaeus accepts for the common good. The chapter then reproduces letters from Lysias, King Antiochus (to Lysias and to the Jewish senate, mentioning Menelaus), and the Roman ambassadors Quintus Memmius and Titus Manius, which grant the Jews their temple, their customs, and amnesty for those who return home.

Chapter 12 body

After a peace agreement, hostile governors including Timotheus and Nicanor refused to let the Jews live quietly, and the men of Joppa drowned at least two hundred Jews they had invited onto boats. Judas avenged them by burning the harbors and fleets at Joppa and Jamnia, defeated an Arab army and made peace with it, and took the city of Caspin with great slaughter. He then routed Timotheus's huge army, took Carnion, Atergatis's temple and Ephron with heavy killing, and spared Scythopolis because its Jewish residents vouched for its people. After Pentecost he fought Gorgias, whose forces fled, and at Adullam the Jews discovered that fallen comrades had worn idol tokens from Jamnia, which explained their deaths. Judas collected two thousand drachmas and sent it to Jerusalem for a sin offering, an act the text links to belief in the resurrection.

Chapter 13 body

In the 149th year, Antiochus Eupator marches against Judea with Lysias and an enormous army, joined by the hypocritical Menelaus, whom the king has put to death at Beroea by being pushed into a tower of ashes. Judas leads the people in three days of prayer and fasting, then camps at Modin and leads a night raid on the king's pavilion, killing two thousand men and felling the leading elephant. The king's attack on the fortress at Bethsura fails, and the traitor Rhodocus is caught and imprisoned. Hearing that Philip has become reckless in Antioch, the king makes peace with the Jews, honors the sanctuary, receives Maccabaeus kindly, and leaves Hegemonides as governor. The men of Ptolemais resent the treaty, but Lysias pacifies them and returns to Antioch.

Chapter 14 body

Demetrius, son of Seleucus, takes the country, and the former high priest Alcimus comes to him with gifts and accuses Judas Maccabaeus and the Hasidaeans of sedition. Demetrius sends Nicanor to Judea with orders to kill Judas, scatter his followers, and install Alcimus as high priest. Nicanor, impressed by Judas's men, makes a covenant with him and even befriends him, urging him to marry and settle, until Alcimus reports this to Demetrius, who orders Judas sent to Antioch as a prisoner. Nicanor turns harsh, Judas hides, and Nicanor threatens the priests that he will level the temple and build a temple to Dionysus unless Judas is handed over, while the priests pray for the temple's protection. Nicanor then sends soldiers to seize Razis, an elder called Father of the Jews, who tries to kill himself rather than be taken and dies after a gruesome struggle, calling on the Lord of life and spirit.

Chapter 15 body

Nicanor, learning that Judas and his men are near Samaria, plans to attack on the Sabbath and mocks the Jews who ask him to honor the day, claiming to be a sovereign on earth. Judas Maccabaeus trusts in the Lord, encourages his company with the law, the prophets and a dream in which Onias and Jeremiah appear and Jeremiah gives Judas a gold sword. Before the battle Judas prays, recalling the angel who destroyed Sennacherib's army under Hezekiah, and asks for a good angel to be sent. Judas's men fight with their hands and pray in their hearts, kill at least thirty-five thousand, and find Nicanor dead in armor. Judas has Nicanor's head, hand and arm taken to Jerusalem, displayed, and hung from the citadel, the people decree that the thirteenth of Adar be kept as a day of honor, and the author closes his book with remarks on his style.
