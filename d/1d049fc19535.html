Abstract written by a model

The Book of Amos, as presented in these nine chapters, is a prophetic text of judgment attributed to Amos, a herdsman of Tekoa, who saw his words concerning Israel in the days of Uzziah of Judah and Jeroboam son of Joash of Israel, two years before the earthquake. The LORD, who roars from Zion, is the central figure; Israel, especially its elites, and the nations around it are the main subjects. Chapters 1 and 2 open with judgments, each introduced by 'for three transgressions, yes, for four,' on Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab, and Judah. Each nation is charged with specific wrongs and threatened with fire on its palaces. The longest section targets Israel for selling the righteous and needy, trampling the poor, and silencing prophets, despite the LORD having brought them out of Egypt. Chapters 3 to 6 develop the case against Israel. Because the LORD chose Israel alone, he will punish it. Rhetorical questions argue that disaster has a cause and that the LORD reveals his plans to the prophets. Samaria's oppression, the 'cows of Bashan,' and empty worship at Bethel and Gilgal are condemned. A list of calamities is each followed by the refrain that Israel has not returned to him. The text laments Israel's fall, urges people to seek the LORD and live, calls for justice to roll on like rivers, warns that the day of the LORD will be darkness, and pronounces woe on the complacent who lounge on ivory beds while Joseph is afflicted. Captivity beyond Damascus and a nation raised against Israel are announced. Chapters 7 and 8 shift to visions. Locusts and fire are averted when Amos pleads for Jacob, but a plumb line signals that the LORD will no longer pass by Israel. Amaziah the priest of Bethel tells Amos to flee to Judah, and Amos answers that the LORD took him from herding and farming sycamore figs to prophesy, then pronounces judgment on Amaziah's family. A basket of summer fruit signals that the end has come, with cheating of the poor condemned, mourning replacing feasts, and a famine of hearing the LORD's words. Chapter 9 shows the Lord beside the altar, promising that no sinner will escape, though the house of Jacob will not be utterly destroyed. The book ends with a promise to raise David's fallen tent, restore Israel from captivity, and plant them permanently in their land amid abundant harvests.

The Book of Amos / Chapter 1 body

Chapter 1 of Amos introduces Amos, a herdsman of Tekoa, who saw his words concerning Israel in the days of Uzziah king of Judah and Jeroboam son of Joash king of Israel, two years before the earthquake. He declares that the LORD will roar from Zion and Jerusalem, causing the shepherds' pastures to mourn and Carmel's top to wither. The LORD then pronounces judgments on Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, and Ammon, each introduced by the formula 'for three transgressions, yes, for four, I will not turn away its punishment.' Each nation is charged with a specific wrong, such as threshing Gilead, delivering captives to Edom, forgetting a brotherly covenant, pursuing a brother with the sword, or ripping open pregnant women of Gilead. For each, the LORD promises fire that will devour its palaces, along with captivity or the cutting off of rulers and inhabitants.

Chapter 2 body

In Chapter 2 the LORD pronounces judgment on three nations using the formula "for three transgressions, yes, for four." Moab will be consumed by fire, its palaces of Kerioth devoured, and its judge and princes killed, because it burned the bones of the king of Edom into lime. Judah will suffer fire on the palaces of Jerusalem because it rejected the LORD's law and statutes and was led astray by lies. The longest section concerns Israel, accused of selling the righteous and needy, trampling the poor, sexual misuse of a maiden, and profaning holy places, despite the LORD destroying the Amorite, bringing them out of Egypt, and raising prophets and Nazirites whom they corrupted and silenced. The LORD declares he will crush Israel like a loaded cart, and neither the swift, strong, mighty, archer, nor horseman will escape.

Chapter 3 body

The LORD addresses the children of Israel, the whole family He brought out of Egypt, declaring that because He chose them alone He will punish them for their sins. Through a series of rhetorical questions about lions, traps, and trumpets, the passage argues that disaster comes only by cause, that evil in a city is the LORD's doing, and that He reveals His plans to His servants the prophets, who must therefore prophesy. Ashdod and Egypt are summoned to assemble on the mountains of Samaria and witness its unrest and oppression, where people hoard plunder and loot in their palaces. The LORD announces that an adversary will overrun the land, pull down strongholds and plunder fortresses, and that only a remnant will be rescued, like scraps from a lion's mouth. He also promises to visit the altars of Bethel, cutting off their horns, and to strike the winter and summer houses so that the houses of ivory and the great houses perish.

Chapter 4 body

In this chapter the Lord GOD addresses the "cows of Bashan" on the mountain of Samaria, who oppress the poor and crush the needy while telling their husbands to bring them drinks. He swears by his holiness that they will be taken away with hooks and fish hooks and go out through breaks in the wall into Harmon. He sarcastically tells the children of Israel to go to Bethel and Gilgal to sin, and to bring sacrifices and free will offerings that please them. He then lists calamities he sent, including lack of bread, withheld rain, blight, mildew, locusts, plagues, sword, and overthrow like Sodom and Gomorrah, each followed by the refrain that Israel has not returned to him. The chapter closes with a call for Israel to prepare to meet its God, the LORD, the God of Armies, who forms the mountains and creates the wind.

Chapter 5 body

Chapter 5 opens with a lamentation over the house of Israel, declaring that the virgin of Israel has fallen and that its cities will be left with only a tenth of their men. The LORD urges Israel to seek him and live rather than seek Bethel, Gilgal, or Beersheba, and he is presented as the creator who brings sudden destruction on the strong. He condemns those who pervert justice, trample the poor, take bribes, and hate the one who reproves in the gate, promising that their stone houses and vineyards will not benefit them. He warns that the day of the LORD will be darkness, not light, rejects Israel's feasts, offerings, and songs, and calls for justice to roll on like rivers. The chapter ends with the LORD announcing captivity beyond Damascus.

Chapter 6 body

Chapter 6 pronounces woe on the complacent notable men of Zion and Samaria, who feast, lounge on ivory beds, play music, and drink wine and anoint themselves with oils while not grieving for the affliction of Joseph. They are told to compare themselves with Calneh, Hamath, and Gath, and are accused of putting off the evil day and turning justice into poison. The LORD swears by himself that he abhors the pride of Jacob and will deliver up the city with all in it, so that they will go captive first and the feasting will end. Death will fill the houses, great and small, and survivors will not dare mention the LORD's name. The LORD announces he will raise up a nation against the house of Israel to afflict them from the entrance of Hamath to the brook of the Arabah.

Chapter 7 body

In Amos 7, the Lord GOD shows Amos three visions: locusts devouring the latter growth after the king's harvest, a judgment by fire that dries up the great deep, and the Lord standing by a wall with a plumb line. Amos pleads for Jacob after the first two visions and the LORD relents, but the plumb line signals that the LORD will no longer pass by Israel, with the high places of Isaac and the house of Jeroboam to fall by the sword. Amaziah the priest of Bethel reports to King Jeroboam that Amos is conspiring and tells Amos to flee to Judah and stop prophesying at the king's sanctuary. Amos answers that he was a herdsman and farmer of sycamore figs whom the LORD took to prophesy to Israel, and he pronounces judgment on Amaziah's family and land and repeats that Israel will be led away captive.

Chapter 8 body

In Amos 8, the Lord GOD shows Amos a basket of summer fruit and declares that the end has come on his people Israel and he will no longer pass them by. He condemns those who swallow up the needy and poor, who wait for the new moon and Sabbath to pass so they can sell grain, cheating with a small ephah, a large shekel, and deceitful balances, and who buy the poor for silver and sell the sweepings with the wheat. The LORD swears by the pride of Jacob never to forget their works, and announces that the land will tremble, the sun will go down at noon, and feasts and songs will turn into mourning like that for an only son. He also promises a famine not of bread or water but of hearing the LORD's words, in which people wander from sea to sea without finding it, and the young faint from thirst. Those who swear by the sin of Samaria and by the gods of Dan and Beersheba will fall and never rise again.

Chapter 9 body

In Chapter 9 the speaker sees the Lord standing beside the altar commanding the striking of the pillars and promising that no one in the sinful kingdom will escape, whether they flee to Sheol, heaven, Carmel, the sea, or captivity. The Lord, described as the GOD of Armies who touches the land and builds his rooms in the heavens, says Israel is like the Ethiopians to him, yet he brought Israel from Egypt, the Philistines from Caphtor, and the Syrians from Kir. He will destroy the sinful kingdom but not utterly the house of Jacob, sifting Israel among the nations without losing a kernel, while the sinners of his people who deny that evil will reach them die by the sword. The chapter ends with the Lord promising to raise up David's fallen tent, bring Israel back from captivity, and plant them permanently in their land amid abundant harvests.
