Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin)

Mencius · about 399 to 200 BC · 48 sections
Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), translated by James Legge (1861/1895), public domain, via Wikisource. Shown here as plain text, with facts drawn from it. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Chinese_Classics

Abstract written by a model

This document is a collection of teachings attributed to Mencius, arranged in two parts, concerning self-cultivation, Heaven’s appointment, moral character, teaching, and benevolent government. Part I begins with the individual: people should know and preserve their Heaven-given nature, accept what is properly appointed, seek inward goods through effort, examine themselves for sincerity, and cultivate shame as a means of correction. Mencius contrasts habitual, unreflective living with the deliberate pursuit of virtue and righteousness. He describes the superior person and distinguished scholar as self-motivated, principled in poverty or prosperity, unimpressed by power or office, and devoted to rectifying themselves. Important examples include Shun, whose eager response to goodness distinguishes him even in remote surroundings; Yao and Shun, for whom benevolence and righteousness were natural; Tang and Wu, who made those principles their own; Hui of Liu Xia, who would not trade purpose for office; and Confucius, whose perspective and progressive learning exemplify the sage’s path. Mencius repeatedly connects moral cultivation to public life. Good rulers secure the people’s ease, prosperity, and virtue through instruction, light burdens, cultivated fields, care for the old, and abundant food. True sovereigns influence people deeply and almost imperceptibly, unlike mere chiefs. Good instruction gains hearts, whereas government gains wealth and fear. He presents king Wen’s care for the old and families as a model, and condemns unjust action, rigid principle detached from circumstances, and pursuit of gain over virtue. Dialogue chapters apply these standards to political and personal cases: Yi Yin may banish Tai Jia only if sharing his proper purpose; Shun would allow Gao Yao to arrest Gu Sou but would abandon the kingdom to care for his father; and scholars deserve genuine honor rather than empty gifts. Mencius defines teaching as varied influence, talent-development, answering inquiry, and prompting self-cultivation, while refusing to lower standards for incapable learners. Part II continues these concerns in summary form, treating benevolent rule, unjust war, the relations among people, sovereigns, government, sages, natural desire, Heaven’s appointment, and Confucius as a model and transmitter of earlier doctrines.

Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin) front matter

Part I / Chapter 1 body

Mencius teaches that a person who exhausts their mental constitution comes to know their nature. By knowing their nature, they come to know Heaven. Preserving one's mental constitution and nourishing one's nature is the way to serve Heaven. A person establishes their Heaven-ordained being by cultivating personal character without being distracted by premature death or long life.

Chapter 2 body

Mencius teaches that everything has an appointment and that a man should submissively accept what may be correctly ascribed to it. He says that understanding Heaven's appointment means not standing beneath a precipitous wall. Death sustained while discharging one's duties may be ascribed to Heaven's appointment, but death under handcuffs and fetters may not.

Chapter 3 body

Mencius distinguishes between two kinds of seeking and getting. When people gain through seeking and lose through neglect, seeking is useful for getting, and the things sought are within themselves. When seeking follows the proper course but getting depends on what is appointed, seeking does not cause getting, and the things sought are outside themselves.

Chapter 4 body

Mencius teaches that all things are already complete in us. He says the greatest delight comes from being conscious of sincerity through self-examination. He adds that vigorous effort at the law of reciprocity, when directed toward perfect virtue, brings one closest to its realization.

Chapter 5 body

Mencius describes a way of living without understanding or examination. He says that pursuing the proper path throughout life without knowing its nature involves acting habitually without reflection. He identifies this as the way of multitudes.

Chapter 6 body

Mencius says that a man may not be without shame. He teaches that recognizing and feeling ashamed of having lacked shame can prevent future occasions for shame. The unit concerns the relationship between a man, shame, and self-correction.

Chapter 7 body

Mencius says that the sense of shame is very important to a man. He observes that people who devise artful contrivances and versatile schemes do not let their sense of shame act. He asks what a person without this sense of shame has in common with other men.

Chapter 8 body

Mencius says that the able and virtuous monarchs of antiquity loved virtue and forgot their power. He asks whether the able and virtuous scholars of antiquity should be treated as an exception, and says they delighted in their own principles and were oblivious of the power of princes. Kings and dukes could not visit such scholars frequently unless they showed the utmost respect and observed all forms of ceremony, and therefore could not easily employ them as ministers.

Chapter 9 body

Mencius advises Song Gou Jian about how to remain perfectly satisfied while travelling to different courts. He says one should be satisfied whether a prince accepts or rejects one's counsels by honoring virtue and delighting in righteousness. Mencius explains that a scholar preserves righteousness in poverty and stays on the proper path in prosperity. He also describes how the men of antiquity benefited the people when successful and cultivated personal character when unsuccessful.

Chapter 10 body

Mencius says that the mass of men wait for a king Wen before receiving a rousing impulse. Scholars distinguished from the mass rouse themselves without a king Wan. The unit contrasts the dependence of the mass of men with the self-motivation of distinguished scholars.

Chapter 11 body

Mencius says that if a man is given the families of Han and Wei and does not become elated when he looks upon himself, he is far beyond the mass of men.

Chapter 12 body

Mencius says that the people should be employed in ways intended to secure their ease. He says that even if the people are toiled, they will not murmur when their labor is intended to secure their ease. He also says that the people should be put to death in ways intended to preserve their lives. Even when they die, they will not murmur at him who puts them to death.

Chapter 13 body

Mencius contrasts the effects of a chief with those of a true sovereign on the people. Under a chief, the people appear brisk and cheerful, while under a true sovereign they show deep contentment and do not murmur even when he slays them. The superior man transforms society through a spiritual influence that spreads like Heaven and Earth, advancing people toward goodness without their knowing who causes it.

Chapter 14 body

Mencius compares kindly words, reputation for kindness, good government, and good instructions. He says reputation for kindness affects men more deeply than kindly words. Good instructions are loved by the people and gain their hearts, while good government is feared and gains their wealth.

Chapter 15 body

Mencius explains that intuitive ability is the ability people possess without learning, while intuitive knowledge is knowledge they possess without thought. He says children instinctively love their parents and, as they grow, love their elder brothers. Filial affection for parents is the working of benevolence, and respect for elders is the working of righteousness. These feelings belong to all under heaven.

Chapter 16 body

Mencius describes Shun living in deep, remote mountains among trees, rocks, deer, swine, and rude inhabitants. In that setting, Shun appears only slightly different from the local people. When Shun hears a single good word or sees a single good action, he responds with irresistible enthusiasm, like a stream or river bursting its banks.

Chapter 17 body

Mencius says that a man should refrain from doing anything his own sense of righteousness tells him not to do. He should also avoid desiring anything that his sense of righteousness tells him not to desire. Mencius teaches that acting according to these restraints is all the man has to do.

Chapter 18 body

Mencius says that men with intelligent virtue and prudence in affairs are generally shaped by sickness and troubles. He identifies the friendless minister and the concubine's son as people who keep their hearts under a sense of peril and take deep precautions against calamity. Mencius says that this experience makes them distinguished for their intelligence.

Chapter 19 body

Mencius distinguishes several kinds of people according to how they serve rulers and society. Persons who serve the prince do so for his countenance and favour, while ministers who seek the tranquillity of the State take pleasure in securing it. The people of Heaven believe they could carry out their principles throughout the kingdom if they held office, and therefore proceed to do so. Great men rectify themselves, and others are thereby rectified.

Chapter 20 body

Mencius describes the three things in which the superior man delights. The first is having both parents alive and brothers free from trouble. The second is having no shame before Heaven or people. The third is finding, teaching, and nourishing the most talented individuals in the kingdom. Being ruler over the kingdom is not among these delights.

Chapter 21 body

Mencius explains what the superior man truly values. Although the superior man may desire broad territory, a numerous people, and the tranquillization of the kingdom, these are not his highest enjoyment. His nature is determinately apportioned by Heaven and is neither increased by broad action nor diminished by poverty and retirement. Benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and knowledge are rooted in his heart and manifest outwardly in his countenance, back, and limbs.

Chapter 22 body

Mencius describes Bo Yi and Tai Gong seeking king Wen because they heard that the chief of the West knew how to nourish the old. He explains that a prince who nourished the old would attract all men of virtue, and that this required regulating fields and dwellings, planting mulberry trees, raising animals, and instructing families. Under king Wen, the people had silk and flesh for the old, while farms secured families against want, so no aged people were starved or famished.

Chapter 23 body

Mencius explains how a sage should govern the kingdom to make the people prosperous and virtuous. He recommends well-cultivated fields, light taxes, seasonal use of food resources, and spending wealth only on prescribed ceremonies. He compares abundant grain and pulse to water and fire, which people readily share. He concludes that when grain and pulse are abundant, the people will be virtuous.

Chapter 24 body

Mencius explains that Confucius’s ascent of the eastern hill and the Tai mountain changed how large Lu and the world beneath heaven appeared to him. He compares this expanded perspective with the sea and the gate of the sage, after which other waters and other people’s words seem less significant. Mencius also teaches that water should be observed in waves and that flowing water advances only after filling the hollows in its course. The student who follows the doctrines of the sage progresses by completing one lesson after another.

Chapter 25 body

Mencius distinguishes between two ways of beginning the day. A person who rises at cock-crowing and earnestly practices virtue is described as a disciple of Shun. A person who rises at cock-crowing and earnestly pursues gain is described as a disciple of Zhi. Mencius says that the interval between the thought of gain and the thought of virtue separates Shun from Zhi.

Chapter 26 body

Mencius compares the principles of Yang, Mo, and Zi Mo. Yang holds that each person should act for himself, while Mo loves all equally and would make any bodily sacrifice to benefit the kingdom. Zi Mo holds a medium between them, but Mencius says that refusing to adapt it to circumstances makes it like rigidly holding one point. Mencius rejects such rigidity because it harms right principle by ignoring many other considerations.

Chapter 27 body

Mencius explains that hunger and thirst can make people mistake any food or drink for having the right taste. He says that hunger and thirst injure the palate and also injure men's minds. A person who prevents these conditions from harming the mind need not be troubled about not being equal to other men.

Chapter 28 body

Mencius says that Hui of Liu Xia would not have changed his firm purpose of life even for the three highest offices of State. The unit concerns Hui of Liu Xia’s steadfastness and his refusal to sacrifice his principles for political position.

Chapter 29 body

Mencius compares a man with definite aims to be accomplished to someone digging a well. He says that digging to a depth of seventy-two cubits and stopping before reaching the spring amounts to throwing away the well. The unit concerns persistence in pursuing definite aims.

Chapter 30 body

Mencius says that benevolence and righteousness were natural to Yao and Shun. Tang and Wu made benevolence and righteousness their own. The five chiefs of the princes feigned them and, having borrowed them for a long time without returning them, made it difficult to know that they did not own them.

Chapter 31 body

Gong Sun Chou asks Mencius about Yi Yin’s banishment of Tai Jia to Tong when Tai Jia was disobedient to reason. The people were pleased when Tai Jia was banished and again pleased when Yi Yin brought him back after Tai Jia became virtuous. Gong Sun Chou asks whether worthy ministers may banish sovereigns in this way when they are not virtuous. Mencius replies that they may do so only if they share Yi Yin’s purpose; otherwise, it would be usurpation.

Chapter 32 body

Gong Sun Chou asks Mencius why superior men are seen eating without labouring, citing the Book of Poetry’s warning against eating the bread of idleness. Mencius explains that when a superior man lives in a country and its sovereign follows his counsels, the country gains tranquillity, wealth, and glory. He adds that when the country’s young follow the superior man’s instructions, they become filial, obedient to their elders, true-hearted, and faithful.

Chapter 33 body

Dian asks Mencius about the business of the unemployed scholar. Mencius says that the unemployed scholar should exalt his aim by setting it simply on benevolence and righteousness. He explains that killing an innocent person is contrary to benevolence and taking what one has no right to is contrary to righteousness. He concludes that when benevolence is the heart’s dwelling and righteousness the path of life, the business of a great man is complete.

Chapter 34 body

Mencius argues that Chen Zhong would refuse the kingdom of Qi if it were offered contrary to righteousness. People regard Chen Zhong as a man of the highest worth because he would decline a dish of rice or a plate of soup. Mencius says that disowning parents and relatives, or rejecting the relations of sovereign and minister and of superiors and inferiors, are greater crimes. He concludes that a man should not receive credit for great excellences merely because he possesses a small one.

Chapter 35 body

Tao Ying asks Mencius what would happen if Gu Sou had murdered a man while Shun was sovereign and Gao Yao was chief minister of justice. Mencius says Gao Yao would apprehend Gu Sou because he had received the law from a proper source. Shun would not forbid the apprehension, but would abandon the kingdom, carry Gu Sou into concealment by the sea-coast, and live there cheerfully for the rest of his life.

Chapter 36 body

Mencius, traveling from Fan to Qi, saw the king of Qi's son and reflected on how position alters a person's air and appearance. He said that the residence, carriages and horses, and dress of the king's son were mostly like those of other men, but his position made him look different. Mencius taught that position has an even greater influence in the wide house of the world. He also recounted that the prince of Lu's voice was recognized as resembling their prince's because their positions corresponded.

Chapter 37 body

Mencius says that feeding a scholar without loving him treats him like a pig, while loving him without respecting him keeps him like a domestic animal. He teaches that honouring and respecting come before offering gifts. A superior man cannot be retained by demonstrations of honour and respect that lack genuine reality.

Chapter 38 body

Mencius explains that bodily organs and their functions belong to Heaven-conferred nature. He says that a man must be a sage to satisfy the design of his bodily organization. The unit concerns the relationship between bodily organization, nature, and sagehood.

Chapter 39 body

King Xuan of Qi wanted to shorten the period of mourning. Gong Sun Chou argued that observing mourning for one whole year was better than eliminating it altogether. Mencius said that the proper response was to teach filial piety and fraternal duty, not merely suggest moderation. When the king's son could observe only a few months because of an impossibility, Mencius said that even one additional day of mourning was better than none.

Chapter 40 body

Mencius identifies five ways in which the superior man effects his teaching. His influence may descend like seasonable rain, perfect virtue, develop talents, answer inquiries, or encourage private self-cultivation and correction. These five methods describe how the superior man teaches different people.

Chapter 41 body

Gong Sun Chou says that Mencius's principles are lofty and admirable but difficult to attain. He asks why Mencius does not adapt his teaching to make the principles seem attainable and encourage learners to exert themselves daily. Mencius replies that a great artificer does not alter the marking-line for a stupid workman, nor did Yi change his rule for drawing the bow for a stupid archer. He says the superior man draws the bow without discharging the arrow, appears to leap with it to the mark, and stands exactly in the middle of the path for those who are able to follow him.

Chapter 42 body

Mencius explains how a person's principles relate to the state of the kingdom. When right principles prevail throughout the kingdom, a person's principles appear together with the person. When right principles disappear, the person must vanish along with those principles. Mencius says he has not heard that one's principles depend on other men for their manifestation.

Chapter 43 body

Gong Du asks Mencius why he did not answer Geng of Tang despite the propriety of showing consideration to him when he appeared in Mencius's school. Mencius explains that he does not answer people who presume on their nobility, talents, age, services performed for him, or old acquaintance. He says that two of these presumptions were chargeable on Geng of Tang.

Chapter 44 body

Mencius teaches that stopping where one should not stop leads to stopping in everything. He says that behaving shabbily toward people who deserve good treatment leads to shabby behavior toward everyone. He also observes that advancing too quickly is followed by a rapid retreat.

Chapter 45 body

Mencius distinguishes the superior man’s different attitudes toward inferior creatures, people generally, and his parents. The superior man is kind to inferior creatures but does not love them. He loves people generally but is not affectionate toward them, while he is affectionate to his parents. He is lovingly disposed to people generally and kind to creatures.

Chapter 46 body

Mencius explains that the wise and the benevolent should embrace broadly while concentrating most earnestly on what is of the greatest importance. He says Yao and Shun did not know or attend to everything, but focused on what mattered most. He contrasts proper mourning and eating practices to show that people may attend to minor rules while neglecting what is most important.

Part II / Chapter 1 .. Chapter 38 body

Mencius discusses benevolent rule, the dangers of unjust war, and the importance of governing through virtue, righteousness, and proper principles. He explains how the people, the sovereign, government, and the doctrines of the sages relate to one another. He also distinguishes natural human desires and feelings from the appointment of Heaven, and describes the superior man, scholars, and sages. Confucius is presented as an example of proper conduct and as a transmitter of earlier doctrines.
