Abstract written by a model

This document is Book XVI of The Analects, presenting Confucius’s judgments on government, conduct, learning, friendship, and propriety. It begins with the head of the Chi family preparing to attack Chwan-yu. Zan Yu and Chi-lu report this to Confucius, who reproaches Zan Yu for aiding the plan, defends Chwan-yu’s rightful place in the State, and argues that rulers should pursue harmony, contentment, and proper order rather than aggression or fear of scarcity. He warns that the Chi family’s own divisions may bring grief within its court. Confucius then describes political decline: when good government prevails, authority over ceremonies, music, and military punishments belongs to the son of Heaven; when it does not, authority falls through the princes to lower officials. He observes that revenue has left the ducal House and government has passed to the Great officers, diminishing the descendants of the three Hwan. The remaining teachings define beneficial and harmful friendships and enjoyments; explain errors in speaking before a superior; and set out the dangers the superior man must guard against in youth, maturity, and old age. Confucius contrasts the superior man, who reveres Heaven, great men, and sages, with the mean man, who does not. He ranks people by knowledge and willingness to learn, and lists nine careful considerations for the superior man, including clarity, kindness, sincerity, reverence, reflection, and righteousness over gain. He praises those who pursue good and avoid evil, while noting he has heard but not seen people who retire to study their aims and practice righteousness. A contrast between duke Ching of Ch'i, whose wealth earned no remembered virtue, and Po-i and Shu-ch'i, praised despite dying of hunger, emphasizes moral reputation. Ch'an K'ang learns from Po-yu that Po-yu’s father taught the Odes and Propriety, while maintaining reserve toward his son. The book ends by explaining titles for a prince’s wife.

The Analects, Book XVI: Ke She / CHAP. I. 1. The head of the Chi family was going to attack body

The head of the Chi family plans to attack Chwan-yu, and Zan Yu and Chi-lu report this to Confucius. Confucius says Zan Yu is at fault for assisting the plan and argues that Chwan-yu has a legitimate place within the State. He teaches that rulers should seek harmony, contented repose, and the proper ordering of their people rather than fear scarcity. Confucius concludes that the Chi family’s internal divisions and hostile movements may bring sorrow within its own court.

CHAP. II. 1. Confucius said, 'When good government prevails body

Confucius explains how authority over ceremonies, music, and punitive military expeditions reflects the quality of government in the empire. Under good government, these powers proceed from the son of Heaven; under bad government, they proceed from the princes and increasingly lower officials. He states that when right principles prevail in the kingdom, government will not be in the hands of the Great officers and the common people will not engage in discussions.

CHAP. III. Confucius said, 'The revenue of the state has left body

Confucius says that the revenue of the state has left the ducal House for five generations. He says that the government has been in the hands of the Great officers for four generations. As a result, the descendants of the three Hwan are much reduced.

CHAP. IV. Confucius said, 'There are three friendships which body

Confucius distinguishes between three advantageous friendships and three injurious friendships. He identifies friendship with the upright, the sincere, and the man of much observation as advantageous. He identifies friendship with the man of specious airs, the insinuatingly soft, and the glib-tongued as injurious.

CHAP. V. Confucius said, 'There are three things men find body

Confucius explains that men find enjoyment in three things that are advantageous and three things that are injurious. Advantageous enjoyments include studying ceremonies and music, speaking of others' goodness, and having many worthy friends. Injurious enjoyments include extravagant pleasures, idleness and sauntering, and the pleasures of feasting.

CHAP. VI. Confucius said, 'There are three errors to which body

Confucius identifies three errors that people may make when they stand in the presence of a man of virtue and station. They may speak when it is not their turn, which is called rashness. They may remain silent when they should speak, which is called concealment. They may speak without looking at the countenance of their superior, which is called blindness.

CHAP. VII. Confucius said, 'There are three things which the body

Confucius teaches that the superior man guards against different faults at different stages of life. In youth, when physical powers are unsettled, he guards against lust. In strength and vigor, he guards against quarrelsomeness. In old age, when animal powers have decayed, he guards against covetousness.

CHAP. VIII. 1. Confucius said, 'There are three things of which body

Confucius teaches that the superior man stands in awe of the ordinances of Heaven, great men, and the words of sages. The mean man does not know the ordinances of Heaven and therefore does not stand in awe of them. He is disrespectful to great men and makes sport of the words of sages.

CHAP. IX. Confucius said, 'Those who are born with the body

Confucius describes four classes of people according to their possession of knowledge and their approach to learning. Those born with knowledge are the highest class. Those who readily gain knowledge through learning are next, followed by those who are dull and stupid but still achieve learning. Those who are dull and stupid and do not learn are the lowest of the people.

CHAP. X. Confucius said, 'The superior man has nine things body

Confucius teaches that the superior man carefully considers nine aspects of conduct. He seeks clarity in seeing and hearing, and kindness and respect in his countenance and demeanor. He aims for sincerity in speech and reverent care in business, questions others about doubts, reflects on the difficulties anger may cause, and considers righteousness when gain is available.

CHAP. XI. 1. Confucius said, 'Contemplating good, and pursuing body

Confucius describes people who contemplate good and pursue it as if they cannot reach it, while contemplating evil and shrinking from it as from boiling water. He says he has seen such men and heard such words. He has heard of living in retirement to study aims and practicing righteousness to carry out principles, but he has not seen such men.

CHAP. XII. 1. The duke Ching of Ch'i had a thousand teams, body

The duke Ching of Ch'i had a thousand teams, each consisting of four horses. On the day of his death, the people did not praise him for a single virtue. Po-i and Shu-ch'i died of hunger at the foot of the Shau-yang mountain, and the people still praise them. The saying is illustrated by this contrast.

CHAP. XIII. 1. Ch'an K'ang asked Po-yu, saying, 'Have you body

Ch'an K'ang asks Po-yu whether his father taught him lessons different from those known by others. Po-yu recounts that his father instructed him to study the Odes so that he could converse properly and the rules of Propriety so that his character could be established. Ch'an K'ang concludes that he has learned about the Odes, the rules of Propriety, and the superior man's distant reserve toward his son.

CHAP. XIV. The wife of the prince of a state is called by him body

The unit explains the titles used for the wife of the prince of a state. The prince calls her FU ZAN, while she calls herself HSIAO T'UNG. The people of the State call her CHUN FU ZAN, and both she and the people of other States use K'WA HSIAO CHUN when referring to her in relation to other States.
