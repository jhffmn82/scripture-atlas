Abstract written by a model

This is Book X of The Analects of Confucius, a sequence of observations about Confucius’s deportment, ritual care, dress, food, and conduct in public and private life. It opens by contrasting his simple, seemingly inarticulate manner in his village with his cautious, detailed speech in the ancestral temple and court. At court and before the prince, he observes precise distinctions in speech, movement, posture, and ceremonial behavior; he approaches official duties, visitors, palace spaces, the ruler’s scepter, and audiences with visible respectful uneasiness. The book then details the superior man’s clothing, fasting arrangements, dietary rules, sacrificial practices, and insistence on a straight mat. It shows him participating appropriately in village drinking and pestilence ceremonies, sending inquiries through messengers, and refusing unknown medicine. His priorities appear when, after a stable burns, he asks whether any person was hurt rather than about the horses. Further scenes describe his responses to princely gifts, his questioning in the ancestral temple, care for friends lacking relatives to bury them, and special reverence for sacrificial flesh. The later chapters cover his unceremonious conduct at home, his formal responses to mourning, full dress, blind people, population tables, weather, and carriage etiquette. It ends with a hill-bridge scene in which the Master remarks on a hen-pheasant appearing in its proper season, while Tsze-lu motions toward it.

The Analects of Confucius, Book X (Heang Tang) / CHAP. I. 1. Confucius, in his village, looked simple and sincere, body

This unit concerns Confucius and contrasts his manner in different settings. In his village, he appeared simple and sincere, as if unable to speak. In the prince's ancestral temple and in court, he spoke minutely on every point but did so cautiously.

CHAP II. 1. When he was waiting at court, in speaking with body

The unit concerns a man's conduct while waiting at court. He speaks freely but straightforwardly with the great officers of the lower grade, and blandly but precisely with those of the higher grade. When the ruler is present, his manner shows respectful uneasiness while remaining grave and self-possessed.

CHAP. III. 1. When the prince called him to employ him in the body

The unit concerns an officer called by the prince to receive a visitor. The officer’s countenance changes, his legs move with difficulty, and he adjusts his movements and robe while approaching the other officers. He then hastens forward with his arms like a bird’s wings. After the visitor retires, the officer reports to the prince that the visitor is not turning round any more.

CHAP. IV. 1. When he entered the palace gate, he seemed to body

The unit concerns a person entering a palace and appearing highly respectful and uneasy in the presence of the prince. At the palace gate, the person bends his body, avoids the middle of the gate-way and threshold, and shows physical signs of anxiety when passing the prince’s vacant place. During the audience, he holds up his robe, bends his body, and restrains his breath. After leaving the audience, he relaxes somewhat but continues to show respectful uneasiness.

CHAP. V. 1. When he was carrying the scepter of his ruler, he body

A ruler’s attendant carries the scepter of his ruler and appears weighed down by it. He holds it between the positions used when bowing and giving something, while his countenance changes and he drags his feet. When presenting presents, he looks placid, and at a private audience he looks highly pleased.

CHAP. VI. 1. The superior man did not use a deep purple, or a body

The unit describes the superior man's clothing, colors, garments, and dressing practices. It states which colors and furs he wore or avoided in different circumstances. It also explains the forms and lengths of his garments, his sleeping dress, and his clothing for mourning, condolence visits, and court appearances.

CHAP. VII. 1. When fasting, he thought it necessary to have body

The unit concerns he and his practices when fasting. He considered it necessary to have his clothes brightly clean and made of linen cloth. He also considered it necessary to change his food and the place where he commonly sat in the apartment.

CHAP. VIII. 1. He did not dislike to have his rice finely body

The unit concerns a person's rules and habits regarding food, eating, and sacrifice. He preferred finely cleaned rice and properly prepared food, while avoiding spoiled, discoloured, ill-cooked, or out-of-season items. He limited his intake of meat and food, set no limit on wine but did not let it confuse him, and always ate ginger. He observed specific practices concerning the flesh received from the prince's sacrifice and the family sacrifice, and offered even coarse food respectfully in sacrifice.

CHAP. IX. If his mat was not straight, he did not sit on it. body

The unit concerns a mat and a person who uses it. The person refuses to sit on the mat if it is not straight. No other events or details are given.

CHAP. X. 1. When the villagers were drinking together, on body

This unit concerns the villagers and the conduct of a man during their communal activities. When the villagers were drinking together, he followed immediately after those who carried staffs went out. When the villagers were performing ceremonies to drive away pestilential influences, he put on his court robes and stood on the eastern steps.

CHAP. XI. 1. When he was sending complimentary inquiries to body

The passage describes how he behaved when sending complimentary inquiries to someone in another State. He bowed twice while escorting the messenger away. When Chi K'ang sent him a present of physic, he bowed and received it but said he did not know it and dared not taste it.

CHAP. XII. The stable being burned down, when he was at body

The stable was being burned down while he was at court. When he returned, he asked whether any man had been hurt. He did not ask about the horses.

CHAP. XIII. 1. When the prince sent him a gift of cooked meat, body

The unit concerns the unnamed attendant’s conduct when the prince sends him different gifts. He adjusts his mat, tastes cooked meat before giving it away, cooks undressed meat and offers it to his ancestors’ spirits, and keeps a living animal alive. When attending the prince at an entertainment, he tastes everything after the prince sacrifices. When ill, he receives the prince with prescribed clothing arrangements, and when summoned, he goes immediately without waiting for his carriage.

CHAP. XIV. When he entered the ancestral temple of the body

In Chapter XIV, he enters the ancestral temple of the State. While there, he asks about everything.

CHAP. XV. 1. When any of his friends died, if he had no body

The unit concerns a man and his conduct toward friends, deceased friends, and presents. When a friend died without dependable relations to perform the necessary offices, he offered to bury him. He did not bow when a friend sent him a present, even if it was a carriage and horses, but he did bow for the flesh of sacrifice.

CHAP. XVI. 1. In bed, he did not lie like a corpse. At home, he body

The unit concerns a man’s conduct in bed, at home, in public, and at entertainments. He does not lie like a corpse in bed or put on formal deportment at home. He changes countenance or salutes ceremoniously in the presence of people in mourning, people in full dress, blind people, population tables, abundant provisions, thunder, or violent wind. He bows forward to the crossbar of his carriage when greeting a person in mourning or someone bearing population tables.

CHAP. XVII. 1. When he was about to mount his carriage, he body

A man is described as he prepares to mount and ride in his carriage. Before mounting, he stands straight and holds the cord; while riding, he does not turn his head fully, speak hastily, or point with his hands.

CHAP. XVIII. 1. Seeing the countenance, it instantly rises. It body

A creature seeing the countenance instantly rises, flies round, and later settles. The Master points out a hen-pheasant on a hill bridge and remarks that it is present at its proper season. Tsze-lu makes a motion toward the hen-pheasant, which smells him three times before rising.
