Abstract written by a model

This document is the book of Ecclesiastes, twelve chapters of reflection by the Preacher, son of David and king in Jerusalem, on what a person gains from life under the sun. Its central figures are the Preacher and God, who gives, judges and sets the limits of human understanding. Its recurring refrain is that all is vanity, a chasing after wind. Chapter 1 opens with the Preacher's declaration of vanity, the endless cycles of nature, and the claim that nothing is new. He finds that great wisdom brings grief. In chapter 2 he tests pleasure and great building works, finds them empty, and sees that the wise and the fool share one fate. He despairs of leaving his labor to a successor, yet says that enjoying one's labor is from God's hand. Chapter 3 gives the catalogue of times for every activity, says God set eternity in human hearts, and recommends rejoicing and doing good. It notes that people share the fate of animals. Chapters 4 to 6 look at oppression, envy, solitary toil, the strength of companions, and the fleeting fame of rulers. They counsel few words before God and prompt payment of vows, and they warn that wealth never satisfies and may go unenjoyed, leaving a stillborn child arguably better off. Chapters 7 to 10 gather proverbs that favor wisdom over folly, patience over anger, and mourning over feasting. They advise against being overly righteous or wicked, and note that no one is free of sin. The Preacher says he found wisdom out of reach and God's work hidden, and that God made mankind upright but people seek many inventions. They urge obedience to the king, observe that justice is delayed and the righteous and wicked sometimes receive each other's due, and affirm that the fear of God will be rewarded. Death comes alike to all, the living have hope, and the dead know nothing. Time and chance happen to everyone, wisdom is better than strength, and a little folly spoils much. Chapter 11 urges generosity, diligent sowing despite uncertainty, and rejoicing in youth with judgment in mind. Chapter 12 ends by urging the reader to remember the Creator in youth before old age and death, pictured in vivid imagery, when the spirit returns to God. It repeats that all is vanity, praises the Preacher's teaching, and concludes that the whole duty of man is to fear God and keep his commandments, since God will judge every work.

Ecclesiastes / Chapter 1 body

Chapter 1 of Ecclesiastes introduces the Preacher, son of David and king in Jerusalem, who declares that all is vanity. He asks what man gains from his labor under the sun and observes that generations, the sun, the wind, and the rivers move in endless cycles while the earth remains. He states that there is nothing new under the sun and that nothing is remembered of former or later things. The Preacher then recounts applying his heart to seek out all that is done under the sky, finding it a heavy burden and a chasing after wind. He concludes that though he gained great wisdom, knowing wisdom, madness and folly is also a chasing after wind, since much wisdom brings much grief and increased knowledge increases sorrow.

Chapter 2 body

In Chapter 2 the narrator, a king in Jerusalem, tests mirth and pleasure, then builds great works: houses, vineyards, gardens, pools, servants, herds, silver, gold, and singers. He allows himself every joy, yet concludes that all of it is vanity and a chasing after wind with no profit under the sun. He compares wisdom and folly, finding wisdom better as light is better than darkness, but sees that the wise man and the fool share one fate and are both forgotten. He comes to hate his labor because he must leave it to a successor who may be wise or foolish, and he despairs. He ends by saying that eating, drinking, and enjoying one's labor is from the hand of God, who gives wisdom, knowledge, and joy to those who please him and toil to the sinner.

Chapter 3 body

Chapter 3 opens with a catalogue of opposite activities, each assigned its own time under heaven, such as birth and death, weeping and laughing, war and peace. The narrator asks what profit a worker gains from labor and reflects that God has given people a burden, made everything beautiful in its time, and set eternity in human hearts without letting them grasp God's whole work. He concludes that the best course is to rejoice, do good, and enjoy food, drink, and labor as God's gift, since God's work is permanent and meant to inspire fear. Observing wickedness in places of justice, he says God will judge the righteous and the wicked, and that God tests people to show they are like animals, sharing one breath, one fate in death, and a return to dust. He closes by saying nothing is better than rejoicing in one's works, because no one can see what will come after.

Chapter 4 body

The speaker observes the oppressions done under the sun, noting the tears of the oppressed who have no comforter while their oppressors hold power, and concludes that the dead, or those never born, are better off than the living. He sees that labor and achievement spring from a neighbor's envy, which is vanity, and contrasts the fool who ruins himself with the value of a quiet handful over two handfuls won through toil. He describes a solitary man with no son or brother whose endless labor never satisfies him, and then praises two companions who reward each other's work, lift each other up, keep warm together, and withstand an attacker, since a threefold cord is not quickly broken. Finally, he says a poor and wise youth is better than an old and foolish king who will not take admonition, even though the youth rose from prison to kingship, and those who come after will not rejoice in him, so this too is vanity.

Chapter 5 body

Chapter 5 begins by advising caution when approaching God's house: listening is better than the sacrifice of fools, words before God should be few, and vows must be paid promptly because God has no pleasure in fools. It then observes that oppression of the poor in a district should not cause surprise, since officials are watched by higher officials, and that the king profits from the field. The speaker turns to wealth, saying that whoever loves silver is never satisfied, that goods increase those who consume them, and that the laboring man sleeps sweetly while the rich man's abundance keeps him awake. He describes as a grievous evil wealth kept to its owner's harm, lost by misfortune, leaving nothing for a son, since the owner leaves as naked as he came and eats in darkness, frustration, sickness and wrath. He concludes that it is good to eat, drink and enjoy one's labor during the life God gives, and that riches with the power to enjoy them are a gift of God who occupies a person with joy of heart.

Chapter 6 body

Chapter 6 describes an evil the speaker has seen under the sun: a man given riches, wealth, and honor by God who lacks nothing he desires, yet is not given power to enjoy them, so a stranger eats them instead. The speaker then considers a man who fathers a hundred children and lives many years but is not filled with good and has no burial, declaring a stillborn child better off, since it comes in vanity, departs in darkness, and has rest. Even a man living two thousand years without enjoying good goes to the same place as everyone. The chapter observes that human labor is for the mouth yet appetite is never filled, that the wise have no advantage over the fool, that man cannot contend with one mightier, and that no one knows what is good for man in his shadow-like life or what will come after him.

Chapter 7 body

Chapter 7 gathers proverbs contrasting wisdom and folly, with the Preacher arguing that the house of mourning, rebuke, patience, and wisdom are better than feasting, fools' laughter, hasty anger, and bribery. He tells readers to consider the work of God, who makes both prosperity and adversity, and observes that a righteous man may perish while a wicked man lives long. He advises against being overly righteous or overly wicked, notes that no righteous person on earth is free of sin, and says people should not heed every word spoken, since they too have cursed others. The Preacher says he tested all this by wisdom but found it far from him, and that what exists is deeply hidden. He reports finding one man among a thousand but no woman, and concludes that God made mankind upright, but they seek many inventions.

Chapter 8 body

Chapter 8 is the narrator's reflection on wisdom, royal authority, and the limits of human understanding. The narrator urges keeping the king's command because of the oath to God, noting that the king does whatever pleases him and that the obedient wise heart knows the proper time and procedure. He observes that no man controls the spirit or the day of death, that one man sometimes has power over another to his hurt, and that the wicked are buried and forgotten. Because sentence against evil is not executed speedily, people's hearts are set to do evil, yet the narrator affirms it will be better for those who fear God and not well for the wicked. He calls it vanity that the righteous and the wicked sometimes receive each other's due, commends mirth (eating, drinking, being joyful), and concludes that man cannot find out the work of God under the sun, however hard he labors or however wise he claims to be.

Chapter 9 body

In Chapter 9 the narrator reflects that the righteous, the wise and their works are in God's hand, and that one event, death, comes alike to the righteous and the wicked, the clean and unclean, the one who sacrifices and the one who does not. He argues that the living have hope, since a living dog is better than a dead lion, while the dead know nothing, have no reward and are forgotten. He urges people to eat, drink, wear white, enjoy life with the wife they love, and work with all their might, because there is no work or knowledge in Sheol. He observes that the race is not to the swift nor the battle to the strong, since time and chance happen to all and no one knows his time. He closes with the story of a little city saved by a poor wise man whom no one remembered, concluding that wisdom is better than strength and weapons of war, though one sinner destroys much good.

Chapter 10 body

Chapter 10 collects proverbs contrasting wisdom and folly. A little folly spoils wisdom and honor like dead flies in perfumer's oil, and the fool's speech begins in foolishness and ends in mischievous madness. The speaker describes an evil under the sun in which folly is given high dignity, the rich sit low, and servants ride horses while princes walk. The chapter warns that ordinary labors such as digging, quarrying, and splitting wood carry risks, and that skill brings success. It then contrasts a land whose king is a child and whose princes feast in the morning with one whose king is of noble birth, and ends by warning against slothfulness and against cursing the king or the rich, even privately.

Chapter 11 body

Chapter 11 advises generous giving and diligent work despite not knowing what the future holds. The speaker urges casting bread on the waters and giving portions to seven or eight, since no one knows what evil may come on the earth. He says that one who watches the wind or clouds will neither sow nor reap, and that people cannot know the way of the wind, the growth of a child in the womb, or the work of God who does all. He therefore tells people to sow seed morning and evening, because they do not know which will prosper. The chapter ends by calling the light sweet and urging the young man to rejoice in his youth while remembering that God will bring him into judgment and that youth and the dawn of life are vanity.

Chapter 12 body

Chapter 12 closes the book by urging the reader to remember their Creator in youth, before the evil days of old age arrive. Old age and death are described through imagery of darkening lights, trembling keepers of the house, bowed strong men, failing desire, a severed silver cord and a broken golden bowl, after which the dust returns to the earth and the spirit returns to God who gave it. The Preacher declares 'Vanity of vanities! All is vanity!' and is described as a wise man who taught the people knowledge, pondered, sought out and set in order many proverbs, and sought acceptable words of truth. The words of the wise are compared to goads and well-fastened nails given from one shepherd, and the reader, addressed as 'my son', is warned that making many books has no end and much study wearies the flesh. The conclusion is to fear God and keep his commandments as the whole duty of man, for God will bring every work and hidden thing into judgment, whether good or evil.
