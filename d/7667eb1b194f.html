Abstract written by a model

This document is Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou), Part I with a brief overview of Part II. It presents Mencius’s teaching that effective rule and personal conduct rest on benevolence, righteousness, propriety, sincerity, self-examination, and the models of ancient sages. He argues that skill and good intentions require laws, instruments, and the practices of the ancient kings; disorder arises when rulers and ministers ignore principle and propriety. States ultimately depend on the person of their heads, families depend on their heads, and kingdoms depend on States. Benevolence wins and preserves thrones by gaining the people’s hearts, whereas oppressive rulers such as Jie and Zhou lose them and drive people toward Tang and Wu. Rulers should imitate Yao, ministers Shun, and princes king Wen; a ruler corrected in mind will make his acts and kingdom correct as well. Mencius repeatedly turns responsibility inward: when love, government, or courtesy fails to receive a response, one should examine oneself. Self-caused disgrace and calamity are especially destructive, while sincerity, grounded in knowing the good, enables a person to move others. The work also treats practical duty: love parents, respect elders, preserve oneself in order to serve parents, and distinguish true filial piety from merely feeding parents. Shun’s devotion to Gu Sou exemplifies great filial piety. Mencius condemns ministers who enrich rulers through oppressive exactions and war, as well as people who reject benevolence and righteousness, pursue food and drink, or seek riches, honours, gain, and advancement deceptively. Dialogues with Chun Yu Kun, Gong Sun Chou, Yue Zheng, and others clarify that principles, not expedients, rescue a kingdom; fathers should avoid teaching sons when it creates alienation; and character may be discerned through words and the pupil of the eye. Part II continues these concerns through teachings on sages, rulers, ministers, scholars, learning, steadfast conduct, and exemplary behavior.

Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou) / Part I / Chapter 1 body

Mencius explains that skill, virtue, and benevolent intentions cannot govern effectively without instruments, laws, and the practices of the ancient kings. He argues that benevolent government brings order and benefits to the people, while rulers and ministers who disregard principle, laws, propriety, and righteousness spread disorder through the kingdom. The text warns that a State is ruined not mainly by weak defenses or poor resources, but when superiors violate propriety and inferiors fail to learn. It also teaches that serving a sovereign properly requires urging difficult achievements, promoting good, and restraining perversity.

Chapter 2 body

Mencius says that the compass and square produce perfect circles and squares, while the sages perfectly exhibit human relations. He teaches that a sovereign should imitate Yao and a minister should imitate Shun in order to fulfill their duties. Confucius says that people have only two courses: virtue and its opposite. The text warns that a ruler who oppresses the people will be slain or endangered, and the kingdom will perish or be weakened; the Book of Poetry presents the downfall of Xia as a warning.

Chapter 3 body

Mencius explains that benevolence enabled the three dynasties to gain the throne, while failure to practice it caused them to lose the throne. He says that benevolence determines whether States and their rulers flourish, are preserved, decay, or perish. He warns that a sovereign who is not benevolent cannot keep the throne, and that a Head of a State who is not benevolent cannot preserve his rule.

Chapter 4 body

Mencius teaches that a person should examine himself when love, government, or politeness receives no responsive return. He says that when desires are not achieved through one's actions, one should examine oneself in every respect. He also says that when a man's person is correct, the whole kingdom will turn to him with recognition and submission. The Book of Poetry teaches that studying to harmonize with the ordinances of God brings much happiness.

Chapter 5 body

Mencius states that people commonly speak of the kingdom, the State, and the family. He explains that the State is the root of the kingdom. He further explains that the family is the root of the State, and the person of its Head is the root of the family.

Chapter 6 body

Mencius says that the administration of government is not difficult when it avoids offending the great families. The influence of the great families extends through the whole State, and the influence of any one State extends through the whole kingdom. When this influence operates, a person's virtue and teachings spread throughout the four seas like rushing water.

Chapter 7 body

Mencius explains that under right government, princes of little virtue or worth submit to those of greater virtue or worth, while under bad government, weaker princes submit to stronger ones. He identifies both patterns as the rule of Heaven, saying those who accord with Heaven are preserved and those who rebel against it perish. He advises small States that are ashamed to receive commands to imitate king Wen, after which they may give laws to the kingdom within five or seven years. He concludes that a prince who loves benevolence will have no opponent in the kingdom, but seeking that result without benevolence is like handling a heated substance without first dipping it in water.

Chapter 8 body

Mencius questions how anyone can speak with princes who are not benevolent and who mistake dangers and calamities for safety and profit. A boy’s song about the water of the Cang Lang leads Confucius to explain that its different applications depend on whether the water is clear or muddy. The unit teaches that a man, family, or State first brings about its own disgrace or destruction before others do so. It concludes that Heaven-sent calamities may be escaped, but self-caused calamities cannot be survived.

Chapter 9 body

Mencius explains that Jie and Zhou lost the throne because they lost the people and their hearts. He says that rulers can gain the kingdom by gaining the people, whose hearts are won by providing what they like and avoiding what they dislike. The people turn toward a benevolent rule, while Jie and Zhou unintentionally drove them toward Tang and Wu. Mencius urges the present rulers of the kingdom to set their wills on benevolence or face sorrow, disgrace, death, and ruin.

Chapter 10 body

Mencius says that it is impossible to speak with those who do violence to themselves or to do anything with those who throw themselves away. He defines doing violence to oneself as rejecting propriety and righteousness in conversation. He defines throwing oneself away as claiming an inability to dwell in benevolence or pursue the path of righteousness. He teaches that benevolence is man's tranquil habitation and righteousness is his straight path, lamenting those who abandon both.

Chapter 11 body

Mencius explains that the path of duty is found in what is near, but men seek it in what is remote. He says the work of duty is found in what is easy, but men seek it in what is difficult. He teaches that if each man loves his parents and shows due respect to his elders, the whole land would enjoy tranquillity.

Chapter 12 body

Mencius explains how those occupying inferior situations can succeed in governing the people by obtaining the confidence of the sovereign. He connects that confidence to being trusted by friends, serving parents so as to please them, and possessing inward sincerity. He teaches that sincerity depends on understanding what is good and calls sincerity the way of Heaven. He also states that complete sincerity moves others, while its absence prevents a person from moving others.

Chapter 13 body

Mencius recounts how Bo Yi and Tai Gong, while avoiding Zhou, lived on the northern and eastern coasts respectively. After hearing of the rise of king Wen, both decided to follow him because they had heard that the chief of the West knew how to nourish the old. Their allegiance represented the fathers of the kingdom joining king Wen, which would draw the sons to him as well. Mencius says that any prince who practiced the government of king Wen would within seven years be giving laws to the kingdom.

Chapter 14 body

Mencius discusses Qiu, who served the head of the Ji family but failed to change his evil ways and exacted double grain from the people. Confucius rejected Qiu as a disciple and called for him to be attacked. The passage condemns ministers who enrich princes, especially those who fight for them and cause mass slaughter. It ranks skilful fighters, league-makers, and those who seize grassy commons for punishment, with the first suffering the highest punishment.

Chapter 15 body

Mencius teaches that the pupil of the eye is the most excellent part of a man's body. The pupil cannot hide a man's wickedness because it is bright when his breast is correct and dull when it is not. By listening to a man's words and looking at his pupil, one can learn his character.

Chapter 16 body

Mencius explains that respectful people do not despise others and economical people do not plunder them. He says a prince who despises and plunders people is merely afraid they will not obey him. Mencius questions whether respectfulness and economy can be shown only through vocal tones and a smiling manner.

Chapter 17 body

Chun Yu Kun asks Mencius whether males and females should avoid touching hands when giving or receiving things. Mencius says this is the general rule but explains that a man must rescue his drowning sister-in-law with his hand. Kun compares the whole kingdom to a drowning person and questions why Mencius will not rescue it. Mencius answers that a drowning kingdom must be rescued with right principles, not by hand.

Chapter 18 body

Gong Sun Chou asks Mencius why the superior man does not personally teach his son. Mencius explains that a teacher must give correct instruction, but anger and reproving admonitions can cause father and son to become offended with each other. He says the ancients exchanged sons for teaching because alienation between father and son is especially inauspicious.

Chapter 19 body

Mencius teaches that serving parents is the greatest service and taking charge of oneself is the greatest charge. He says that those who preserve themselves can serve their parents, and that service of parents and charge of oneself are roots of other duties. The examples of Zengzi and Zeng Yuan distinguish nourishing a parent’s will from merely nourishing the mouth and body. Serving parents as Zengzi served his is presented as filial piety.

Chapter 20 body

Mencius says that remonstrating with a sovereign about the mal-employment of ministers or blaming errors of government is not enough. He says only the great man can rectify what is wrong in the sovereign's mind. If the prince is benevolent, righteous, and correct, his acts and everything under him will reflect those qualities, and once the ruler is rectified, the kingdom will be firmly settled.

Chapter 21 body

Chapter 21 records a statement by Mencius. Mencius says that some praise cannot be expected. He also says that reproach can occur when the parties involved have been seeking to be perfect.

Chapter 22 body

Mencius explains why men are ready with their tongues. He says this readiness arises simply because they have not been reproved. The unit concerns the relationship between men's readiness with their tongues and the absence of reproof.

Chapter 23 body

Mencius comments on the evil of men. He says that men like to be teachers of others. The unit concerns men, their evil, and their attitude toward teaching others.

Chapter 24 body

Yue Zheng traveled to Qi and came to see Mencius. Mencius questioned whether Yue Zheng had come specifically to see him and learned that Yue Zheng had arrived the previous day. Yue Zheng explained that his lodging-house had not been arranged. Mencius taught that a scholar's lodging-house should be arranged before visiting an elder, and Yue Zheng admitted that he had done wrong.

Chapter 25 body

Mencius addresses the disciple Yue Zheng about his coming there in the train of Zi Ao. Mencius says that Yue Zheng came only because of the food and drink. He expresses disbelief that Yue Zheng, having learned the doctrine of the ancients, would act with a view to eating and drinking.

Chapter 26 body

Mencius identifies three things as unfilial and says that having no posterity is the greatest. Shun married without informing his parents because he feared having no posterity. Superior men judge that Shun’s action was equivalent to informing his parents.

Chapter 27 body

Mencius describes the richest fruits of benevolence, righteousness, wisdom, propriety, and music. Benevolence is fulfilled in the service of one's parents, while righteousness is fulfilled in obeying one's elder brothers. Wisdom knows these two things and does not depart from them; propriety orders and adorns them; music rejoices in them, causing them to grow until joy is expressed through dancing feet and moving hands.

Chapter 28 body

Mencius describes Shun as uniquely able to regard the whole kingdom’s joyful submission as insignificant compared with gaining his parents’ hearts. Shun fully served his father, Gu Sou, until Gu Sou found delight in what was good. Gu Sou’s change transformed the kingdom, establishing proper duties between fathers and sons, which is called great filial piety.

Part II / Chapter 1 .. Chapter 33 body

Mencius discusses the conduct of sages, rulers, ministers, scholars, and superior men. He explains how benevolence, righteousness, propriety, learning, self-examination, and steadfast conduct should guide people. Confucius is mentioned for his judgments, praise of exemplary conduct, and teaching that sages are like other men. The final story condemns deceptive pursuit of riches, honours, gain, and advancement.
