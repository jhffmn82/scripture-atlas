Mencius, Book VI (Gaozi)

Abstract written by a model

This unit presents Mencius’s teachings on human nature, moral cultivation, honour, government, and adversity. In debate with Gao and Gong Du, he argues that people tend toward goodness and possess beginnings of benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and knowledge. Circumstance, neglect, and lack of thought can hide this nature, but nourishment, reflection, and steadfast attention recover it; learning is the search for one’s lost mind. He urges people to value Heaven’s nobility, righteousness above life, and what is great over what is small. Mencius then distinguishes true honour, grounded in benevolence and righteousness, from honours conferred by others, and teaches that benevolence must be sufficient, mature, and learned through complete rules. Turning to public life, he treats propriety as responsive to circumstances, connects Yao and Shun’s way to filial and fraternal duty, and insists rulers and ministers reject profit, conquest, unjust taxation, and improper political conduct. He praises love of goodness as fit for governing Lu, explains when superior men accept or leave office, and closes by portraying suffering as preparation for great responsibility, distress as a source of reform, ease as a cause of ruin, and even refusal to teach as instruction.

Mencius, Book VI (Gaozi) front matter

Part I / Chapter 1 .. Chapter 16 body

The unit concerns Mencius's debates with Gao and Gong Du about man's nature, arguing that it tends toward good and contains the beginnings of benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and knowledge. Mencius explains that circumstances, neglect, and failure to think can obscure this proper nature, while nourishment, reflection, and steadfast attention preserve it. He teaches that the human mind should seek its lost goodness, that learning consists in this recovery, and that people should cultivate what is great rather than what is small. The unit also distinguishes the nobility of Heaven from the nobility of man and states that righteousness may be valued above life and death avoided only at the cost of virtue.

Chapter 17 .. Chapter 20 body

Mencius discusses honour, benevolence, maturity, and instruction. He says men naturally desire honour, but true honour comes from benevolence and righteousness rather than from what men confer. He compares benevolence overcoming opposition to water overcoming fire, while warning that insufficient benevolence fails and encourages the non-benevolent. He also teaches that benevolence must mature and that students should follow the complete rules demonstrated by their teachers.

Part II / Chapter 1 .. Chapter 13 body

Mencius teaches that rules of propriety remain important but must be understood in relation to circumstances, and that people can follow the ways of Yao and Shun through filial piety and fraternal duty. He argues that rulers and ministers should be guided by benevolence and righteousness rather than profit, conquest, excessive taxation, or enrichment. Mencius criticizes the five chiefs of the princes, the princes, and the Great officers for violating proper political order and praises those who love what is good. He advises the prince of Lu against employing an uninstructed army and rejoices that Yue Zheng loves what is good, since this is sufficient qualification for governing Lu.

Chapter 14 .. Chapter 16 body

Mencius explains the principles by which superior men accepted or left office, depending on a prince’s respect and willingness to act on their words. He describes how Heaven prepares people for great offices through suffering, toil, poverty, and frustration, citing Shun and others as examples. He teaches that distress can lead to reform and strength, while ease and pleasure can lead to a kingdom’s ruin. Mencius also says that refusing to teach someone can itself become a form of teaching.
