Abstract written by a model

Sutrakritanga, Book II begins with Sudharman recounting Mahâvîra’s teachings. The First Lecture, “the Lotus,” uses a lotus-pool simile to explain the world, Karman, pleasure, teachers, the Law, preaching, and Nirvâna, then rejects doctrines identifying soul with body or making elements, Self, or Fate the sole cause of everything. It urges renunciation, nonviolence, mendicancy, chastity, and freedom from possessions and passions, stressing that neither wealth, relations, nor the body protects against suffering and death. The following lectures distinguish sinful, meritorious, and mixed activity: violence, lying, theft, pride, deceit, greed, harmful occupations, and occult practices bring bad Karman, hell, and Samsâra, while careful monks destroy Karman and attain Kêvala, liberation, Nirvâna, and final beatitude. The text repeatedly teaches that all beings fear pain and must not be harmed. It examines beings’ births, bodies, food, growth, and Karman, calling for care concerning food; explains that sin can arise through mental, verbal, and bodily activity even without deliberate reflection; and presents controlled conduct as renunciation of activity. A monk is instructed to reject error, affirm the teachings of the Ginas, and seek final liberation. Ârdraka defends Mahâvîra against Gôsâla, a Buddhist, a Vêdic Priest, a Vêdântin, and a Hastitâpasa, emphasizing individual souls, compassion, avoidance of new Karman, and Kêvala knowledge. Finally, Gautama debates Udaka at Nâlandâ about vows against injury and rebirth through Karman. Udaka accepts Gautama’s view and joins Mahâvîra’s creed of five great vows and Pratikramana.

Sutrakritanga, Book II / First Lecture, Called the Lotus. body

Sutrakritanga, Book II, First Lecture, Called the Lotus, records a discourse that Sudharman heard from Mahâvîra. Mahâvîra explains a lotus-pool simile in which four men enter the mud seeking a white lotus, while a monk remains on the bank and calls the lotus to rise. The simile identifies the world, Karman, pleasures and amusements, people, the king, heretical teachers, the Law, the church, preaching, and Nirvâna. The lecture then criticizes four doctrines: that soul and body are identical, that everything consists of five elements, that the Self causes everything, and that Fate causes everything.

I say: here in the East, West, North, and South there are some men, viz. Âryas, body

This unit concerns the conduct and reflections of monks, householders, Sramanas, and Brâhmanas, especially renunciation, mendicant's life, nonviolence, and freedom from possessions and passions. It teaches that pleasures and amusements, relations, possessions, and the body cannot protect anyone from illness, suffering, or death. It explains that living beings experience pain and should not be killed, abused, tormented, or driven away, and that monks must avoid acquiring property, sinful actions, and improperly obtained food. The Law guides monks toward control, chastity, the cessation of Karman and sins, Liberation, Nirvâna, and final beatitude.

Second Lecture, Called on Activity. body

Sudharman reports Mahâvîra’s discourse on Activity, which briefly concerns merit and demerit. The unit explains thirteen kinds of committing sins, including violence, lying, taking what is not freely given, pride, deceit, greed, and actions connected with religious life. It describes how men and other beings acquire bad Karman through harmful acts, while a monk’s careful religious activity produces Karman that is destroyed immediately. It also condemns occult sciences and various violent, deceitful, and greedy occupations as practices that lead away from perfection, liberation, Nirvâna, and final delivery.

Now the explanation of the second subject, viz. merit, is as follows: body

The unit explains demerit, merit, and the mixed state through the conduct and destinies of different men, monks, and followers of the Sramanas. Demeritorious men commit violence and other sins, acquire bad Karman, and descend into hell and Samsâra, while meritorious monks attain Kêvala, absolute perfection, enlightenment, deliverance, and final beatitude. Followers of the Sramanas practice partial restraint and are reborn as gods. The text also describes three hundred and sixty-three philosophical schools and teaches, through a vessel full of burning coals, that all living beings fear pain and that violence leads to suffering.

Third Lecture, Called Knowledge of Food. body

The Third Lecture, Called Knowledge of Food, is a discourse that Sudharman heard from Mahâvîra about living beings, their birth, growth, food, bodies, and Karman. It describes beings arising in earth, water, fire, wind, plants, animals, human beings, parasites, and other bodies, and explains what they consume and how their bodies develop. It teaches that all living beings experience, undergo, and are shaped by Karman, and urges care and circumspection regarding food.

Fourth Lecture, Called Renunciation of Activity. body

The Fourth Lecture, Called Renunciation of Activity, concerns the Self, sin, and the conditions by which activity becomes sinful or is renounced. Mahâvîra teaches that even one who does not consciously consider mental, verbal, and bodily activity may still commit sins through failure to avoid harm to the six classes of living beings. The Âkârya explains this through the examples of a murderer, sentient and senseless beings, and intentions directed toward living beings generally. The unit concludes that a monk avoids the five cardinal sins and passions, becomes controlled and restrained, and renounces activity through careful conduct.

Fifth Lecture, Called Freedom from Error. body

The unit concerns a clever monk who practises chastity and instructs him to reject heretical alternatives that lead to error rather than truth. It affirms the existence of the world, Gîva, Agîva, Dharma, Adharma, bondage, liberation, Âsrava, the stoppage of Âsrava, Karman, and various moral and spiritual distinctions. The monk is told to conform to the opinions taught by the Ginas and to wander until he reaches final liberation.

Sixth Lecture, Called . body

The unit concerns Ârdraka’s defense of Mahâvîra, the conduct of Sramanas, and disputes with Gôsâla, a Buddhist, a Vêdic Priest, a Vêdântin, and a Hastitâpasa. Ârdraka teaches that Mahâvîra remains single in spirit while teaching many monks, avoids new Karman, and leads others toward Môksha. The unit condemns eating seeds, using cold water, sexual intercourse, accepting specially prepared food, slaughter, and teachings that deny guilt for harming living beings. It also distinguishes individual souls, praises compassion and right conduct, and states that Kêvala knowledge enables one to teach the whole Law and save others.

Seventh Lecture, Called Nâlandâ. body

This unit concerns Gautama’s debate with Udaka, the son of Pêdhâla, about renunciation of injury to movable and immovable living beings and the consequences of such vows. Gautama rejects the claim that renouncing injury to only one class of beings can end injury altogether, explaining that beings change forms within the Circle of Births through Karman. The discussion examines householders’ vows, fasting and death practices, limits of renunciation, and the rebirth of living beings. Udaka accepts Gautama’s teaching and, with Gautama, goes to the Venerable Ascetic Mahâvîra, where he changes from the creed which enjoins four vows to the creed which enjoins the five great vows and the Pratikramana.
