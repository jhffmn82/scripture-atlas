Abstract written by a model

This document is Paul's Letter to the Galatians, a six-chapter epistle written by Paul, an apostle commissioned through Jesus Christ and God the Father, together with the brothers with him, to the assemblies of Galatia. Its central concern is that the Galatians are deserting the true Good News for a different, perverted gospel that insists on circumcision and works of the law. The most important figures are Paul, the Galatians, Christ, Abraham, and the Jerusalem leaders (Peter/Cephas, James, John), with Barnabas and Titus in supporting roles. Chapter 1 opens with greeting, astonishment at the Galatians' quick defection, and a curse on anyone preaching a different gospel. Paul insists his message came by revelation of Jesus Christ, and recounts his former persecution of the assembly, his call, his time in Arabia, Damascus, Jerusalem, Syria and Cilicia. Chapter 2 describes his Jerusalem visit after fourteen years, where Titus was not compelled to be circumcised and James, Cephas, and John extended fellowship, agreeing Paul and Barnabas would go to the Gentiles. He then recounts opposing Peter at Antioch for withdrawing from Gentiles, and argues that people are justified by faith in Christ, not works of the law, and that Christ lives in him. Chapter 3 rebukes the Galatians as foolish, appeals to Abraham's faith, and argues that Christ redeemed believers from the law's curse, that the promise to Abraham and his offspring, Christ, is not annulled by the later law, which served as a tutor until faith came; all in Christ are one, children of God and heirs. Chapter 4 uses the image of an heir under guardians, God sending his Son so believers receive adoption and the Spirit crying 'Abba, Father,' rebukes the return to elemental principles and observed days, and gives an allegory of Hagar and the free woman representing two covenants, concluding the readers are children of promise. Chapter 5 urges standing firm in Christ's liberty, warns that circumcision obligates one to the whole law, says love fulfills the law, and contrasts the deeds of the flesh with the fruit of the Spirit. Chapter 6 gives instructions for community life: restoring the faulty gently, bearing burdens, supporting teachers, and sowing to the Spirit rather than the flesh. Writing in large letters, Paul accuses those compelling circumcision of avoiding persecution, boasts only in the cross, declares a new creation matters, mentions bearing Jesus's marks, and closes with a blessing and prayer for grace.

Paul’s Letter to the Galatians / Chapter 1 body

Paul, an apostle commissioned through Jesus Christ and God the Father rather than by men, writes with the brothers who are with him to the assemblies of Galatia. After a greeting of grace and peace, he expresses astonishment that the Galatians are quickly deserting the one who called them for a different "good news," which he says is not another gospel but a perversion by those who trouble them. He pronounces a curse on anyone, even himself or an angel from heaven, who preaches a different gospel. He insists that the Good News he preached came not from man but through a revelation of Jesus Christ, and he recounts his former persecution of the assembly, his call by God, his time in Arabia and Damascus, his brief visit to Peter and James in Jerusalem, and his time in Syria and Cilicia, where the assemblies of Judea heard of his change and glorified God in him.

Chapter 2 body

The speaker, Paul, recounts going up to Jerusalem after fourteen years with Barnabas and Titus, laying before the respected leaders the Good News he preaches to the Gentiles. Titus, a Greek, was not compelled to be circumcised, and Paul refused to yield to the false brothers who sought to bring them into bondage. James, Cephas, and John gave Paul and Barnabas the right hand of fellowship, agreeing that they would go to the Gentiles and the leaders to the circumcision, asking only that they remember the poor. Paul then describes resisting Peter to his face at Antioch, where Peter withdrew from eating with Gentiles for fear of those from James, and the other Jews and Barnabas followed him. Paul argues that a person is justified by faith in Christ and not by works of the law, and says he has been crucified with Christ, who now lives in him.

Chapter 3 body

In Galatians chapter 3 the writer rebukes the Galatians as foolish for turning from faith to the works of the law, asking whether they received the Spirit by works or by hearing with faith. He points to Abraham, who believed God and was counted righteous, to argue that those of faith are Abraham's children and are blessed with him. The law brings a curse on those who rely on its works, but Christ redeemed believers from that curse by becoming a curse for them, so that the blessing of Abraham and the promise of the Spirit might reach the Gentiles. The writer argues that the promise made to Abraham and his offspring, who is Christ, was confirmed by God and is not annulled by the law that came 430 years later. The law was added because of transgressions and served as a tutor until faith came, and now all who are in Christ are children of God, one in Christ, and Abraham's offspring and heirs according to promise.

Chapter 4 body

In Chapter 4 the author, writing to the brothers who desire to be under the law, explains that an heir who is a child is no different from a bondservant until the day appointed by the father. He says that when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, born to a woman and under the law, to redeem those under the law so they might receive adoption as children, and sent the Spirit of his Son into their hearts crying "Abba, Father!" He rebukes them for turning back to the weak and miserable elemental principles and observing days, months, seasons, and years, recalls how they once received him as an angel of God, and expresses his fear and perplexity about them. He then offers an allegory of Abraham's two sons, one by the servant Hagar, born according to the flesh, and one by the free woman, born through promise, representing two covenants and two Jerusalems. He concludes that the readers, like Isaac, are children of promise and not children of a servant, but of the free woman.

Chapter 5 body

Paul urges his readers to stand firm in the liberty Christ gave them and warns that receiving circumcision cuts them off from Christ and obligates them to keep the whole law. He expresses confidence that they will not be swayed, says those who trouble them will bear judgment, and notes that his own persecution shows he does not preach circumcision. He explains that freedom is for serving one another through love, which fulfills the whole law, and warns against biting and devouring one another. He contrasts walking by the Spirit with the flesh, lists the deeds of the flesh and the fruit of the Spirit, and closes by saying those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh and should walk by the Spirit without conceit or envy.

Chapter 6 body

In the closing chapter of the letter, the author instructs the brothers on how to live in community: restore a person caught in a fault gently, bear one another's burdens, examine one's own work, and support those who teach the word. He warns that God is not mocked and that what a man sows he will reap, with sowing to the flesh bringing corruption and sowing to the Spirit bringing eternal life, and urges them not to grow weary in doing good. Writing in large letters with his own hand, he accuses those who compel circumcision of seeking a good impression and avoiding persecution for the cross of Christ, and of wanting to boast in the recipients' flesh. He declares that he boasts only in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, that neither circumcision nor uncircumcision matters but a new creation, and that he bears the marks of Jesus on his body. He ends by blessing those who walk by this rule, and with a prayer for grace on the brothers.
