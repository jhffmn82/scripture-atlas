The Wisdom of Solomon

author unknown · 20 sections
The Wisdom of Solomon, from the World English Bible, Catholic Edition, dedicated to the public domain by eBible.org. Shown here as plain text, with facts drawn from it. https://ebible.org/eng-web-c/

Abstract written by a model

This document is a nineteen-chapter wisdom text that exhorts rulers to righteousness, contrasts the fates of the righteous and the ungodly, praises Wisdom, and recounts how she acted in sacred history. It opens by addressing the judges of the earth, urging them to love righteousness and warning that Wisdom will not enter an evil soul. God did not make death; the ungodly summon it. Chapters 2 to 5 give the reasoning of the ungodly, who think life brief and ending in oblivion, so they revel, oppress the poor and plot against the righteous man who calls himself God's child. The narrator replies that God created man for incorruption. The righteous are held in God's hand, tested like gold, and given authority, while the ungodly and their children are punished. Virtue, even in childlessness or early death, brings immortality. In the end the ungodly see the righteous man vindicated and lament, and creation fights for the Lord against his enemies. Chapters 6 to 9 turn to kings and rulers, who are warned that judgment will fall on those who fail to judge rightly, and are urged to seek Wisdom. A speaking king says he is mortal like everyone, prayed for understanding and valued Wisdom above thrones and riches. He describes her as a spirit, a breath of God's power and an image of his goodness. He seeks her as a bride, expecting counsel, honor and immortality, and then prays that God will send her to guide him, since without her no one counts for anything. Chapters 10 to 12 and 16 to 19 retell the story of God's people. Wisdom saved the righteous from the first father through the flood, the burning cities, the dungeon and the exodus, led them across the Red sea, and brought water from rock. The enemies, by contrast, were punished through the very things they worshiped: blood-filled rivers, vermin, locusts, flies, storms, and a heavy darkness, while God's people received quails, bread from heaven, healing and a pillar of fire. A death at midnight struck the oppressors, and a blameless man stopped wrath among the righteous. The pursuing Egyptians met a strange death at the sea. Throughout, God is shown as merciful, loving all things, correcting sinners gradually so they may repent, and judging in gentleness. Chapters 13 to 15 condemn idolatry: nature-worshipers are barely excused, while makers of idols, such as the woodcutter and potter, are ridiculed for praying to lifeless things. Idolatry is traced to grief and royal flattery and called the beginning, cause and end of every evil. The text closes with creation reordering itself like the notes of a lute, and God glorifying his people at every time and place.

The Wisdom of Solomon front matter

Chapter 1 body

Chapter 1 addresses the judges of the earth, urging them to love righteousness and to seek the Lord with a good mind and singleness of heart. It says wisdom will not enter a soul that devises evil, and that she, as a spirit who loves man, does not leave a blasphemer unpunished because God witnesses his heart and hears his tongue. The spirit of the Lord fills the world and knows what is said, so no unrighteous speech or murmuring stays hidden, and Justice will convict wrongdoers. The audience is warned against slander and against drawing death upon themselves, since God did not make death and created all things to have being. The chapter ends by saying righteousness is immortal, while ungodly men summon death with their hands and words, treat him as a friend, and make a covenant with him.

Chapter 2 body

Chapter 2 presents the reasoning of the ungodly, who believe life is short, random, and ends in oblivion with no release from Hades. From this they conclude that they should enjoy wine, perfumes, flowers, and revelry, and should oppress the righteous poor, the widow, and the aged, making their own strength the law. They single out the righteous man, who annoys them by reproaching their sins, claiming knowledge of God, and calling himself God's child, and they plot to test him with insult, torture, and a shameful death to see whether God will deliver him. The narrator declares that they were led astray and blinded by their wickedness, not knowing God's mysteries or the prize for blameless souls. The chapter closes by stating that God created man for incorruption in the image of his own everlastingness, and that death entered the world through the devil's envy and is experienced by those who belong to him.

Chapter 3 body

Chapter 3 contrasts the fates of the righteous and the ungodly. The righteous are held in God's hand, appear to the foolish to have died but are at peace, and are tested by God like gold in a furnace, then accepted, made to shine, and given authority over nations. The ungodly, who neglected righteousness and despised wisdom, are punished according to their reasoning, with futile hopes and works, foolish wives, wicked children, and cursed descendants. The chapter says the undefiled barren woman and the faithful eunuch are blessed with fruit and a delightful inheritance in the Lord's sanctuary. Children of adulterers are said not to reach maturity, to be without honor in old age or hope if they die young, and to share in the grievous end of an unrighteous generation.

Chapter 4 body

Chapter 4 contrasts childlessness joined with virtue against the numerous offspring of the ungodly. Virtue brings immortality, is imitated and longed for, and triumphs forever, while the ungodly's illegitimate offshoots are compared to shaky branches that are broken, bear useless fruit, and testify against their parents. A righteous man who dies early is at rest, because true old age is measured by understanding and an unspotted life, not by years. God, pleased with him, took him away from wickedness so that evil would not corrupt his soul. The peoples fail to understand this, but the ungodly will see the wise man's end, be scorned by the Lord, be dashed to the ground, and be convicted by their own lawless deeds.

Chapter 5 body

Chapter 5 describes the righteous man standing boldly before those who once afflicted him and dismissed his labors. Seeing him, they are terrified and amazed at his salvation, and they lament that they mocked him and went astray from the way of truth, comparing their vanished pride and riches to a shadow, a ship's wake, a bird's flight, and an arrow's path. The ungodly's hope is likened to chaff, foam, and smoke, while the righteous live forever with their reward in the Lord and receive a royal crown and diadem from his hand. The Lord takes zeal, righteousness, impartial judgment, holiness, and wrath as armor and weapons, and the whole creation joins him in battle against his enemies with lightning, hailstones, sea, rivers, and a mighty wind, so that lawlessness desolates the land and overturns the thrones of princes.

Chapter 6 body

The speaker addresses kings, judges, and rulers, reminding them that their dominion comes from the Lord and the Most High, who will examine their works and plans. Because these officers of his kingdom did not judge rightly, keep the law, or follow God's counsel, a stern and swift judgment will fall on those in high places, while the lowly may be pardoned and the mighty will be mightily tested. The speaker urges the princes to desire his words and learn wisdom, describing Wisdom as radiant, easily found by those who love and seek her, and meeting the worthy on their paths. Desire for instruction is called the beginning of Wisdom, leading through love and observance of her laws to immortality, closeness to God, and a kingdom. The speaker promises to explain what Wisdom is and how she came into being without hiding mysteries, and says a multitude of wise men is salvation to the world and an understanding king is stability for his people.

Chapter 7 body

In Chapter 7 the narrator, a king, declares that he is mortal like everyone else, descended from the first-formed man, formed in his mother's womb, and born with the same cry as all people. He says he prayed and was given understanding and a spirit of wisdom, which he valued above sceptres, thrones, riches, gems, health, beauty and light. He credits God as the guide of wisdom and the giver of unerring knowledge of the universe, including the elements, times and seasons, stars, creatures, winds, plants and roots. Wisdom is described as the architect of all things and as a spirit that is subtle, pure, all-powerful and all-surveying, a breath of God's power, a reflection of everlasting light and an image of his goodness. The chapter ends by saying that God loves the one who dwells with wisdom and that wisdom is fairer than the sun, since night overcomes daylight but evil does not prevail against wisdom.

Chapter 8 body

Chapter 8 is a first-person speech in which the speaker praises wisdom and describes his love for her, seeking her as a bride. He says wisdom reaches through all things, lives with God and is loved by him, and is richer and more profitable than any possession, teaching soberness, understanding, righteousness, and courage. The speaker lists what he expects from taking her to live with him: counsel in cares, glory and honor among elders and rulers, authority over peoples and nations, and rest at home. He also expects immortality and an eternal memory, and he reasons that immortality is akin to wisdom. Recognizing that he cannot possess wisdom unless God gives her, he pleads with the Lord with his whole heart, and the chapter ends as his prayer begins.

Chapter 9 body

In Chapter 9, a king prays to the God of his ancestors and Lord of mercy, who made all things by his word and formed man by his wisdom to have dominion and rule in holiness and righteousness. The speaker, calling himself God's servant and the son of God's handmaid, says he is weak, short-lived and little able to understand judgment and laws, and that even a perfect man counts for nothing without God's wisdom. He recalls that God chose him as king and judge of his people and commanded him to build a sanctuary and altar on the holy mountain, a copy of the holy tent. He asks God to send Wisdom, who sits by God's thrones, knew his works and was present at the world's making, so that she may guide him, make his works acceptable and make him worthy of his father's throne. He ends by observing that mortal thoughts are unstable, that the body weighs down the soul, and that people were taught what pleases God and were saved only through wisdom and God's holy spirit sent from on high.

Chapter 10 body

Chapter 10 recounts how wisdom saved and guided a series of righteous figures from the first formed father of the world through the exodus. She guarded the first father and gave him strength to rule, while the unrighteous man who killed his brother perished in his own rage. She saved a righteous man from the flood by a piece of wood, preserved another blameless to God amid confounded nations, and delivered a righteous man from the fire that fell on the five cities, whose wickedness left a smoking waste and a pillar of salt. She guided a fugitive from his brother's wrath and prospered him, and she stayed with a sold righteous man in the dungeon until he received a kingdom's sceptre. Finally she delivered a holy people from a nation of oppressors through a servant of the Lord, led them over the Red sea, drowned their enemies, and moved them to praise God.

Chapter 11 body

Chapter 11 recounts how Wisdom prospered the works of the righteous under a holy prophet as they crossed a trackless desert, withstood foes, and received water from flinty rock when they thirsted and called on God. The same waters that benefited them became punishment for their enemies, whose river turned to clotted blood in rebuke of the decree to slay babies. God tested the righteous as a father admonishing, but searched out the ungodly as a stern king condemning, and the enemies came to recognize the Lord when they heard of the others' benefit. For their worship of irrational reptiles and vermin, God punished the ungodly with a multitude of irrational creatures, so they would learn that a man is punished by the very things through which he sins. The chapter closes by noting that God, though able to destroy by a single breath and arranging all things by measure, number, and weight, has mercy on all, loves all that exists, and spares all things because they are his.

Chapter 12 body

Chapter 12 addresses the Lord, whose incorruptible spirit is in all things and who corrects sinners gradually so they may repent. It describes the old inhabitants of the holy land, accused of enchantments, child slaughter and ritual banquets of flesh and blood, whom the Lord chose to destroy by the hands of 'our fathers' so the land could receive a worthy colony of God's servants. Instead of destroying them at once, the Lord sent hornets and judged them little by little, giving a chance to repent although he knew their wickedness was inborn. The text argues that the Lord's power is the source of his righteousness and that he judges in gentleness, which teaches his people to be kind and to hope for repentance and mercy. It ends with the unrighteous who lived in folly and worshipped despised animals as gods, who were mocked, then punished through those same creatures, and so came to recognize the true God.

Chapter 13 body

Chapter 13 condemns men who lacked perception of God and failed to recognize the architect from his works, instead taking fire, wind, air, stars, water, or heavenly luminaries to be gods. It argues that if they admired the beauty or power of these things, they should understand that their Sovereign Lord, who created them, is far greater, and it grants these seekers small blame while still saying they are not excused for not finding him sooner. It then turns to those who call men's handiwork, such as gold, silver, animal likenesses, and stone, gods, and describes a woodcutter who saws down a tree, makes a useful vessel, cooks with the scraps, and carves a leftover knotty piece into the image of a man or animal. He paints it red, sets it in a wall secured with iron so it won't fall, and then prays to it for goods, marriage, children, health, life, aid, a safe journey, and success in business, though it is lifeless, weak, and unable to move or act.

Chapter 14 body

Chapter 14 begins with a seafarer who trusts a fragile piece of wood, and credits God's providence for giving a path through the sea, noting that wood saved the hope of the world on a raft when proud giants perished. It blesses the wood through which righteousness comes but curses the hand-made idol and its maker, since both are hateful to God and will be punished. The chapter traces the origin of idols to a grieving father's image of his dead child, to a custom hardened into law by princes, and to images of distant kings and an artisan's flattering beauty that drew the crowd to worship. It describes the resulting moral ruin, including child-slaughter, secret rites, adultery, murder, theft, perjury and disorder in marriage, calling idol worship the beginning, cause and end of every evil. It ends by declaring that worshipers who swear false oaths by lifeless idols will face the just penalty meant for sinners.

Chapter 15 body

The speaker addresses God as gracious, true, patient, and merciful, and says that the speakers, God's people, remain his even when they sin, since knowing him is perfect righteousness and the root of immortality. The chapter then ridicules idol makers, especially a potter who molds clay into vessels and also into a vain god, though he is himself made of earth and will soon return his borrowed soul. It says this potter is ignorant of the one who made him, treats life as a game for profit, and knows he sins. It also condemns the enemies of God's people who oppressed them and counted lifeless idols as gods, and it closes by saying that the creatures they worship are the most hateful and senseless, and lack God's praise and blessing.

Chapter 16 body

Chapter 16 contrasts God's treatment of his own people with his treatment of their oppressors. The oppressors are punished through vermin, locusts, flies, and strange storms of hail, rain and fire, while God's people receive quails as a delicacy and bread from heaven. When serpents bit God's people, their suffering was brief and a token of salvation healed them, showing that God's word and not herb or poultice heals, and that God holds authority over life and death. The fire is shown to change its behavior, burning hotter in water against the ungodly and forgetting its power to nourish the righteous, while snow and ice endure the fire. The chapter concludes that creation serves its maker, that God's word preserves those who trust him, and that people must rise before the sun to give thanks, since the hope of the unthankful melts like frost.

Chapter 17 body

Chapter 17 describes how lawless men, who thought they held a holy nation in their power, were themselves imprisoned by a heavy darkness and a long night. Their secret sins did not hide them: they were separated by a curtain of forgetfulness and haunted by terrifying sounds and phantoms. No fire or star could light the night, and their magic arts and boasted understanding failed, so those who promised to cure fear were sick with it. The text explains that wickedness is cowardly, condemned by conscience, and that fear is a surrender of reason's help. Everyone among them, whether farmer, shepherd, or laborer, was bound by one chain of darkness and paralyzed even by ordinary sounds of wind, birds, water, rocks, and beasts, while the rest of the world had clear light.

Chapter 18 body

Chapter 18 contrasts the great light given to God's holy ones with the darkness imposed on the Egyptians, who had imprisoned God's children. God provided a pillar of fire as guide for the people's journey, and punished the Egyptians for plotting to kill the holy ones' babes by taking away their children and destroying their army in a mighty flood. At midnight God's all-powerful word, a stern warrior bearing a sharp sword, descended and filled the land with death, while dreams forewarned the Egyptians why they were dying. The righteous also experienced death in the wilderness, but a blameless man, using prayer, incense, and the remembrance of oaths and covenants, stopped the wrath. His robe and diadem, bearing the world, the glories of the fathers, and God's majesty, caused the destroyer to yield.

Chapter 19 body

Chapter 19 describes how unmerciful indignation came upon the ungodly Egyptians, whom God foreknew would change their minds and pursue the people they had just sped on their way. While the Egyptians were still mourning their dead, a foolish decision drove them after the fugitives, so that they would fill up their lacking punishment and meet a strange death. Meanwhile creation was made new in obedience to God's commands: a cloud overshadowed the camp, and dry land rose from the Red sea as an unhindered highway, along which God's people passed, praising their deliverer. The chapter recalls earlier plagues of lice, frogs and quails, and contrasts the Egyptians' enslaving of benefactor guests with the harshness of others who refused strangers, noting the darkness that struck them at the righteous man's doors. It closes by comparing the elements to the changing notes of a lute, with land and water creatures exchanging places, fire and water reversing their natures, and God magnifying and glorifying his people at every time and place.
