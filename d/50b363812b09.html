The Book of Nehemiah

author unknown · about 399 to 299 BC · 13 sections
The Book of Nehemiah, from the World English Bible, Catholic Edition, dedicated to the public domain by eBible.org. Shown here as plain text, with facts drawn from it. https://ebible.org/eng-web-c/

Abstract written by a model

The Book of Nehemiah is a first-person and third-person account of Nehemiah, cupbearer to King Artaxerxes, who leads the rebuilding of Jerusalem's wall and the renewal of its community. In chapter 1, in Susa, he hears from his brother Hanani that Jerusalem's wall is broken and its gates burned, and he weeps, fasts, and prays, confessing Israel's sins. In chapter 2 the king, seeing his sadness, grants his request to go to Judah, with letters for safe passage and timber. Nehemiah inspects the walls at night and rallies the Jews, while Sanballat, Tobiah, and Geshem mock and accuse them. Chapter 3 lists the priests, families, and guilds who repaired each section and gate, noting that the Tekoite nobles would not join the work. Chapter 4 shows opposition growing into a plot to attack; Nehemiah answers with prayer, guards, and armed builders, with a trumpeter to rally the people. Chapter 5 turns to an internal crisis: the poor cry out against nobles who exact usury and take their fields and children. Nehemiah confronts them, secures an oath to restore what was taken, and recalls that in twelve years as governor he took no food allowance. In chapter 6 the enemies try four times to lure him to Ono, send a letter accusing him of rebellion, and hire Shemaiah to frighten him into the temple; he refuses all of it. The wall is finished in fifty-two days, and the nations perceive God's hand, though Tobiah still corresponds with nobles of Judah. Chapter 7 sets Hanani and Hananiah over the city, which is large but sparsely settled, and reproduces the genealogy of the first returnees, totaling 42,360, with gifts given to the work. Chapter 8 centers on Ezra reading the law at the water gate; the people weep, are told that the joy of the LORD is their strength, and celebrate the feast of booths. Chapter 9 is a day of fasting, confession, and a long Levite prayer recounting God's mercy and Israel's rebellion, ending in a written covenant. Chapter 10 lists those who sealed it and their pledges: no intermarriage, Sabbath observance, debt and seventh-year release, and support for the temple. Chapter 11 describes resettling Jerusalem by lot, one in ten, and lists settlements. Chapter 12 lists priests and Levites and describes the wall's dedication, with two thanksgiving processions meeting at God's house. Chapter 13 shows Nehemiah, back from the king, reversing lapses: he removes Tobiah from a temple room, restores the Levites' portions, stops Sabbath trading, and confronts foreign marriages, repeatedly asking God to remember him.

The Book of Nehemiah / Chapter 1 body

Nehemiah, son of Hacaliah, is in Susa the palace in the twentieth year, in the month Chislev, when his brother Hanani and some men from Judah come to him. They report that the Jewish remnant in the province is in great affliction and reproach, and that Jerusalem's wall is broken down and its gates burned. Nehemiah sits down, weeps, mourns for several days, and fasts and prays before the God of heaven. In his prayer he confesses the sins of Israel, including his own and his father's house, recalls God's word to Moses about scattering and regathering, and asks for mercy before 'this man'. The chapter ends by noting that Nehemiah was cupbearer to the king.

Chapter 2 body

In the twentieth year of Artaxerxes the king, the cupbearer narrator, visibly sad before the king, explains that his ancestral city lies waste with its gates burned. The king asks his request, the narrator prays to the God of heaven, and he asks to be sent to Judah to build the city. The king grants him letters to the governors beyond the River and to Asaph, keeper of the king's forest, for timber, and sends army captains and horsemen with him. After arriving in Jerusalem, the narrator secretly inspects the broken walls by night, then urges the Jews, priests, nobles and rulers to rebuild, and they agree. Sanballat the Horonite, Tobiah the Ammonite servant, and Geshem the Arabian ridicule the effort and accuse them of rebelling, but the narrator answers that the God of heaven will prosper the builders and that the opponents have no portion in Jerusalem.

Chapter 3 body

Chapter 3 lists the many people and groups who rebuilt and repaired the wall and gates of Jerusalem, section by section. Eliashib the high priest and his fellow priests started the work at the sheep gate, and the sons of Hassenaah, Hanun with the people of Zanoah, Malchijah son of Rechab, and Shallun built the fish, valley, dung, and spring gates, along with the old gate. Rulers of districts, goldsmiths, perfumers, merchants, Levites, temple servants' neighbors, and families working across from their own houses each repaired assigned portions. The Tekoites repaired two sections, though their nobles would not put their necks to the Lord's work. The listing ends where it began, with the goldsmiths and merchants repairing between the ascent of the corner and the sheep gate.

Chapter 4 body

Sanballat and Tobiah the Ammonite mock the Jews who are rebuilding the wall of Jerusalem, and the narrator prays that God will turn their reproach back on them. The people keep building until the wall reaches half its height, which angers Sanballat, Tobiah, the Arabians, the Ammonites, and the Ashdodites, who conspire to attack Jerusalem. The narrator responds with prayer and a watch, then posts armed guards by family groups and urges the nobles, rulers, and people not to fear but to remember the Lord. After the enemies learn their plan is known and God has frustrated it, the work resumes with half the servants building and half holding weapons, builders working with one hand and holding a weapon with the other, and a trumpeter stationed by the narrator to rally the people. The narrator also has everyone lodge inside Jerusalem to guard at night and work by day, and he and his men do not take off their clothes.

Chapter 5 body

Chapter 5 describes a great cry from the people and their wives against their fellow Jews, who were mortgaging fields, vineyards, and houses to buy grain during famine and to pay the king's tribute, and who were bringing sons and daughters into bondage. The narrator, whose speech is in the first person, became very angry, consulted with himself, and confronted the nobles and rulers for exacting usury from their brothers. He held a great assembly against them, reminded them that he had redeemed Jews sold to the nations, and urged them to stop the usury and restore the fields, vineyards, olive groves, houses, and the hundredth part they had charged. The nobles and rulers agreed, took an oath before the priests, and the assembly said Amen and praised the LORD after he shook out his lap as a sign of what would befall anyone who broke the promise. He then recounts that during his twelve years as governor, from the twentieth to the thirty-second year of Artaxerxes the king, he did not take the governor's food allowance, unlike former governors, and fed one hundred fifty men at his table, and he closes by asking God to remember him for his good.

Chapter 6 body

Nehemiah's enemies Sanballat, Tobiah, and Geshem the Arabian learn that the wall is built with no breach left, though the gates have no doors yet. Sanballat and Geshem four times invite Nehemiah to meet them in the plain of Ono, intending harm, and Nehemiah refuses each time, saying he is doing a great work. Sanballat then sends an open letter accusing Nehemiah of plotting rebellion and kingship, which Nehemiah denies as invention, and the hired Shemaiah urges him to hide in the temple, which Nehemiah refuses, discerning that God had not sent him. The wall is finished on the twenty-fifth of Elul in fifty-two days, and the surrounding nations lose confidence, perceiving the work was done by God. Meanwhile nobles of Judah, bound to Tobiah by oath and marriage ties, exchange letters with him and report Nehemiah's words to him, and Tobiah sends letters to put Nehemiah in fear.

Chapter 7 body

After the wall is finished and the doors, gatekeepers, singers, and Levites are in place, the narrator puts his brother Hanani and Hananiah, governor of the fortress, in charge of Jerusalem and gives orders about when the gates are opened and shut and how watches are kept. The city is wide and large but has few people and unbuilt houses, so God moves the narrator to gather the nobles, rulers, and people to be listed by genealogy. He finds the book of the genealogy of those who first came up from Babylonian captivity with Zerubbabel, Jeshua, and others, and the chapter reproduces its lists by family and town, then priests, Levites, singers, gatekeepers, temple servants, and Solomon's servants. Some families, including three priestly houses, could not prove their ancestry and the priests among them were disqualified from the priesthood until a priest could consult Urim and Thummim. The assembly totals forty-two thousand three hundred sixty, plus servants, singers, and animals, and the governor, heads of households, and the rest of the people give gold, silver, basins, and priests' garments to the work before everyone settles in their cities in the seventh month.

Chapter 8 body

On the first day of the seventh month, all the people gathered in the wide place before the water gate and asked Ezra the scribe to bring the book of the law of Moses. Ezra read it from early morning until midday while the Levites helped the people understand it, and the people wept when they heard it. Nehemiah the governor, Ezra, and the Levites told them not to mourn because the day was holy and the joy of the LORD was their strength, so the people went to eat, drink, send portions, and celebrate. On the second day the heads of households, priests, and Levites studied the law with Ezra and found the command to dwell in booths during the feast of the seventh month. The people then built temporary shelters in many places and lived in them with very great gladness, as had not been done since the days of Joshua, and the reading continued daily through the seven-day feast and the solemn assembly on the eighth day.

Chapter 9 body

On the twenty-fourth day of the month, the children of Israel gather with fasting, sackcloth, and dirt on them, separate themselves from all foreigners, and confess their sins and the iniquities of their fathers. They read the book of the law for a fourth of the day and confess and worship for another fourth. Levites including Jeshua, Bani, and Kadmiel cry out to the LORD and lead a long prayer that blesses him as creator, recalls his choosing of Abram, his rescue of the people from Egypt, his giving of the law at Sinai, and his provision in the wilderness and in the land. The prayer recounts the repeated pride, rebellion, and disobedience of the fathers, and the LORD's repeated mercy, deliverance, and refusal to make a full end of them. It ends with the confession that the people are servants in the land, under kings set over them because of their sins, and that they make a sure covenant, write it, and have their princes, Levites, and priests seal it.

Chapter 10 body

Chapter 10 lists those who sealed the covenant: Nehemiah the governor, a group of priests, the Levites, and the chiefs of the people. The rest of the people, including priests, Levites, gatekeepers, singers, temple servants, and their families, joined them in a curse and an oath to walk in God's law given by Moses. They pledged not to intermarry with the peoples of the land, not to buy goods on the Sabbath or holy days, and to forego the seventh year crops and the exaction of every debt. They also committed to a yearly third-of-a-shekel charge for the temple service and to supplying wood, first fruits, firstborn, and tithes. The chapter ends with the resolve, 'We will not forsake the house of our God.'

Chapter 11 body

Chapter 11 describes who settled in Jerusalem, the holy city. The princes of the people lived there, and the rest of the people cast lots so that one in ten would dwell in Jerusalem while nine parts stayed in the other cities; the people blessed those who volunteered. The chapter lists the chiefs from Judah (including Athaiah and Maaseiah) and Benjamin (including Sallu), the priests, the Levites, the gatekeepers, and the singers, with their numbers and overseers. The temple servants lived in Ophel, and the rest of Israel, the priests, and the Levites lived in their own cities. Finally the chapter names the villages where the children of Judah lived from Beersheba to the valley of Hinnom and where the children of Benjamin lived from Geba onward, noting that some Levite divisions of Judah settled in Benjamin's territory.

Chapter 12 body

Chapter 12 lists the priests and Levites who returned with Zerubbabel and Jeshua, the high priestly line from Jeshua to Jaddua, and the heads of priestly households in the days of Joiakim. It records the Levite chiefs, singers, and gatekeepers who served according to David's commandment. The narrator (Nehemiah) then describes the dedication of Jerusalem's wall, for which Levites and singers were gathered, and the priests, Levites, people, gates, and wall were purified. Two great thanksgiving companies processed along the wall in opposite directions and met at God's house, where sacrifices were offered and the joy of Jerusalem was heard far away. The chapter ends with men appointed over the storerooms for offerings and tithes and a note that the portions for singers, gatekeepers, and Levites were given as each day required.

Chapter 13 body

Nehemiah 13 recounts reforms Nehemiah carried out after returning to Jerusalem from the king. The reading of the law led the people to separate the mixed multitude from Israel, and Nehemiah found that the priest Eliashib had given Tobiah a great room in the temple courts, which he cleared and restored to its storage use. He restored the Levites' neglected portions and appointed treasurers, stopped Sabbath trading by Judeans and Tyrian merchants by shutting the gates, and confronted men who had married foreign women, including a grandson of Eliashib who was son-in-law to Sanballat. Nehemiah repeatedly asks God to remember him for these acts.
