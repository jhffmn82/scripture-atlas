Abstract written by a model

This document is the First Letter to Timothy, a pastoral epistle in six chapters in which Paul, an apostle, instructs Timothy, his true child in the faith, on how to guard sound teaching and order the community at Ephesus. The most important figures are Paul, Timothy, and Christ Jesus, with Hymenaeus and Alexander appearing as cautionary examples. Chapter 1 opens with greetings and Paul's charge that Timothy stay at Ephesus and command certain men not to teach a different doctrine or follow myths and endless genealogies. The goal of the command is love from a pure heart, a good conscience, and sincere faith. Paul thanks Christ for appointing him despite his past as a blasphemer and persecutor, calling himself the chief of sinners who received mercy. He urges Timothy to wage the good warfare and names Hymenaeus and Alexander, who shipwrecked their faith. Chapter 2 turns to worship: prayers should be made for all people, including kings, because God desires all to be saved and Christ Jesus is the one mediator who gave himself as a ransom for all. Men are told to pray without anger, women to dress modestly, and the author does not permit a woman to teach or exercise authority over a man. Chapter 3 lists qualifications for overseers and servants, including blamelessness, self-control, good household management, and testing before service. It describes the assembly as God's house and ends by declaring the mystery of godliness great: God revealed in the flesh and received up in glory. Chapter 4 warns that in later times some will fall away, following doctrines of demons that forbid marriage and certain foods. The writer insists that all creation is good when received with thanksgiving, and urges Timothy to refuse profane fables, train in godliness, be an example despite his youth, attend to reading, exhortation, and teaching, and watch himself and his teaching. Chapter 5 gives guidance for treating older and younger men and women, with extended rules on widows, including who should be enrolled and the duty of families to support their own. It also covers honoring elders, requiring two or three witnesses for accusations, avoiding prejudice and hasty laying on of hands, and using a little wine for the stomach. Chapter 6 addresses bondservants, condemns those who teach differently and treat godliness as gain, and says godliness with contentment is great gain while love of money is a root of all kinds of evil. Timothy is told to flee these things, pursue righteousness, fight the good fight, and keep the commandment until Christ appears. Rich people are charged to hope in God and share, and Timothy is told to guard what was entrusted to him and avoid empty chatter.

1 Timothy / Chapter 1 body

Paul, an apostle, writes to Timothy, his true child in the faith, greeting him with grace, mercy, and peace. He reminds Timothy to stay at Ephesus and command certain men not to teach a different doctrine or follow myths and endless genealogies. Paul says the goal of the command is love from a pure heart, a good conscience, and sincere faith, and that some have turned to vain talking as would-be teachers of the law, which is good when used lawfully. Paul thanks Christ Jesus for appointing him to service despite his past as a blasphemer and persecutor, calling himself the chief of sinners who obtained mercy as an example to future believers. He charges Timothy to wage the good warfare, holding faith and a good conscience, and names Hymenaeus and Alexander as men who shipwrecked their faith and whom he delivered to Satan so they might learn not to blaspheme.

Chapter 2 body

The author urges that petitions, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all people, including kings and those in high places, so that believers may lead tranquil and godly lives. He grounds this in God our Savior, who desires all people to be saved and to know the truth, and in the one God and one mediator, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all. The author states he was appointed preacher, apostle, and teacher of the Gentiles. He then instructs men to pray everywhere with holy hands, without anger and doubting, and women to dress modestly and adorn themselves with good works. Finally, he says he does not permit a woman to teach or exercise authority over a man, citing Adam being formed first and Eve being deceived, and says the woman will be saved through childbearing if they continue in faith, love, and holiness with sobriety.

Chapter 3 body

Chapter 3 sets out the qualifications for two groups of church leaders: the overseer and the servants. The overseer must be without reproach, faithful to one wife, self-controlled, a good teacher, gentle, not greedy, and one who rules his own household well; he must not be a new convert and must have a good reputation among outsiders. Servants must likewise be reverent, honest, not addicted to wine or greedy for money, hold the faith with a pure conscience, and be tested first, and their wives must also be reverent and faithful. The writer says he hopes to come soon, but is writing so the reader will know how people ought to behave in God's house, which is the assembly of the living God. The chapter closes by declaring the mystery of godliness great: God was revealed in the flesh and received up in glory.

Chapter 4 body

The chapter opens with the Spirit's warning that in later times some will fall away from the faith, following seducing spirits and doctrines of demons spread by hypocritical liars who forbid marriage and command abstinence from foods. The writer counters that everything God created is good and sanctified by God's word and prayer when received with thanksgiving. He then urges the addressee to instruct the brothers, refuse profane fables, and exercise himself toward godliness, which holds promise for this life and the next. The addressee is told to be an example to believers despite his youth, to attend to reading, exhortation, and teaching, and not to neglect the gift given him by prophecy and the laying on of the elders' hands. The chapter closes by urging him to watch himself and his teaching, since doing so will save both himself and his hearers.

Chapter 5 body

Chapter 5 gives instructions on how to treat different groups within the community. It opens with how to address older and younger men and women, then spends most of its length on widows: who counts as a widow indeed, who should be enrolled (over sixty, wife of one man, known for good works), why younger widows should be refused enrollment and encouraged to marry, and the duty of families to support their own widows so the assembly is not burdened. It then turns to elders, who are to be counted worthy of double honor, protected from accusations without two or three witnesses, and publicly reproved if they sin. The chapter closes with commands to act without prejudice, not to lay hands hastily on anyone, to keep oneself pure, to use a little wine for the stomach and frequent infirmities, and a note that sins and good works eventually become evident.

Chapter 6 body

Chapter 6 gives instructions to Timothy about how different groups should live and what he must guard. Bondservants are told to honor their masters, including believing masters, so that God's name and the doctrine are not blasphemed. Those who teach a different doctrine, being conceited and quarrelsome and treating godliness as a means of gain, are to be avoided, while godliness with contentment is called great gain and the love of money is named a root of all kinds of evil. Timothy is told to flee these things, pursue righteousness and the other virtues, fight the good fight, and keep the commandment blameless until the appearing of Jesus Christ, whom God will show at the right time. Rich people are to be charged not to be arrogant or trust in riches but to hope in God, do good, and share, and Timothy is finally told to guard what was entrusted to him and avoid empty chatter and falsely called knowledge.
