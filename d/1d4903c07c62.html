Paul’s Letter to the Romans

author unknown · 17 sections
Paul’s Letter to the Romans, from the World English Bible, Catholic Edition, dedicated to the public domain by eBible.org. Shown here as plain text, with facts drawn from it. https://ebible.org/eng-web-c/

Abstract written by a model

This document is a letter from Paul, an apostle and servant of Jesus Christ, to the believers in Rome, set out in sixteen chapters. It argues that salvation and righteousness come by faith in Jesus Christ, for Jew and Greek alike, and then draws out how believers should live. Paul opens by greeting the Romans, expressing longing to visit, and declaring he is not ashamed of the Good News, the power of God for salvation. He shows humanity under God's wrath for suppressing the truth (ch. 1), then argues that judging others is inexcusable and that Jews and Gentiles will be judged impartially, with a true Jew being one inwardly (ch. 2). Both are under sin, and all are justified freely by grace through redemption in Christ, not by works of the law (ch. 3). Abraham is the model, counted righteous by faith before circumcision (ch. 4). Justified believers have peace with God, and Adam's trespass is contrasted with Christ's act of righteousness, where grace abounds more (ch. 5). Paul rejects continuing in sin so grace may increase: believers, baptized into Christ's death, are dead to sin and alive to God, since sin's wages are death but God's gift is eternal life (ch. 6). Using a marriage analogy, he says believers have died to the law, which is holy yet exposes sin, and he describes an inner struggle and deliverance through Jesus Christ (ch. 7). Life in the Spirit brings no condemnation, sonship, hope amid suffering, and the assurance that nothing can separate believers from God's love in Christ (ch. 8). Paul then grieves for his kinsmen Israel, discussing God's election, mercy, and a saved remnant, while Gentiles attained righteousness by faith (ch. 9). He prays for Israel's salvation, saying Christ fulfills the law and that confessing Jesus as Lord and believing in the resurrection saves everyone who calls on him (ch. 10). God has not rejected Israel; using the olive tree image, he warns Gentiles against boasting and says all Israel will be saved, praising God's wisdom (ch. 11). The closing chapters turn practical: present bodies as a living sacrifice, use differing gifts in one body, love sincerely, and overcome evil with good (ch. 12). He instructs submission to authorities, paying what is owed, and love as fulfilling the law (ch. 13), and urges acceptance of the weak in faith over food and days without judging (ch. 14). Paul asks the strong to bear with the weak, describes his ministry to the Gentiles, and announces his trip to Jerusalem with a contribution and his plan to visit Rome on the way to Spain (ch. 15). The letter ends by commending Phoebe, greeting many named individuals including Prisca, Aquila, Andronicus, and Junia, warning against divisive people, and passing on greetings from Timothy, Tertius (who wrote the letter), and others, with a final blessing of grace (ch. 16).

Paul’s Letter to the Romans front matter

Chapter 1 body

Paul, a servant of Jesus Christ and an apostle set apart for the Good News of God, greets the believers in Rome, identifying Jesus as David's offspring according to the flesh and as Son of God declared with power by the resurrection. He thanks God that the Romans' faith is proclaimed throughout the world, says he prays for them constantly and longs to visit them to impart a spiritual gift and be mutually encouraged. He explains that he has often planned to come but been hindered, and says he is a debtor to Greeks and foreigners, wise and foolish alike, and eager to preach in Rome. He declares he is not ashamed of the Good News, the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, in which God's righteousness is revealed from faith to faith. He then states that God's wrath is revealed against people who suppress the truth, who knew God through creation yet worshiped images and creatures, and whom God gave up to uncleanness, same-sex passions, a reprobate mind, and a long list of vices.

Chapter 2 body

Chapter 2 addresses the man who judges others, saying he is without excuse because he practices the same things and will not escape God's judgment. God's goodness is meant to lead to repentance, and God will pay back everyone according to their works, with wrath for the self-seeking and glory, honor, and peace for those who do good, Jew first and also Greek. Because God shows no partiality, those who sinned without the law perish without it and those under the law are judged by it, while Gentiles who do the law's things by nature are a law to themselves through conscience. The text then turns to the Jew who bears the name, rests on the law, and teaches others, but who steals, commits adultery, and dishonors God by disobeying the law, so that God's name is blasphemed among the Gentiles. It concludes that circumcision profits only for a doer of the law, and that a true Jew is one inwardly, with circumcision of the heart, in the spirit, whose praise is from God.

Chapter 3 body

Chapter 3 opens with an imagined objector asking what advantage the Jew has, and answers that the Jews were entrusted with God's revelations, and that some Jews' lack of faith does not nullify God's faithfulness. The text rejects the argument that human unrighteousness or lies, by showing God's righteousness, should excuse sinners, since God must judge the world. It declares that Jews and Greeks alike are under sin, supported by a chain of quoted scriptures saying no one is righteous. The law speaks to those under it so that every mouth is closed, and no one is justified by works of the law, which only brings knowledge of sin. Apart from the law, God's righteousness has been revealed through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe, since all have sinned; they are justified freely by grace through the redemption in Christ, whom God sent as an atoning sacrifice. Boasting is excluded, God justifies both circumcised and uncircumcised by faith, and faith establishes the law rather than nullifying it.

Chapter 4 body

Chapter 4 takes up what Abraham, called our forefather, found according to the flesh, and argues that he was counted righteous by faith and not by works. It cites Scripture ("Abraham believed God") and David's blessing on the one whom God counts righteous apart from works. It argues that this righteousness was credited before Abraham was circumcised, so he is father both of the uncircumcised who believe and of the circumcised who walk in his faith. The promise that he would be heir of the world came through the righteousness of faith, not the law, so that it would be by grace and sure to all his offspring. The chapter ends with Abraham's unwavering faith in God, who gives life to the dead, and says that righteousness will likewise be accounted to those who believe in him who raised Jesus our Lord, who was delivered up for trespasses and raised for justification.

Chapter 5 body

Chapter 5 says that believers, justified by faith, have peace with God through Jesus Christ and rejoice in hope and even in suffering, which leads through perseverance and proven character to hope. It shows God's love in Christ dying for the weak, ungodly, sinners and enemies, so that believers are reconciled and will be saved from God's wrath by his life. It then contrasts Adam, through whose trespass sin and death entered and reigned over all, with Jesus Christ, whose one act of righteousness and obedience brings abundant grace, the gift of righteousness, and life. The law came in so that the trespass might abound, but grace abounded more, to reign through righteousness to eternal life.

Chapter 6 body

The chapter answers the question of whether believers should continue in sin so that grace may increase, and it answers with a firm no. It teaches that those baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death, buried with him, and will share his resurrection, so they should walk in newness of life. Christ, raised from the dead, dies no more, and believers are told to consider themselves dead to sin and alive to God. The writer urges them not to let sin reign or to present their members to sin, but to present them to God as instruments of righteousness. He contrasts being servants of sin, which ends in death, with being servants of righteousness and of God, which brings sanctification and eternal life, and he closes by saying that the wages of sin is death while God's free gift is eternal life in Christ Jesus.

Chapter 7 body

The speaker addresses brothers who know the law, using the marriage analogy of a woman bound to her husband only while he lives to show that believers have died to the law through the body of Christ and are joined to him who was raised from the dead. He then asks whether the law is sin, answering that it is not, since the law revealed sin and coveting, while sin used the commandment to produce coveting, deceive, and kill him. The law is declared holy, righteous, and good, and sin is shown to be exceedingly sinful through it. The narrator describes an inner struggle in which he does what he hates and fails to do what he desires, attributing this to sin dwelling in his flesh and a law in his members warring against the law of his mind. He cries out for deliverance from the body of death, thanks God through Jesus Christ, and concludes that with his mind he serves God's law but with his flesh sin's law.

Chapter 8 body

This chapter declares that those in Christ Jesus face no condemnation, because the law of the Spirit of life has freed them from the law of sin and death, which God accomplished by sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh. It contrasts living according to the flesh, which brings death and hostility toward God, with living according to the Spirit, which brings life and peace. Those led by the Spirit are children of God and heirs with Christ, though they suffer now and wait with hope for adoption and the redemption of their bodies. The creation, subjected to vanity in hope, groans together with believers and awaits deliverance into the liberty of the glory of the children of God, while the Spirit helps believers' weakness by interceding for them. The chapter closes by affirming that God works all things for good for those who love him, that he foreknew, predestined, called, justified and glorified them, and that nothing can separate them from God's love in Christ Jesus.

Chapter 9 body

The speaker, Paul, declares in Christ that he feels great sorrow for his kinsmen the Israelites, even wishing himself accursed for their sake, and lists their privileges, including Christ coming from them according to the flesh. He argues that God's word has not failed, because not all who descend from Israel or Abraham are true children; the line runs through Isaac and Jacob by promise and election, not works. He answers the charge of unrighteousness by pointing to God's mercy toward Moses, the hardening of Pharaoh, and the potter's right over the clay, and says God endured vessels of wrath while making known the riches of his glory on vessels of mercy called from Jews and Gentiles. Citing Hosea and Isaiah, he shows that a remnant of Israel will be saved, and he concludes that Gentiles attained righteousness by faith while Israel, pursuing the law by works, stumbled over the stumbling stone laid in Zion.

Chapter 10 body

The speaker, addressing his brothers, expresses his heart's desire and prayer that Israel be saved, testifying that they have zeal for God but without knowledge. He says they sought to establish their own righteousness and did not submit to God's righteousness, while Christ is the fulfillment of the law for everyone who believes. Contrasting Moses' righteousness of the law with the righteousness of faith, he says the word is near, in the mouth and heart, and that confessing Jesus as Lord and believing God raised him from the dead brings salvation. He states there is no distinction between Jew and Greek, since the same Lord is rich to all who call on him, and then traces the sequence of calling, believing, hearing, preaching, and being sent. He concludes that although the message went out to all the earth, Israel did not all listen and remained a disobedient and contrary people, while those who did not seek God found him.

Chapter 11 body

The writer, who identifies himself as an Israelite of the tribe of Benjamin and an apostle to the Gentiles, argues that God has not rejected his people Israel. He points to a remnant chosen by grace, as in the days of Elijah, while the rest were hardened. Israel's fall has brought salvation to the Gentiles, and this is meant to provoke Israel to jealousy. Using the image of an olive tree, he warns Gentile believers, grafted in as a wild olive, not to boast over the broken-off natural branches, since God can graft them in again. He declares that a partial hardening has come on Israel until the fullness of the Gentiles comes in, that all Israel will be saved, and that God's gifts and calling are irrevocable, ending with praise of God's wisdom.

Chapter 12 body

The writer urges the brothers, by the mercies of God, to present their bodies as a living sacrifice and to be transformed rather than conformed to this world, so they may prove God's will. He tells everyone not to think too highly of themselves, since God has apportioned each a measure of faith. Believers are described as many members forming one body in Christ, each with differing gifts such as prophecy, service, teaching, exhorting, giving, ruling, and showing mercy, to be exercised faithfully. The chapter then lists practical commands: love without hypocrisy, hating evil and clinging to good, honoring one another, enduring troubles, showing hospitality, blessing persecutors, and rejoicing and weeping with others. It closes by forbidding revenge, leaving vengeance to God, calling for feeding one's enemy, and urging believers to overcome evil with good.

Chapter 13 body

Chapter 13 instructs the readers to be in subjection to the higher authorities, since all authority is from God and resisting it means withstanding God's ordinance and receiving judgment. Rulers are described as God's servants who are a terror to evil but bring praise for good, bearing the sword against wrongdoers, so the readers are to submit for conscience' sake as well as because of wrath. The readers are told to pay taxes, customs, respect, and honor to whom they are owed. They are to owe no one anything except love, which fulfills the law because it sums up the commandments against adultery, murder, stealing, and coveting and does no harm to a neighbor. The chapter closes with a call to awaken because salvation is nearer, to throw off deeds of darkness, walk properly as in the day, and put on the Lord Jesus Christ without making provision for the flesh.

Chapter 14 body

Chapter 14 instructs believers to accept the one who is weak in faith without quarreling over opinions, specifically about eating only vegetables and observing certain days. Those who eat and those who abstain are told not to despise or judge each other, since each answers to the Lord, and all will stand before Christ's judgment seat and give account to God. The text states that nothing is unclean of itself, yet believers must not grieve or cause a brother to stumble by their food, and should pursue peace and mutual building up because God's Kingdom is righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit. Whoever eats with doubt is condemned because it is not of faith. The chapter closes with a doxology to the only wise God through Jesus Christ, who establishes believers according to the Good News and the revealed mystery.

Chapter 15 body

In Romans 15, Paul urges the strong among the Roman believers to bear with the weak, please their neighbors, and accept one another as Christ accepted them, citing Christ's example and the Scriptures. He explains that Christ became a servant of the circumcision to confirm the promises to the fathers so that the Gentiles might glorify God, supporting this with quotations from Scripture. Paul describes his own ministry as a priestly servant of Christ Jesus to the Gentiles, having fully preached the Good News from Jerusalem around to Illyricum and aiming to preach where Christ was not yet named. He announces that he is going to Jerusalem with a contribution from Macedonia and Achaia for the poor saints, and afterward plans to travel by way of the Romans to Spain. He asks the Romans to strive with him in prayer for deliverance from the disobedient in Judea and for his service to be acceptable, and closes with a blessing of peace.

Chapter 16 body

The chapter opens with Paul commending Phoebe, a servant of the assembly at Cenchreae, and asking the recipients to receive and assist her. He then sends a long series of greetings to named individuals and households, including Prisca and Aquila, who risked their necks for his life, and Andronicus and Junia, who are notable among the apostles. Paul urges the brothers to watch out for and turn away from those who cause divisions and stumbling, saying they serve their own belly and deceive the innocent. He promises that the God of peace will quickly crush Satan under their feet. The chapter closes with greetings from Timothy, Lucius, Jason, Sosipater, Tertius (who wrote the letter), Gaius, Erastus and Quartus, and a final blessing of grace.
