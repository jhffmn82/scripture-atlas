Deuteronomy

author unknown · 624 BC · 35 sections
Deuteronomy, from the World English Bible, Catholic Edition, dedicated to the public domain by eBible.org. Shown here as plain text, with facts drawn from it. https://ebible.org/eng-web-c/

Abstract written by a model

This document is the book of Deuteronomy, a series of farewell addresses by Moses to all Israel in Moab, east of the Jordan, in the fortieth year, just before the people enter the land the LORD swore to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Its central figures are the LORD, Moses, the people of Israel, and Joshua, who is named as Moses' successor. It opens with Moses' historical review: the command at Horeb, the appointment of judges, the refusal at Kadesh Barnea and the resulting forty years of wandering, and the victories over Sihon and Og, whose land was given to Reubenites, Gadites, and half of Manasseh. Moses is barred from crossing the Jordan and may only view the land from Pisgah. He then urges obedience to the statutes and ordinances, warns against idols, and recites the ten commandments given at Horeb. He calls Israel to love the LORD with all heart, soul, and might, to teach the words to their children, and to remember the LORD's deliverance from Egypt. He warns against forgetting the LORD in prosperity, recalls the golden calf and the new tablets, and instructs Israel to destroy the Canaanite nations and their worship sites. A long law code follows. It covers worship at one chosen place, enticement to idolatry, clean and unclean food, tithes, debts, slaves, festivals, judges, kings, priests, prophets, cities of refuge, warfare, and many matters of family, sexual conduct, property, and justice for the vulnerable. First fruits and tithes are accompanied by confessions of the LORD's deliverance. Moses commands that the law be inscribed on stones at Mount Ebal, with blessing declared from Gerizim and curses from Ebal. A long list of blessings for obedience and curses for disobedience follows, ending in scattering and exile. A covenant is made in Moab, and Moses promises restoration if Israel returns, setting before them the choice of life or death. Moses, aged 120, commissions Joshua, has the law written and placed beside the ark, and teaches Israel a song warning of their future rebellion and the LORD's vengeance. He blesses the tribes one by one. He then climbs Mount Nebo, sees the land, and dies in Moab. He is buried in an unknown place, Israel mourns thirty days, Joshua leads them, and no prophet since has equaled Moses.

Deuteronomy front matter

Chapter 1 body

In the fortieth year, beyond the Jordan in Moab, Moses begins to declare the law to all Israel, recalling the LORD's command at Horeb to leave and possess the land sworn to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. He recounts appointing wise, respected men as heads, captains, and judges over the tribes, and charging the judges to judge impartially. At Kadesh Barnea, after twelve men spied out the land and reported it good, Israel refused to go up, murmuring that the LORD hated them and fearing the Amorites and Anakim. The LORD swore that the evil generation would not see the land except Caleb, barred Moses too, and named Joshua and the little ones as those who would enter. Israel then presumed to attack against the LORD's warning, was routed by the Amorites as far as Hormah, wept without being heard, and stayed many days in Kadesh.

Chapter 2 body

Speaking in the first person, the narrator recounts how Israel, after circling Mount Seir for many days, was told by the LORD to turn north and pass peacefully through the lands of Esau's descendants, Moab, and Ammon, because the LORD had given those lands to them. The text notes that earlier peoples such as the Emim, Horites, Zamzummim, and Avvim were displaced from these lands. After thirty-eight years from Kadesh Barnea, the generation of men of war had died out, and the LORD commanded Israel to cross the Arnon and begin possessing the land of Sihon the Amorite, king of Heshbon. Sihon refused passage after Israel offered words of peace, because the LORD hardened his spirit; he fought Israel at Jahaz and was defeated, and Israel destroyed his cities and took the livestock and plunder. Israel did not approach the land of Ammon, as the LORD had forbidden.

Chapter 3 body

In Deuteronomy 3, the speaker (who is addressed by the LORD as "me" and later pleads to enter the land, i.e. Moses) recounts how Israel defeated Og, king of Bashan, at Edrei after the LORD told them not to fear. They took all sixty fortified cities of Argob, utterly destroyed the inhabitants, and kept the livestock and plunder. The conquered land east of the Jordan, from the Arnon to Mount Hermon, was distributed: the Reubenites and Gadites received Gilead's southern portion, and the half-tribe of Manasseh received the rest of Gilead and Bashan, with Jair and Machir named in the allotment. The men of valor of these tribes were commanded to cross the Jordan armed ahead of their brothers while their families stayed behind, and Joshua was told not to fear because the LORD fights for Israel. The speaker begged to cross the Jordan and see the good land, but the LORD refused, allowing him only to view it from Pisgah and commanding him to commission Joshua, while they stayed in the valley near Beth Peor.

Chapter 4 body

In Deuteronomy 4, Moses urges Israel to obey the statutes and ordinances he teaches so that they may live and possess the land the LORD gives them, and he warns them neither to add to nor take from the commandments. He recalls the LORD's judgment on those who followed Baal Peor and the day at Horeb when the LORD spoke from the fire without any visible form, which is the basis for forbidding carved images and the worship of the sun, moon, and stars. Moses tells them he will die east of the Jordan, warns that idolatry will bring scattering among the nations, yet promises that the merciful LORD will be found when they seek him with all their heart. He points to the LORD's unique acts in bringing Israel out of Egypt as proof that the LORD alone is God, and calls them to keep his commandments. Finally, Moses sets apart three cities beyond the Jordan for unintentional killers, and the chapter introduces the law he set before Israel in the valley opposite Beth Peor, in the land taken from Sihon and Og.

Chapter 5 body

Moses summons all Israel to hear the statutes and ordinances, reminding them that the LORD made his covenant at Horeb with those alive today, speaking face to face out of the fire while Moses stood between them because they were afraid. Moses recites the commandments the LORD spoke: no other gods, no carved images, no misuse of his name, keeping the Sabbath in remembrance of Egypt, honoring parents, and prohibitions against murder, adultery, stealing, false testimony, and coveting. The LORD wrote the words on two stone tablets and gave them to Moses. The people, terrified by the fire and voice, ask Moses to go near and hear the rest and tell them, promising to hear and do it. The LORD approves their words, longs for a heart in them to fear him, sends them back to their tents, and tells Moses to stay and receive the commandments, statutes, and ordinances to teach them for life in the land. Moses closes by urging Israel to walk in all the LORD's way without turning aside.

Chapter 6 body

In Chapter 6, Moses-style address to Israel presents the LORD's commandments, statutes, and ordinances to be kept in the land they are about to possess, so that they may fear the LORD and prosper. Israel is told to hear that the LORD is one and to love him with all their heart, soul, and might, keeping his words on their hearts, teaching them to their children, and binding and writing them on hands, foreheads, door posts, and gates. They are warned that when the LORD brings them into a land of cities, houses, cisterns, vineyards, and olive trees they did not build or plant, they must not forget him who brought them out of Egypt, must not follow other gods, and must not tempt him as at Massah. When their son asks the meaning of the laws, they are to tell him of slavery under Pharaoh in Egypt and the LORD's mighty deliverance, and that keeping all the commandments will be righteousness for them.

Chapter 7 body

Chapter 7 gives the Israelites instructions for entering the land, where the LORD your God will cast out seven nations greater and mightier than they: the Hittite, Girgashite, Amorite, Canaanite, Perizzite, Hivite, and Jebusite. The Israelites are told to utterly destroy these nations, make no covenant or marriages with them, and break down their altars, pillars, Asherah poles, and engraved images, lest they be led to serve other gods. The text grounds this in Israel's status as a holy people chosen not for its size but because the LORD loves them and keeps the oath sworn to their fathers. Obedience to the commandments, statutes, and ordinances is promised to bring blessings of fertility, abundant produce and livestock, and freedom from sickness, while those who hate the LORD are repaid. The Israelites are told not to fear the larger nations, to remember what the LORD did to Pharaoh and Egypt, and to expect the nations to be driven out little by little, and they must not covet the silver and gold on the idols lest they become a devoted thing.

Chapter 8 body

In Chapter 8, the speaker exhorts the Israelites to keep all the commandments so that they may live, multiply, and possess the land the LORD swore to their fathers. He reminds them that the LORD led them forty years in the wilderness to humble and test them, feeding them with manna and keeping their clothing and feet from wearing out, as a father disciplines a son. He describes the good land they are entering, rich in water, grain, fruit, olive trees, honey, iron, and copper. He warns that prosperity could lead them to forget the LORD and claim their wealth as the work of their own power. He declares that the LORD gives the power to get wealth, and that if they forget him and serve other gods, they will perish like the nations the LORD destroys before them.

Chapter 9 body

Moses addresses Israel as they are about to cross the Jordan to dispossess great, fortified nations including the tall sons of Anak. He tells them the LORD will go before them as a devouring fire, but that they are receiving the land because of the nations' wickedness and the LORD's oath to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, not because of their own righteousness, for they are stiff-necked. Moses recounts Israel's rebellion at Horeb: while he was on the mountain forty days and nights receiving the stone tablets, the people made a molten calf, the LORD threatened to destroy them, and Moses broke the tablets, burned and ground the calf, and threw its dust into the brook. He recalls falling down before the LORD another forty days and nights, praying for Israel and Aaron, and lists further provocations at Taberah, Massah, Kibroth Hattaavah, and Kadesh Barnea. His prayer asks the LORD to remember Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and not destroy the people who are his inheritance.

Chapter 10 body

In Deuteronomy 10, Moses recounts how the LORD told him to cut two new stone tablets like the first and make an ark of wood. He went up the mountain, the LORD rewrote the ten commandments on the tablets, and Moses placed them in the ark he had made of acacia wood. A parenthetical aside notes Israel's journeys, Aaron's death at Moserah with Eleazar succeeding him as priest, and the LORD's setting apart of the tribe of Levi to carry the ark and minister, with the LORD as their inheritance. Moses says he stayed forty days and nights on the mountain, the LORD listened and would not destroy the people, and told him to lead them to possess the promised land. Moses then asks what the LORD requires of Israel: fear, obedience, love, and wholehearted service, grounded in the LORD's ownership of heaven and earth, his choice of Israel's fathers, his justice and love for the foreigner, and the growth of Israel from seventy persons to a multitude.

Chapter 11 body

The speaker urges the Israelites to love the LORD their God and keep all his commandments, reminding them that they themselves saw his works against Pharaoh and Egypt, the Red Sea, the wilderness years, and the judgment on Dathan and Abiram. He contrasts the land they are about to possess, a land of hills and valleys watered by rain and under the LORD's constant care, with Egypt's irrigated gardens. Obedience will bring rain, produce, and victory over greater nations, with borders reaching from the wilderness and Lebanon to the Euphrates and the western sea, while turning to other gods will bring drought and quick perishing from the land. The Israelites are told to lay up the words in heart and soul, bind them on hand and forehead, teach them to their children, and write them on doorposts and gates. Finally, a blessing and a curse are set before them, to be placed on Mount Gerizim and Mount Ebal once they cross the Jordan and possess the land.

Chapter 12 body

In Chapter 12, the speaker instructs the Israelites on how to live in the land the LORD is giving them. They must utterly destroy the nations' worship sites, altars, pillars, Asherah poles and images, but must not worship the LORD in that way. Offerings, tithes, vows and sacrifices are to be brought only to the one place the LORD chooses, where the people and the Levite rejoice before him. Meat may be killed and eaten within their own gates, including when the chosen place is too far away, but the blood must never be eaten and must be poured out on the earth. The chapter ends with warnings not to imitate the nations' worship, which includes burning sons and daughters in fire, and not to add to or take away from the commands.

Chapter 13 body

Chapter 13 gives three laws about enticement to worship other gods. A prophet or dreamer of dreams who urges following other gods must not be heeded even if his sign comes true, because the LORD your God is testing Israel's love, and that prophet is to be put to death. A close relative or friend who secretly entices someone to serve other gods must not be pitied, spared, or concealed, and is to be stoned, with the hearer's hand first and then all the people's. If a city is reported to have been drawn away by wicked fellows, the people must investigate diligently, and if it is true they must strike down its inhabitants and livestock, burn the city with its plunder, and leave it a permanent heap. The chapter ends by promising that the LORD will turn from his anger and show mercy and multiplication if Israel keeps his commandments and takes nothing of the devoted thing.

Chapter 14 body

Chapter 14 addresses the Israelites, called children of the LORD their God and a holy people chosen as his own possession, and forbids them to cut themselves or shave their foreheads for the dead. It lists which animals, fish, and birds may be eaten and which are unclean, including the camel, hare, rabbit, pig, creatures without fins and scales, and a long list of birds. It forbids eating anything that dies of itself, though it may be given or sold to a foreigner, and forbids boiling a young goat in its mother's milk. The chapter then commands tithing the yearly increase, to be eaten before the LORD in the place he chooses, or exchanged for money if the way is too far. Every third year the tithe is stored within the gates for the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow, so that the LORD may bless the work of the Israelites' hands.

Chapter 15 body

Chapter 15 gives Israelites commands from the LORD their God about debts, poverty, slaves, and firstborn animals. Every seventh year creditors must cancel debts owed by fellow Israelites, though they may still collect from a foreigner. Israelites are told to lend generously to a poor brother without a wicked or grieving heart, because the poor will never cease from the land. A Hebrew servant who has served six years must be freed in the seventh year and furnished liberally from flock, threshing floor, and wine press, remembering Israel's slavery in Egypt; a servant who loves his master may instead have his ear pierced with an awl and serve forever. Firstborn male animals are to be dedicated to the LORD and eaten yearly at the chosen place, unless defective, and their blood must be poured out on the ground.

Chapter 16 body

Chapter 16 gives the Israelites instructions for three annual festivals to be kept for the LORD their God at the place he will choose: Passover with the seven days of unleavened bread, the feast of weeks, and the feast of booths. It specifies how Passover is sacrificed and eaten, that all households including servants, Levites, foreigners, the fatherless, and widows rejoice, and that all males appear three times a year, each giving as able. The chapter then commands that judges and officers be appointed in every gate according to the tribes to judge with righteous judgment, without perverting justice, showing partiality, or taking bribes. It ends by forbidding an Asherah beside the LORD's altar and any sacred stone, which he hates.

Chapter 17 body

Chapter 17 sets out rules for worship, justice, and kingship in Israel. Sacrifices of defective oxen or sheep are forbidden as an abomination to the LORD. Anyone, man or woman, who serves other gods or the sun, moon, or stars must be diligently investigated and, if the charge is certain, stoned at the gates on the testimony of two or three witnesses, with the witnesses casting the first stones. Cases too hard for local judgment are to be taken to the Levitical priests and the judge at the place the LORD chooses, and their verdict must be followed without turning right or left, on pain of death. When the people later ask for a king, they must set over them the one the LORD chooses, from among their brothers, who must not multiply horses, wives, silver, or gold, and must write and read his own copy of the law all his life.

Chapter 18 body

Chapter 18 describes the provision for the priests and Levites, who have no tribal inheritance because the LORD himself is their inheritance, and lists the portions the people must give them. It also allows any Levite who comes to the chosen place to minister there and receive equal portions. The chapter then forbids Israel from imitating the abominations of the nations in the land, naming practices such as passing children through fire, divination, sorcery, and necromancy, which are the reason the LORD drives those nations out. It promises that the LORD will raise up a prophet like the speaker from among Israel's brothers, in answer to the people's request at Horeb not to hear the LORD's voice or see the fire again. Finally, it sets the penalty of death for a prophet who speaks presumptuously or in the name of other gods, and gives a test: a prophecy that does not come to pass was not spoken by the LORD.

Chapter 19 body

Chapter 19 sets out laws for Israel once the LORD your God gives them the land. Three cities of refuge are to be set apart so that a man slayer who killed a neighbor unintentionally, with no prior hatred, can flee there and live, safe from the avenger of blood; three more are to be added if the border is enlarged. A man who hates his neighbor, lies in wait, and kills him is to be taken from the city by the elders and handed to the avenger of blood to die. The chapter also forbids moving a neighbor's landmark, requires two or three witnesses to establish a matter, and prescribes that a false witness suffer the penalty he intended for his brother, following the principle of life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot.

Chapter 20 body

Chapter 20 gives Israel rules for warfare. Before battle the priest tells the people not to fear because the LORD their God, who brought them out of Egypt, goes with them to fight for them. The officers then exempt men who have built an undedicated house, planted a vineyard not yet used, pledged to marry a woman not yet taken, or who are fearful and faint-hearted, and afterward appoint army captains. Distant cities must first be offered peace: if they accept they become forced laborers, and if they refuse they are besieged, their males killed, and women, children, livestock and plunder taken. The cities of the Hittites, Amorites, Canaanites, Perizzites, Hivites and Jebusites must be utterly destroyed so Israel is not taught their abominations, and during sieges fruit-bearing trees must be spared while non-food trees may be cut for bulwarks.

Chapter 21 body

Chapter 21 gives several laws for Israel in the land the LORD gives. When a slain man is found in a field and the killer is unknown, the elders of the nearest city must break the neck of an unworked heifer in an unplowed valley with running water, wash their hands over it, and declare their innocence so the blood is forgiven. A man who takes a beautiful captive woman as wife must let her shave her head, change her clothes, and mourn her parents a month, and if he no longer delights in her he must let her go free rather than sell her. A man with two wives must give the double portion to the firstborn son even if that son is the hated wife's. A stubborn, rebellious, gluttonous and drunken son is brought by his parents to the elders and stoned by the men of the city, and a man put to death and hanged on a tree must be buried the same day.

Chapter 22 body

Chapter 22 gives a series of commands about neighborly duty, purity, and sexual conduct. The addressee must return a brother's straying ox, sheep, donkey, garment or any lost item, and must help lift a fallen animal. Further rules cover cross-dressing, taking a hen with her young, a roof railing, mixed seed, mixed plowing, mixed cloth, and cloak fringes. The longest section handles a husband who falsely accuses his new wife of lacking virginity: her parents present the tokens before the elders, who punish a false accuser with chastisement, a hundred-shekel fine and a ban on divorce, while a proven charge means the young lady is stoned. The chapter ends with rulings on adultery, a betrothed virgin in the city versus the field, an unbetrothed virgin seized by a man, and a ban on taking one's father's wife.

Chapter 23 body

Chapter 23 gives laws for the Israelites on who may enter the LORD's assembly, excluding those emasculated by crushing or cutting, those born of a forbidden union, and Ammonites and Moabites, who are barred because they withheld bread and water and hired Balaam to curse Israel, while Edomites and Egyptians are not to be abhorred and their third-generation children may enter. It then sets rules for keeping the military camp holy, including removing uncleanness and burying excrement, because the LORD walks in the middle of the camp. It forbids returning an escaped servant to his master, forbids prostitution among Israelites and the use of prostitutes' wages in the LORD's house, and forbids lending to a brother on interest while permitting it to a foreigner. It closes by requiring that vows to the LORD be paid, and by allowing a person in a neighbor's vineyard or grain field to eat grapes or pluck ears by hand but not carry grapes away in a container or use a sickle.

Chapter 24 body

Chapter 24 gives laws on marriage, divorce, and justice toward the vulnerable. A man who finds something unseemly in his wife may give her a certificate of divorce, and if she remarries and is divorced or widowed, her first husband may not take her again, as that is an abomination to the LORD. A newly married man is exempt from the army and business for one year to cheer his wife. Other laws forbid taking a mill as a pledge, require returning a poor man's garment pledge by sundown, prohibit kidnapping and enslaving fellow Israelites (a death penalty), and command prompt daily payment of poor hired servants. The chapter ends by forbidding the perversion of justice for foreigners, the fatherless, and widows, and by leaving forgotten sheaves, olives, and grapes for them, recalling Israel's slavery in Egypt.

Chapter 25 body

Chapter 25 sets out several laws for Israel. Judges are to justify the righteous and condemn the wicked, and a wicked man sentenced to a beating may receive no more than forty stripes so that he is not degraded. The ox treading grain must not be muzzled. When a man dies without a son, his brother must marry the widow so the dead man's name is not blotted out of Israel; if the surviving brother refuses, the widow brings the matter to the elders, removes his sandal, and spits in his face, and his house is given a shameful name. The chapter also commands cutting off the hand of a woman who grabs a man's private parts while he fights her husband, forbids diverse weights and measures as an abomination to the LORD, and ends with the command to remember what Amalek did to the feeble rearmost Israelites and, once given rest, to blot out Amalek's memory.

Chapter 26 body

Chapter 26 instructs the Israelites, once they possess and dwell in the land the LORD gives them, to bring the first of the ground's fruit in a basket to the place the LORD chooses. There they give it to the priest, who sets it before the altar, and they recite a confession recounting their father the Syrian, the Egyptian oppression, and the LORD's deliverance and gift of a land flowing with milk and honey. They are to rejoice with the Levite and the foreigner. In the third year, the year of tithing, they give the tithe to the Levite, foreigner, fatherless, and widow, then declare before the LORD that they have obeyed and ask for his blessing. The chapter closes with the LORD commanding them to keep his statutes and ordinances, and with the mutual declaration that they are his people and he is their God, who will set them high above all nations.

Chapter 27 body

Moses and the elders of Israel command the people to set up large plastered stones on Mount Ebal after crossing the Jordan, inscribe all the words of the law on them, and build an altar of uncut stones for burnt and peace offerings. Moses and the Levitical priests then tell Israel to be silent and listen, declaring that today they have become the people of the LORD their God and must obey his commandments. Moses assigns six tribes (Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Joseph, Benjamin) to stand on Mount Gerizim for the blessing and six (Reuben, Gad, Asher, Zebulun, Dan, Naphtali) on Mount Ebal for the curse. The Levites are to declare a series of curses, covering secret idolatry, dishonoring parents, moving a neighbor's landmark, misleading the blind, withholding justice from the foreigner, fatherless and widow, various sexual offenses, secret murder, taking a bribe to kill the innocent, and failing to uphold the law, and all the people answer 'Amen' to each.

Chapter 28 body

Chapter 28 is a speech addressed to the Israelites, in which the LORD's commandments are tied to blessings for obedience and curses for disobedience. Verses 1-14 promise blessings in city, field, family, livestock, harvest, and battle, and say the people will be set high above all nations as head and not tail. Verses 15-57 give a much longer list of curses: confusion, disease, drought, defeat, loss of family and property, and domination by foreigners and by a fierce nation from far away that besieges their cities and drives them to eat their own children. Verses 58-68 close by warning of further plagues, the diseases of Egypt, a people reduced to few in number, scattering among all peoples where they serve other gods, constant fear, and a return to Egypt in ships to be offered as slaves with no buyer.

Chapter 29 body

Moses gathers all Israel in the land of Moab to enter a covenant with the LORD in addition to the one made at Horeb. He recalls the LORD's acts in Egypt, the forty years in the wilderness in which clothes and sandals did not wear out, and the defeat of Sihon and Og, whose land was given to the Reubenites, Gadites, and half-tribe of the Manassites. He stresses that the covenant and oath bind everyone present, from leaders to the foreigner who draws water, and also those not present, and he warns against any man, woman, family, or tribe turning to the gods of the nations. Anyone who hears the curse yet assures himself of peace will not be pardoned, and the LORD will blot out his name. Moses describes how future generations and nations will see the land made sulfur, salt, and burning like Sodom and Gomorrah, and will conclude that Israel abandoned the covenant and was uprooted; he ends by saying secret things belong to the LORD, while revealed things belong to Israel so they may do the law.

Chapter 30 body

In Chapter 30, the speaker promises that after the Israelites experience the blessing and the curse and take them to heart among the nations where the LORD has driven them, they can return to the LORD with all their heart and soul. The LORD will then release them from captivity, gather them from every distant place, bring them back to the land of their fathers, circumcise their hearts, and prosper them, while putting the curses on their enemies. The speaker says the commandment is not too hard or too distant, not in heaven or beyond the sea, but very near, in their mouth and heart. He sets before them life and prosperity against death and evil, and calls heaven and earth as witnesses. He urges them to choose life by loving the LORD, obeying his voice, and clinging to him, so that they may dwell in the land sworn to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

Chapter 31 body

Moses, aged 120, tells all Israel he can no longer lead and that the LORD will go before them and give them victory, with Joshua as their leader. He encourages Joshua publicly, writes down the law, gives it to the priests and elders, and orders it read to all Israel every seven years at the feast of booths. The LORD summons Moses and Joshua to the Tent of Meeting, commissions Joshua, and predicts that Israel will turn to other gods and suffer for it. He instructs Moses to write a song as a witness against Israel, and Moses writes and teaches it. Moses then has the Levites place the law book beside the ark as a witness against Israel's rebellion, and he speaks the song to the whole assembly.

Chapter 32 body

Chapter 32 presents the Song of Moses, in which Moses calls heaven and earth to hear him proclaim the LORD as the faithful, just Rock. The song recounts how the LORD found Jacob in the wilderness, cared for him, and gave him rich provision, but Jeshurun grew fat, abandoned God, and sacrificed to demons and new gods. The LORD responds with anger, announcing famine, plague, beasts, and the sword, yet restrains himself from erasing Israel lest the enemies boast. He declares that vengeance is his, that there is no god beside him, and that he will avenge his servants and atone for his land and people. Moses and Joshua son of Nun recite the song to all Israel, Moses urges them to obey the law as their life, and the LORD tells Moses to go up Mount Nebo, view Canaan, and die there because of his trespass at Meribah of Kadesh.

Chapter 33 body

Chapter 33 presents the blessing with which Moses, the man of God, blessed the children of Israel before his death. It opens by describing the LORD coming from Sinai, Seir, and Mount Paran with a fiery law, and notes that Moses commanded a law as an inheritance for Jacob's assembly. Moses then blesses the tribes in turn, including Reuben, Judah, Levi, Benjamin, Joseph, Zebulun, Issachar, Gad, Dan, Naphtali, and Asher, each with a distinct word about protection, land, strength, or service. The chapter closes by praising God, who rides the heavens and is an eternal dwelling place with everlasting arms, and by declaring Israel happy as a people saved by the LORD whose enemies will submit to them.

Chapter 34 body

Moses goes up from the plains of Moab to Mount Nebo, to the top of Pisgah opposite Jericho, where the LORD shows him the whole land from Gilead to Dan, Naphtali, Ephraim, Manasseh, Judah, the Western Sea, the south, and the plain of Jericho to Zoar. The LORD tells him this is the land sworn to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, but that Moses will see it and not cross over. Moses dies there in Moab at age 120, with undimmed eyes and undiminished strength, and is buried in a valley opposite Beth Peor, his tomb unknown to this day. The children of Israel mourn him for thirty days. Joshua the son of Nun, filled with the spirit of wisdom because Moses laid hands on him, is obeyed by Israel, and the chapter ends by saying no prophet since has equaled Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face.
