Abstract written by a model

This document is Paul's First Letter to the Corinthians, in sixteen chapters, written (with Sosthenes named in the greeting) to the assembly of God at Corinth. Its central concern is a divided, puffed-up community that needs correction, and the letter moves from unity to conduct, worship, and hope. It opens with thanks for the grace given in Christ, then pleads for unity after Chloe's household reported factions claiming Paul, Apollos, Cephas, or Christ. Paul argues that the cross is God's power and wisdom, though it seems foolishness to the world, and that boasting belongs only in the Lord. Chapters 2 to 4 develop this: he came in weakness, speaking God's Spirit-revealed wisdom; the Corinthians are still fleshly babies; he and Apollos are mere servants, God gives the increase, and Christ is the foundation. Paul calls himself their father in Christ, sends Timothy, and warns he may come with a rod or in love. Chapters 5 and 6 address discipline and conduct: a man living with his father's wife must be put out, since yeast leavens the whole lump; lawsuits before unbelievers are rebuked; and the body is a temple of the Holy Spirit, bought with a price. Chapter 7 answers their written questions on marriage, singleness, separation, and remaining in one's calling. Chapters 8 to 10 treat food sacrificed to idols: knowledge puffs up but love builds up, believers must not cause a weak brother to stumble, Paul defends his apostleship and his giving up of rights for the Good News, and Israel's wilderness failures serve as warnings against idolatry. Chapters 11 to 14 concern gatherings: head coverings, abuses at the Lord's supper with the tradition of bread and cup, and the Spirit's varied gifts given for the common good, like members of one body. Chapter 13 exalts love as greater than faith and hope. Chapter 14 prefers prophecy to uninterpreted languages and ends with order in meetings. Chapter 15 restates the Good News of Christ's death and resurrection, appearances including to Paul himself, argues against denying resurrection, and describes the imperishable body and final victory over death. Chapter 16 closes with a weekly collection for the saints in Jerusalem, Paul's travel plans via Ephesus and Macedonia, instructions about Timothy, Apollos, and Stephanas's household, final greetings, and a blessing of grace and love.

Paul’s First Letter to the Corinthians / Chapter 1 body

Paul, together with Sosthenes, greets the assembly of God at Corinth and gives thanks for the grace and gifts given them in Christ Jesus, affirming that God is faithful and will confirm them blameless until the day of the Lord. Paul then begs them to be united, because people from Chloe's household reported contentions in which members claimed to follow Paul, Apollos, Cephas, or Christ. He recalls that he baptized only Crispus, Gaius, and the household of Stephanas, and says Christ sent him to preach the Good News rather than to baptize. He argues that the word of the cross seems foolishness to those who are dying but is the power of God to those being saved, and that God has made the wisdom of the world foolish. God chose the foolish, weak, and lowly so that no flesh would boast before him, and anyone who boasts should boast in the Lord.

Chapter 2 body

In this chapter the speaker, addressing the brothers, says he came to them without eloquence or human wisdom, determined to know only Jesus Christ and him crucified, and he came in weakness, fear, and trembling. His preaching demonstrated the Spirit and power so that their faith would rest on God's power rather than men's wisdom. He says he speaks God's hidden, foreordained wisdom among the full grown, which the rulers of this world did not know, or they would not have crucified the Lord of glory. God reveals these things through the Spirit, who searches the deep things of God and teaches the words spoken. The natural man cannot receive or discern them, while the spiritual person discerns all things, and believers have Christ's mind.

Chapter 3 body

The writer, Paul, tells the Corinthian believers that he could not address them as spiritual but only as fleshly babies in Christ, fed with milk rather than solid food. Their jealousy, strife, and factions, in which some say they follow Paul and others Apollos, show they are still fleshly. Paul and Apollos are only servants, one planting and one watering, while God gives the increase. The believers are God's farming, building, and temple, and Paul laid the foundation, Jesus Christ, on which others build work that fire on the Day will test. The chapter closes by warning against worldly wisdom and boasting in men, since all things belong to the believers, who are Christ's, and Christ is God's.

Chapter 4 body

In Chapter 4, the writer, speaking for himself and Apollos, asks that he and his fellow workers be regarded as Christ's servants and stewards of God's mysteries, who must be found faithful. He says he does not care about being judged by the Corinthians or any human court, and that he does not even judge himself, because the Lord is his judge and will reveal hidden things and the counsels of hearts. He rebukes the Corinthians for being puffed up and boasting of what they received, contrasting their self-satisfied reigning with the apostles' hunger, dishonor, and suffering as a spectacle to the world. He calls them his beloved children, says he became their father in Christ Jesus through the Good News, urges them to imitate him, and announces that he is sending Timothy to remind them of his ways. He warns that he will come shortly, if the Lord is willing, to test the power of the puffed-up, and asks whether they want him to come with a rod or in love and gentleness.

Chapter 5 body

In Chapter 5, the author addresses a report of sexual immorality among the Corinthian believers, in which a man has his father's wife, a sin not even named among the Gentiles. He rebukes the believers for being arrogant instead of mourning, and says that though absent in body he has already judged the man and directs them to deliver him to Satan for the destruction of the flesh so the spirit may be saved. He uses the image of yeast, which leavens the whole lump, to urge them to purge out the old yeast and keep the feast with sincerity and truth, since Christ, the Passover, has been sacrificed. He clarifies an earlier letter: they are not to avoid the sexual sinners of the world, but not to associate or even eat with a professing brother who is immoral, covetous, an idolater, a slanderer, a drunkard, or an extortionist. God judges those outside, while the believers are to judge those within and put away the wicked man.

Chapter 6 body

The writer rebukes the believers for taking lawsuits against one another before unbelievers instead of settling matters within the assembly, arguing that the saints will judge the world and angels and so should be able to decide small disputes. He says it is already a defect to have lawsuits and that they would do better to be wronged or defrauded, yet they themselves wrong and defraud their brothers. He warns that the unrighteous, including those listed in a catalogue of sins, will not inherit God's Kingdom, and reminds them that some of them were once such people but were washed, sanctified, and justified in the name of the Lord Jesus and the Spirit of God. He then addresses bodily conduct, insisting that the body is for the Lord and not for sexual immorality, that believers' bodies are members of Christ, and that the body is a temple of the Holy Spirit, bought with a price, so they must glorify God in it.

Chapter 7 body

In Chapter 7, Paul answers questions the Corinthians wrote to him about marriage and related matters. He says it is good not to touch a woman, yet urges each man and woman to have a spouse because of sexual immorality, and that husband and wife owe each other affection and authority over each other's bodies. He commands that married believers not separate, gives his own judgment about marriages with unbelievers, and allows separation if the unbeliever departs. He teaches that each person should remain in the calling in which he was called, whether circumcised, uncircumcised, bondservant or free. He advises the unmarried, virgins and widows to remain as they are because of the present distress and the shortness of time, while affirming that marrying is not sin, so that believers may attend to the Lord without distraction.

Chapter 8 body

Chapter 8 addresses the question of eating things sacrificed to idols. The writer says that those with knowledge know no idol is anything and that there is one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, but warns that knowledge puffs up while love builds up. Not everyone has this knowledge: some with a conscience about idols eat as if the food were sacrificed to an idol, and their weak conscience is defiled. Those with knowledge are told to take care that their liberty does not become a stumbling block, since a weak brother for whom Christ died may perish. The writer concludes that sinning against the brothers is sinning against Christ, and that he himself will eat no meat forever if food causes his brother to stumble.

Chapter 9 body

In this chapter the speaker, who is Paul, defends his apostleship to the Corinthian believers, calling them the seal of it, and argues that apostles have a right to eat, drink, take a believing wife, and be supported by their work. He supports this right with examples of soldiers, vineyard planters and shepherds, the law's command not to muzzle an ox, temple servants, and the Lord's ordinance that those who proclaim the Good News should live from it. Paul says he has not used this right so as not to hinder the Good News, and that he preaches under necessity and offers it without charge. He explains that he made himself a servant to all, becoming like Jews, those under the law, those without law, and the weak, in order to gain and save some. He closes with the image of runners in a race and athletes who exercise self-control, saying he runs with purpose and disciplines his body so he will not be disqualified after preaching to others.

Chapter 10 body

The speaker addresses the brothers, recalling that the fathers were all under the cloud, passed through the sea, were baptized into Moses, and shared spiritual food and drink from a rock that was Christ, yet God was displeased with most of them and they were overthrown in the wilderness. Their idolatry, sexual immorality, testing of Christ, and grumbling, each followed by death, are presented as examples and admonitions for those on whom the ends of the ages have come. The speaker assures them that no temptation is beyond what is common to man, that God is faithful and provides a way of escape, and he urges them to flee idolatry. He contrasts the cup and bread of the Lord, a sharing in Christ's blood and body, with sacrifices to demons, saying they cannot partake of both tables. He closes with guidance on eating food in the butcher shop or at an unbeliever's meal, giving up one's liberty for the sake of another's conscience, and doing all to the glory of God without causing stumbling.

Chapter 11 body

The writer urges the brothers to imitate him as he imitates Christ, praises them for keeping the traditions, and then addresses two problems in their gatherings. First he sets out an order of headship (God, Christ, man, woman) and instructs that a man praying or prophesying should not have his head covered while a woman should be covered, appealing to creation, the angels, nature, and the custom of God's assemblies, while noting that man and woman are interdependent in the Lord. Second, he rebukes the Corinthians because their assemblies bring divisions and because their meal is not the Lord's supper, as some go hungry while others are drunken. He recalls the tradition he received from the Lord about the bread and cup on the night Jesus was betrayed, warns that eating and drinking unworthily brings guilt and judgment (shown in weakness, sickness, and death among them), and tells them to examine themselves, wait for one another, and eat at home if hungry.

Chapter 12 body

Addressing the brothers about spiritual things, the writer reminds them that they were once led astray to mute idols and that only the Holy Spirit enables anyone to say "Jesus is Lord." He explains that there are various gifts, services and workings, all from the same Spirit, Lord and God, and that the Spirit gives each person a manifestation for the profit of all, including wisdom, knowledge, faith, healings, miracles, prophecy, discerning of spirits, languages and interpretation. He compares Christ and the church to a body with many members, all baptized into one Spirit, whether Jews or Greeks, bond or free. God arranged the members as he desired, so no member can say it has no need of another, and weaker or less honorable members receive more honor so that members care for one another and share suffering and honor. The chapter closes by listing the roles God set in the assembly, noting that not all have the same gift, and urging earnest desire for the best gifts while promising to show a most excellent way.

Chapter 13 body

Chapter 13 describes the supreme importance of love. The speaker says that without love, even speaking in the languages of men and angels, prophecy, all knowledge, mountain-moving faith, giving away goods to the poor, and giving his body to be burned amount to nothing. He then lists the qualities of love: it is patient and kind, bears, believes, hopes, and endures all things, and never fails, while prophecies, languages, and knowledge will be done away with because they are partial. The speaker contrasts his childhood with adulthood and now seeing dimly in a mirror with one day seeing face to face and knowing fully. He concludes that faith, hope, and love remain, and that love is the greatest of the three.

Chapter 14 body

In this chapter the writer urges the believers to pursue love and desire spiritual gifts, especially prophesying, because it edifies the assembly while speaking in another language edifies only the speaker unless it is interpreted. He uses the examples of pipe, harp, trumpet, and foreign languages to show that unintelligible speech profits no one, and he says he would rather speak five words with understanding than ten thousand in another language. He explains that other languages are a sign to unbelievers and prophesying a sign to believers, and that an unbeliever who comes in would think the assembly crazy if all spoke in languages, but would be convicted and worship God if all prophesied. He sets rules for gatherings: two or three speak in languages in turn with an interpreter, two or three prophets speak while others discern, and those without an interpreter stay silent. He also tells wives to be quiet in the assemblies, insists his words are the Lord's commandment, and closes by saying all things should be done decently and in order.

Chapter 15 body

The speaker, who is named in the text only as the one who preached to the brothers, restates the Good News he delivered: Christ died for sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared to Cephas, the twelve, over five hundred brothers, James, all the apostles, and finally to the speaker himself, who calls himself the least of the apostles because he persecuted the assembly of God. He argues against those among the addressed brothers who deny the resurrection of the dead, saying that if there is no resurrection then Christ was not raised, preaching and faith are vain, and believers remain in their sins. He affirms that Christ has been raised as the first fruits, that as in Adam all die so in Christ all will be made alive, and that Christ will reign until death, the last enemy, is abolished and the Son is subjected to God so that God may be all in all. He answers how the dead are raised using the images of seed and differing bodies and glories, contrasting the perishable natural body with the imperishable spiritual body and the first man Adam with the last Adam. He declares a mystery that all will be changed at the last trumpet, death will be swallowed up in victory, and he urges the brothers to be steadfast because their labor in the Lord is not in vain.

Chapter 16 body

Paul closes his letter by instructing the Corinthians, as he did the assemblies of Galatia, to set aside money weekly for the collection for the saints, to be carried to Jerusalem by men they approve. He outlines his travel plans: he will stay at Ephesus until Pentecost because of a great open door and many adversaries, then pass through Macedonia and hopefully stay or winter with the Corinthians. He asks them to receive Timothy without fear and send him on in peace, reports that Apollos will come when he has opportunity, and urges them to honor the house of Stephanas and to acknowledge Stephanas, Fortunatus, and Achaicus. He ends with greetings from the assemblies of Asia, Aquila and Priscilla, and all the brothers, a curse on anyone who doesn't love the Lord Jesus Christ, and a blessing of grace and love.
