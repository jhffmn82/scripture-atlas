Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra)

Hermann Jacobi · 27 sections
Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra), translated by Hermann Jacobi, Sacred Books of the East vols. 22 (1884) and 45 (1895), public domain, via sacred-texts.com. Shown here as plain text, with facts drawn from it. https://sacred-texts.com/jai/

Abstract written by a model

The Akaranga Sutra, Book II, translated by Hermann Jacobi, is a comprehensive rulebook for monks, nuns, mendicants, Sramanas, and Nirgranthas. It begins with detailed instructions for begging food: alms must not harm living beings or be impure, specially prepared, improperly offered, or sought through desire, competition, gesture, speech, or favoritism. Mendicants must avoid festivals and unsuitable houses, share food impartially, handle doubtful food, drink, meat, salt, and provisions for sick mendicants by prescribed rules, and respect fellow ascetics who follow differing commands of the Gina. It then governs lodging, walking, speech, clothes, bowls, and possessions, requiring inspection, restraint, permission, non-injury, careful travel, truthful and harmless speech, and limited, suitable property. The Second Part adds rules for postures, study places, easing nature, and avoiding sensory attachment to sounds, colours, forms, care, adornment, and reciprocal bodily services. The Third Part recounts Mahâvîra’s descent, birth, renunciation, twelve years of ascetic practice, attainment of Kevala, Nirvâna, and teaching first to gods and then to men. Mahâvîra teaches Gautama and other Sramanas and Nirgranthas the five great vows: abstaining from killing, lying, taking what is not given, sexual pleasures, and attachments, each with five clauses. The final lecture presents liberation: recognizing temporary residence, abandoning bonds and attachment, enduring suffering and pleasure without harm, and practicing austerity, wisdom, meditation, restraint, and the great vows until one quits the path of births.

Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra), Book II, translated by Hermann Jacobi / Second Book / First Part. / First Lecture, Call body

The First Lecture, Called Begging of Food, in the Akaranga Sutra concerns monks, nuns, and mendicants collecting and accepting alms. It sets rules against accepting food affected by living beings, mildew, seeds, sprouts, water, dust, or other impurities, while allowing food recognised as sufficiently processed. It also regulates conduct toward householders, heretics, and other ascetics during begging-tours and religious activities. The lecture concludes by describing the whole duty of a monk or nun.

Second Lesson. body

This unit concerns rules for a monk or nun on a begging-tour regarding when food and related offerings may be accepted. Food prepared for large entertainments, festivals, or communal occasions is generally impure and unacceptable unless prepared by another person or offered after others have received their due share. A monk or nun must not go toward a festive entertainment, especially when it is more than half a yogana away, and should travel in the opposite direction when hearing where it is held. The Kevalin explains that accepting such food can incur sin, while a layman may improperly alter an upâsraya or prepare a couch for the mendicant.

Third Lesson. body

The Third Lesson concerns rules for mendicants, monks, and nuns regarding festive entertainments, feasts, alms, food, travel, and religious conduct. The Kevalin explains that attending entertainments preceded or followed by feasts can lead to illness, sensual temptation, and sin. Monks and nuns must avoid certain entertainments, doubtful food, impure houses, and unsafe conditions, and must wear the complete outfit in specified situations. The unit also regulates entering a householder’s abode, visiting out-of-door places, wandering between villages, and accepting alms.

Fourth Lesson. body

The unit concerns the conduct of a monk or nun on a begging-tour and when they may attend entertainments or enter a householder’s abode. They should avoid festivals, wedding breakfasts, funeral dinners, and family dinners under specified conditions involving food, living beings, crowds, and obstacles to study. They may enter a householder’s abode for alms only after milking, cooking, and distribution are complete. Mendicants should not visit related families before begging to obtain food, but may later accept respectful alms at the proper time.

Fifth Lesson. body

The unit concerns the conduct of a monk or nun on a begging-tour, especially when approaching a householder's abode and encountering obstacles, dangerous beings, or other recipients of alms. They must avoid hazards when a byway exists, obtain permission before clearing a secured entrance, and clean a soiled body only with suitable materials in a secluded place. They must not compete with other Sramanas, Brâhmanas, guests, paupers, or beggars for food, stand in their sight, or address the householder before them. Food received for all must be impartially divided or eaten equally, without choosing the largest, most conspicuous, savoury, delicious, nice, or otherwise desirable portions.

Sixth Lesson. body

The Sixth Lesson concerns the conduct of a monk or nun on a begging-tour. It instructs them to avoid gatherings of hungry animals, improper behavior around a householder’s abode, and gestures or speech used to solicit food. It explains when food and utensils are impure or acceptable based on the condition of the giver’s hand and the preparation of the food. It prohibits accepting food prepared over fire because doing so causes harm to fire-bodies.

Seventh Lesson. body

The unit concerns a monk or nun on a begging-tour and the kinds of food and water they may accept. They must reject food placed in elevated, earthen, or living matter, and food or water that has been altered through certain actions by a layman. The Kevalin explains that some preparations may injure living beings or cause sin. Certain washed or used water may be accepted only when it has changed and been strained, while other water is unacceptable.

Eighth Lesson. body

The Eighth Lesson sets out rules for a monk or nun on a begging-tour concerning unacceptable liquids, scents, raw plants, fruits, vegetables, grains, and other substances. Such a monk or nun must reject items that are impure, unmodified, contain seeds or particles, or harbor living beings. The lesson also instructs them not to smell pleasant food or drink scents with eagerness or desire. It concludes by declaring this the whole duty.

Ninth Lesson. body

The Ninth Lesson concerns mendicants, monks, and nuns and their conduct when receiving food or drink. They must avoid âdhâkarmika food, food prepared for them or for others in certain circumstances, and food from houses of their relations. They must consume all received food and drink without selecting preferred portions, and should consult nearby fellow-ascetics before rejecting excess. Food placed before a door may be accepted only with the householder’s permission.

Tenth Lesson. body

The Tenth Lesson concerns rules for mendicants and nuns about collecting, distributing, accepting, and consuming alms. A mendicant who collects food for many must consult an appropriate religious superior before distributing it and must reveal what was received without hiding or keeping the best portions. Mendicants and nuns must reject impure and unacceptable items, including partly edible sugar-cane, pods, meat or fish with many bones, and certain salt offered without proper knowledge. Inadvertently accepted meat, fish, fossil salt, or sea salt must be handled according to prescribed rules of eating, disposal, permission, or sharing.

Eleventh Lesson. body

The unit concerns mendicants, including a sick mendicant, and the proper handling of food and drink received through begging. It describes sinful ways of concealing or misrepresenting food intended for a sick mendicant. It sets out seven rules for begging food and seven corresponding rules for begging drink, including special provisions for moisture, cleaning, and water used for watering flour or sesamum. Mendicants who follow different rules are instructed to respect one another as all acting according to the commandment of the Gina.

Second Lecture, Called Begging for a Couch. / First Lesson. .. Third Lesson. body

This unit concerns monks and nuns seeking lodging and couches while avoiding injury to living beings, dependence on householders, and occasions for sin. It teaches that lodging-places must be inspected, cleaned, and suitable, and that householders must not prepare or alter them specifically for mendicants. It sets out restrictions on householders' lodgings, rules for staying in temporary residences, and precautions for entering, leaving, sleeping, and easing nature. It also explains four rules for begging for a couch and the conditions under which a couch may be accepted or returned.

Third Lecture, Called Walking. / First Lesson. .. Third Lesson. body

The Third Lecture, Called Walking, teaches how a monk or a nun should remain in suitable places during the rainy season and travel safely and circumspectly afterward. It gives rules for wandering from village to village, avoiding living beings, dangerous roads, forests, armies, thieves, and unsuitable settlements. It also explains how to cross by boat or through shallow water without assisting with boatwork or harming living beings. Throughout the pilgrimage, the monk or nun should remain composed, avoid unnecessary speech and gestures, and collect themselves for contemplation.

Fourth Lecture, Called Modes of Speech. / First Lesson. .. Second Lesson. body

This unit concerns how a monk or nun should understand and employ speech. It distinguishes truth, untruth, truth mixed with untruth, and speech that is neither truth nor untruth, and instructs them to avoid sinful, blamable, harsh, divisive, or harmful words. It gives precise and restrained ways to speak about men, women, diseases, qualities, objects, animals, plants, sounds, colours, smells, tastes, and touches. It teaches that all past, present, and future Arhats have taught these four kinds of speech.

Fifth Lecture, Called Begging of Clothes. / First Lesson. .. Second Lesson. body

The Fifth Lecture, Called Begging of Clothes, concerns monks and nuns obtaining, accepting, wearing, maintaining, and protecting clothes. The First Lesson sets rules for acceptable materials, quantities, inspection, rejection of impure or unsuitable clothes, four clothes-begging rules, and proper airing or drying. The Second Lesson regulates wearing, borrowing, exchanging, dyeing, and protecting clothes while travelling, including conduct when facing thieves. The unit ends by stating the whole duty for mendicants who wear clothes.

Sixth Lecture, Called Begging for a Bowl. / First Lesson. .. Second Lesson. body

The unit concerns monks and nuns begging for, accepting, inspecting, and carrying an alms-bowl. They may beg for bowls made of bottle-gourd, wood, or clay, but must not accept expensive or otherwise impure bowls, including bowls prepared with food or drink. They must inspect bowls for living beings, seeds, grass, and dust, and handle wet bowls and rain carefully. Householders may offer bowls or water, but the mendicants must refuse offerings that are impure or unacceptable.

Seventh Lecture, Called Regulation of Possession. / First Lesson. .. Second Lesson. body

The Seventh Lecture, Called Regulation of Possession, concerns how Sramanas, monks, and nuns may acquire, use, share, and relinquish dwellings and objects. It requires permission before taking possession of places or property and prohibits taking what has not been given or using possessions in ways that cause harm or offense. It sets rules for food, borrowed articles, mangoes, sugar-cane, garlic, and suitable dwelling places. Mahâvîra explains that dominion is fivefold, while the Second Lesson describes seven rules of possession.

Second Part. / The Seven Lectures. / Eighth Lecture. body

The Eighth Lecture concerns monks and nuns who wish to perform religious postures. They must enter a village or a scot-free town and avoid accepting places infected by eggs or living beings. A mendicant may choose one of four rules, ranging from standing while changing position and moving slightly to standing perfectly motionless without leaning. The fourth rule also requires abandoning care of the body, hair, beard, other body parts, and nails.

Ninth Lecture. body

The Ninth Lecture concerns monks and nuns choosing a pure place for study. They should not accept a place infected by eggs or living beings, but may accept one free from them. The rules previously stated about couches are repeated for places for study as far as “water-plants.” Parties of two, three, four, or five mendicants who go to study should not embrace, bite, or scratch each other.

Tenth Lecture. body

The Tenth Lecture concerns the rules governing where and how a monk or nun may ease nature. They must avoid grounds and places containing eggs or living beings, places prepared or used for harmful or unsuitable purposes, and many specified public, sacred, cultivated, or inhabited locations. They should use a chamber-pot and go to a secluded place where no people pass or see them, then leave the excrements on a heap of ashes or a similar spot.

Eleventh Lecture. body

The lecture sets out rules for monks and nuns concerning sounds and places associated with hearing them. They should not resolve to visit performances, gatherings, dangerous places, or other locations for the sake of hearing instrumental, vocal, or other sounds. They should not seek or become enraptured with sounds of this or the other world, whether heard or unheard, seen or unseen. The lecture presents this as their whole duty.

Twelfth Lecture. body

The Twelfth Lecture concerns rules for monks and nuns regarding various colours or forms. It says they should not deliberately go where they will see such colours or forms merely to please the eye. The examples include colours or forms in wreaths, dressed images, dolls, clothes, woodwork, plastering, paintings, jewelry, ivory-work, strings, and leaf-cutting. Earlier instructions about sounds are to be repeated, except for passages on music.

Thirteenth Lecture. body

The Thirteenth Lecture concerns how a mendicant should respond when another person, especially a householder, performs various acts of care or adornment for him. The mendicant should neither be pleased with nor prohibit actions that relate to him and produce karman, including washing, treating wounds, grooming, decorating, or attempting cures. It states that sensation results from former actions and that all sorts of living beings experience sensation. The lecture concludes by declaring this to be the whole duty.

Fourteenth Lecture. body

The Fourteenth Lecture concerns a mendicant’s conduct regarding reciprocal actions that relate to himself and produce karman. He should neither be pleased with nor prohibit such actions, including wiping or rubbing each other’s feet. The whole Thirteenth Lecture is to be repeated in this context. The unit ends by stating the whole duty and marking the end of the Second Part, called the Seven Lectures.

Third Part. / Fifteenth Lecture, Called the Clauses. body

This unit recounts the life of the Venerable Ascetic Mahâvîra from his descent from heaven through his birth, renunciation, twelve years of ascetic practice, and attainment of Kevala. It describes his conception in Devânandâ’s womb, transfer to Trisalâ’s womb, birth in Kundapura, and the divine ceremonies performed by gods and goddesses. Mahâvîra renounced household life in the palankin Kandraprabhâ, adopted holy conduct, attained Manahparyâya knowledge, and later reached Nirvâna and the highest knowledge and intuition called Kevala. After attaining Kevala, he first taught the law to the gods and afterward to men.

The Venerable Ascetic Mahâvîra endowed with the highest knowledge and intuition body

The Venerable Ascetic Mahâvîra taught the five great vows and their twenty-five clauses to the Sramanas and Nirgranthas, including Gautama. The vows concern abstinence from killing, lying, taking what is not given, sexual pleasures, and attachments. Each vow is explained through five clauses governing conduct, thought, speech, possessions, sensuality, and the senses. A Nirgrantha who correctly practises these vows and clauses is truly Houseless.

Fourth Part. / Sixteenth Lecture, Called the Liberation. body

The Sixteenth Lecture, Called the Liberation, teaches that creatures have only temporary residences and that the wise should meditate on the supreme truth, abandon family bonds, acts, and attachments, and live as mendicants. The mendicant endures insults, blows, calamities, pain, and pleasure without harming beings, while austerity, wisdom, and glory increase through steadfast meditation and restraint. The great vows, proclaimed by the infinite Gina, illuminate the three worlds and produce peace and disinterestedness. The unit explains that renouncing samsâra, understanding bondage and deliverance, and living with wisdom and restraint lead to becoming a Maker of the end and quitting the path of births.
