Scripture Atlas

The scriptures as a navigable knowledge graph, grounded in their own words
Walk

Scripture Atlas maps sacred texts into a graph you can walk: the people, places, events and ideas of each book, the facts the text states about them, and the timeline of each tradition's major events. A model organizes the text into the graph, but it is never the source of truth: the text is. Every fact on this site links to the verbatim words it was read from, and every quotation opens the chapter it came from. The summaries around the facts are written by a model, and every page marks them so; the timelines are counted from the text.

Nothing is merged. When the same person, place or event appears in two books, one page links both, and each claim stays with the book that makes it. The search below runs in your browser over every fact, summary and verse: by keywords, by meaning, or both, and it can walk the graph from what it finds.

The library at a glance: each collection a hub, each work a leaf. Every circle opens its page.

How these timelines were made

Which events. For each religion, every event the reader found in the religion's works, ranked by the number of chapters that tell it, then the number of works, then the number of facts about it; the top 40 told in 2 or more chapters (when fewer than 10 reach 2 chapters, the top 10), and with them every event tied with the last one in on all three counts, so no tie at the cut is broken by name. Code counted these from the text; no model chose them. The reader found the events, and each event’s page holds the verses it was read from.

Their order. Each event sits where the text first tells it: the works in canon order, then the chapter. Events a source dates are also shown on a time axis by that date.

Dates. No date comes from the text or from a model. For each event a model searched English Wikipedia, the Jewish Encyclopedia (1906), the Catholic Encyclopedia (1913, New Advent) and Wikidata for a page that dates it, and proposed the page and the date. Code then fetched the page and kept the date only when a sentence on that page names the event, by a word of the event’s own name (the page’s title or the person alone does not count: a sentence giving Mahavira’s birth year does not date his descent from heaven), and states the date; that sentence is quoted, and the date is shown as it words it. A second model then read two or three of the event’s own passages, verbatim from the religion’s works, beside the sentence, and the date was kept only when it judged the sentence to describe that same event as those passages tell it (so Jesus’ baptism does not date the Book of Mormon’s baptisms); its answer is kept with the date. A date stands on the time axis only at a year its own words state, so a count such as “in his 18th year” or “the 2666th year after creation” dates nothing here. Britannica was not used: its pages refuse automated fetches, so nothing from it could be checked. A date is the source’s, in its own words; a traditional date is given as the source gives it. An event no page dates this way stays undated.

About

Each religion is one collection. The Bible is the World English Bible, Catholic Edition: the 73 books of the Catholic canon in their traditional order, a modern English translation dedicated to the public domain by eBible.org. Its footnotes are left out; the words of the text are as eBible.org prints them.

The site is built by the ThreadAtlas pipeline, a knowledge graph designed so that a person can check every claim against its source. Claude Sonnet read the Bible, found its entities and facts, and wrote their summaries; OpenAI's GPT-5.6 models (Luna and Terra) read the other texts, and judged which entities across books are the same one. Code located every quotation in the text before a fact was kept.

A timeline shows each collection's 40 events told in the most chapters, counted from the text (then by the number of works, then by facts), in the order the text first tells them: works in canon order, then chapter. Nothing on it comes from a model's own knowledge: no eras or dates are given, since the texts do not state them, and each event opens its page, its claims and their verses.

The store this site was built from holds 261 documents. 261 are the books and papers on this site: The Bible, 73 works; Qur'an and Prophetic Teachings, 116 works; Book of Mormon, 15 works; Jain Āgamas and Early Monastic Scriptures, 7 works; Works of Guru Nanak and Sikh Daily Prayers, 6 works; Avesta: Zoroastrian Ritual and Religious Texts, 10 works; Taoist Classics, 2 works; Confucian Classics: Analects, Great Learning, Doctrine of the Mean, and Mencius, 29 works; Kojiki (Records of Ancient Matters), 3 works. Across all of it, 11,621 entities link their appearances in different documents.

How it works

  1. Extraction. Each book is read on its own and cut into its chapters. Every boundary must copy text found on the line it cites.
  2. Ingestion. Per book: the entities, the facts with verbatim quotes, an account of each entity in each chapter, and summaries. A fact is kept only if its quote is found in its chapter's text.
  3. Global merge. Which entities in different books are the same one? A judge model rules on each offered pair; one page links the instances without merging them. Each religion is its own collection, sorted by a model into groups of books.
  4. Timeline. Each collection's events, ranked by how many chapters tell of them and ordered by where the text first tells them.
  5. Wiki. This site, rendered from the store by one deterministic pass: no page is written by hand.
