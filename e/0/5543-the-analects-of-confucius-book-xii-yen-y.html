The Analects of Confucius, Book XII (Yen Yuan)

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

This document is a sequence of teachings and exchanges centered on the Master, Confucius, concerning virtue, the superior man, government, knowledge, and friendship. It begins with Yen Yuan, Chung-kung, and Sze-ma Niu asking about perfect virtue. The Master defines it through self-restraint, propriety, reciprocity, careful speech, and freedom from anxiety and fear gained through self-examination. Tsze-hsia reassures the anxious Sze-ma Niu that reverent conduct, respect, and propriety make all within the four seas one’s brothers. The discussion then defines intelligence as resistance to slander and startling claims, and identifies public confidence as government’s indispensable foundation, above military equipment and even food when choices must be made. Other passages argue that substance and accomplishment belong together, that rulers should ensure their people have plenty, and that sincerity and movement toward right are necessary for virtue. Government is presented as proper fulfillment of roles, consistent attention to affairs, rectification by rulers’ own correctness, and prevention rather than mere settlement of litigation. Confucius rejects killing the unprincipled as a governing method, instead urging rulers to desire goodness, since people follow their superiors like grass bends beneath wind. He also connects theft to Chi K'ang’s covetousness. The latter chapters distinguish true distinction from notoriety, teach self-correction rather than attacking others, define benevolence as loving people and knowledge as knowing them, and recommend employing the upright. The document ends with guidance on friendship: admonish faithfully and tactfully, withdraw if necessary, and use cultural friendship to help virtue.

CHAP. I. 1. Yen Yuan asked about perfect virtue. The Master

Yen Yuan asks the Master about perfect virtue. The Master says that perfect virtue consists in subduing oneself and returning to propriety, and that practicing this for one day would cause all under heaven to ascribe perfect virtue to the person. Yen Yuan asks for the steps, and the Master instructs him not to see, hear, speak, or move contrary to propriety. Yen Yuan resolves to practice this lesson despite his lack of intelligence and vigour.

CHAP. II. Chung-kung asked about perfect virtue. The Master

Chung-kung asks the Master about perfect virtue. The Master teaches that it involves treating everyone as a great guest, employing people as if assisting at a great sacrifice, and not doing to others what one would not wish done to oneself. He also says perfect virtue means having no murmuring against one in the country or family. Chung-kung acknowledges his deficiencies but resolves to practise this lesson.

CHAP. III. 1. Sze-ma Niu asked about perfect virtue.

Sze-ma Niu asks the Master about perfect virtue. The Master says that a person of perfect virtue is cautious and slow in speech. Niu questions whether this caution and slowness alone constitute perfect virtue. The Master explains that awareness of the difficulty of doing leads a person to speak cautiously and slowly.

CHAP. IV. 1. Sze-ma Niu asked about the superior man. The

Sze-ma Niu asks the Master about the superior man. The Master says that the superior man has neither anxiety nor fear. When internal examination discovers nothing wrong, there is nothing to be anxious about or fear.

CHAP. V. 1. Sze-ma Niu, full of anxiety, said, 'Other men all

Sze-ma Niu is anxious because he has no brothers while other men have them. Tsze-hsia tells him that death and life have appointed outcomes, and that riches and honours depend on Heaven. He teaches that a superior man should reverentially order his conduct, respect others, and observe propriety. By doing so, all within the four seas will be his brothers.

CHAP. VI. Tsze-chang asked what constituted intelligence. The

Tsze-chang asks the Master what constitutes intelligence. The Master says a person is intelligent when neither gradually soaking slander nor startling statements can successfully influence him. He adds that such resistance may also be called farseeing.

CHAP. VII. 1. Tsze-kung asked about government. The Master

Tsze-kung asks the Master about the requisites of government. The Master says government requires sufficient food, sufficient military equipment, and the people's confidence in their ruler. When forced to give up one, military equipment should be given up first, followed by food if necessary. The state cannot stand if the people have no faith in their rulers, although death has always been the lot of all men.

CHAP. VIII. 1. Chi Tsze-ch'ang said, 'In a superior man it is

Chi Tsze-ch'ang says that a superior man needs only substantial qualities and questions the need for ornamental accomplishments. Tsze-kung rebukes him, saying that his words reveal him as a superior man but that spoken words cannot be taken back. Tsze-kung explains that ornament and substance are equally important. He compares a tiger or leopard hide stripped of hair to a dog or goat hide stripped of hair.

CHAP. IX. 1. The Duke Ai inquired of Yu Zo, saying, 'The year

Duke Ai asks Yu Zo what should be done because the year is one of scarcity and returns for expenditure are insufficient. Yu Zo advises him to tithe the people by taking one tenth. Duke Ai says that even with two tenths he finds the revenue insufficient. Yu Zo explains that when the people have plenty, their prince will not want, and when the people are in want, their prince cannot enjoy plenty alone.

CHAP. X. 1. Tsze-chang having asked how virtue was to be

Tsze-chang asks the Master how virtue can be exalted and delusions discovered. The Master teaches that faithfulness and sincerity should be held as first principles, with continual movement toward what is right. He identifies it as a delusion to wish a man to live and then also wish him to die. The unit ends with a statement about making a difference despite wealth not being the reason.

CHAP. XI. 1. The Duke Ching, of Ch'i, asked Confucius about

The Duke Ching, of Ch'i, asks Confucius about government. Confucius says government exists when the prince is prince, the minister is minister, the father is father, and the son is son. The duke agrees that if these roles are not properly fulfilled, he could not enjoy his revenue even if he possessed it.

CHAP. XII. 1. The Master said, 'Ah! it is Yu, who could with

The Master identifies Yu as someone who could settle litigations with half a word. Tsze-lu is described as never sleeping over a promise. The unit concerns the Master, Yu, Tsze-lu, and litigations.

CHAP. XIII. The Master said, 'In hearing litigations, I am like

The Master says that in hearing litigations, he is like any other person. He emphasizes that the important goal is to cause the people to have no litigations.

CHAP. XIV. Tsze-chang asked about government. The Master

Tsze-chang asks the Master about government. The Master explains that the art of governing requires keeping its affairs before the mind without weariness. He adds that governing affairs should be practised with undeviating consistency.

CHAP. XV. The Master said, 'By extensively studying all

The Master teaches that extensive study of all learning should be combined with restraint under the rules of propriety. He says that this combination enables a person not to err from what is right.

CHAP. XVI. The Master said, 'The superior man seeks to

The Master says that the superior man seeks to perfect the admirable qualities of men. He does not seek to perfect their bad qualities. The mean man does the opposite.

CHAP. XVII. Chi K'ang asked Confucius about government.

Chi K'ang asks Confucius about government. Confucius explains that governing means rectifying. He says that if a leader guides the people with correctness, they will dare not be incorrect.

CHAP. XVIII. Chi K'ang, distressed about the number of

Chi K'ang was distressed by the number of thieves in the state and asked Confucius how to eliminate them. Confucius said that if Chi K'ang were not covetous, the thieves would not steal even if rewarded to do so. The advice connects the thieves' stealing with Chi K'ang's covetousness.

CHAP. XIX. Chi K'ang asked Confucius about government,

Chi K'ang asks Confucius about government and whether killing the unprincipled benefits the principled. Confucius says that a ruler should not use killing in carrying on government. He advises rulers to show desires for what is good so that the people will be good. He compares the relation between superiors and inferiors to the wind and grass, with inferiors bending when superiors act upon them.

CHAP. XX. 1. Tsze-chang asked, 'What must the officer be, who

Tsze-chang asks the Master what qualifies an officer as distinguished. The Master explains that being known throughout the State or clan is notoriety, not distinction. A distinguished man is solid, straightforward, righteous, attentive to others' words and expressions, and humble. A notorious man only appears virtuous while acting against virtue and remaining self-satisfied.

CHAP. XXI. 1. Fan Ch'ih rambling with the Master under the

Fan Ch'ih asks the Master how to exalt virtue, correct cherished evil, and discover delusions. The Master praises the question and teaches that doing what must be done before seeking success exalts virtue. He says that attacking one's own wickedness rather than others' corrects cherished evil. He describes morning's anger that disregards one's own life and involves one's parents as delusion.

CHAP. XXII. 1. Fan Ch'ih asked about benevolence. The Master

Fan Ch'ih asks the Master about benevolence and knowledge. The Master says benevolence is to love all men and knowledge is to know all men. When Fan Ch'ih does not understand, the Master explains that employing the upright and putting aside the crooked can make the crooked upright. Tsze-hsia interprets this by citing Shun's use of Kao-yao and T'ang's use of I Yin, after which those devoid of virtue disappeared.

CHAP. XXIII. Tsze-kung asked about friendship. The Master

Tsze-kung asks The Master about friendship. The Master advises faithfully admonishing a friend and skillfully leading him on. If the friend proves impracticable, the person should stop and avoid disgracing himself.

CHAP. XXIV. The philosopher Tsang said, 'The superior man

Tsang says that the superior man meets with his friends on grounds of culture. Through their friendship, the superior man helps his virtue. The statement concerns the relationship between culture, friendship, and virtue.
