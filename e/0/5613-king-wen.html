king Wen

person · 5 works · 31 quoted facts · 17 connections
Also called: King Wan, King Wen, Wan, Wen, chief of the West, he, his, king Wan, king Wen's, the chief of the West

Abstract written by a model

In The Doctrine of the Mean, King Wan has illustrious unceasing virtue as the son of King Chi and father of King Wu. In Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang), king Wen is portrayed as a marvellous ruler who shares abundance and practises benevolent royal government for the people. In Mencius, Book II (Gong Sun Chou), king Wen ultimately becomes sovereign of the kingdom despite beginning with only a territory of one hundred square li. In Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou), king Wen nourishes the old, regards people as wounded, practises principles in the Middle Kingdom, and dies in Bi Ying. In Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), king Wen nourishes old people through agricultural and household regulations, giving the people a rousing impulse as they await him.

king Wen in Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang)

Mencius · about 299 BC · the work’s page · read it where king Wen first appears

Abstract written by a model

King Wen is portrayed as a marvellous ruler whose tower, pond, park, animals, birds, fish, and turtles share an image of abundance. His seventy-square-li park was open to grass-cutters, fuel-gatherers, and hunters, showing that he shared it with the people. He served the Kun barbarians despite commanding a great country, practised true royal government, and showed valour by stopping the march to Chu, consolidating Zhou’s prosperity, meeting the nation’s expectations, and giving repose to the kingdom’s people. His benevolent government prioritized four destitute classes, limited taxes and prohibitions, protected criminals’ families from inherited guilt, and did not take possession without the people’s approval.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 2

King Wen had a park containing seventy square li. He allowed grass-cutters, fuel-gatherers, and hunters to enter it, sharing it with the people.

Chapter 3

King Wen is presented as an example of a ruler who served the Kun barbarians. His valour consisted in one burst of anger that stopped the march to Chu, consolidated the prosperity of Zhou, met the expectations of the nation, and gave repose to all the people of the kingdom.

Chapter 5

king Wen's government of Qi cultivated one-ninth of the land for the government, salaried descendants of officers, inspected strangers at passes and markets without taxing goods, imposed no prohibitions on ponds and weirs, and did not involve the families of criminals in their guilt. He made the four classes of destitute people the first objects of his benevolent regard.

Abstract written by a model

King Wen has great virtue and lives a hundred years before dying, yet his influence does not penetrate throughout the kingdom during his lifetime. Beginning with a territory of one hundred square li, he faces difficulty attaining royal dignity, but ultimately becomes sovereign of the kingdom with only a hundred li.

king Wen in Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou)

Mencius · about 299 BC · the work’s page · read it where king Wen first appears

Abstract written by a model

King Wen, a chief of the West born in Zhou near mount Qi and western wild tribes, nourishes the old and regards people as wounded. Ashamed to receive commands, he practices principles in the Middle Kingdom; his rise draws Bo Yi and Tai Gong, and imitation of him enables States to give laws. He dies in Bi Ying.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 7

King Wen is presented as the model for a prince who is ashamed to receive commands. Imitating king Wen is said to enable a large State to give laws to the kingdom in five years and a small State in seven years.

Chapter 13

King Wen's rise caused Bo Yi and Tai Gong to decide to follow him. Their joining him represented the fathers of the kingdom coming to follow him.
