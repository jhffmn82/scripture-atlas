Abstract written by a model

In Sutrakritanga (Sûtrakritâṅga), Book I, the Law is presented as the highest doctrine guiding practitioners through non-injury, restraint, renunciation, and destruction of Karman toward Liberation. In Sutrakritanga (Sûtrakritâṅga), Book I, the creed is presented as a pure, complete, unparalleled law requiring faithful teaching, non-hostility, and renunciation. In Sutrakritanga, Book II, the Law is presented as a permanent teaching of nonviolence, purity, control, renunciation, and Nirvâna that leads practitioners to final beatitude. In Sutrakritanga, Book II, the Law is presented as the supreme right path of vows, control, compassion, harmless conduct, and pure livelihood. In Uttaradhyayana Sutra, the Law of ascetics is presented as teaching nonviolence and careful conduct, saving those who follow it and gaining both worlds for them. In Uttaradhyayana Sutra, the Law is presented as a noble, true teaching and enduring discipline that guides practitioners toward perfection and freedom from rebirth. In Uttaradhyayana Sutra, the Law is presented as a dharma of self-control, penance, right conduct, and meditation that frees the soul from Karman and leads to Nirvâna. In Uttaradhyayana Sutra, righteousness is presented as sought by a Sramana through austerities, study, self-control, proper food, suitable companionship, and seclusion.

Law in Sutrakritanga (Sûtrakritâṅga), Book I

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where Law first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Law is the highest, pure, complete, and unparalleled doctrine of the Ginas, Kêvalins, Kâsyapa, Mahâvîra, and Arhats. Associated with knowledge, self-control, restraint, and freedom from Karman, it is taught, argued, demonstrated, pondered, searched for, and made manifest by those who have learned and considered it. Its central demands are non-injury to living beings, truthfulness, restraint from taking what is not freely given, freedom from sexual pleasure and property, renunciation, detachment, austerities, careful conduct, proper speech, subduing the senses, and guarding against transgression in words and thoughts. It distinguishes sin, rejects false doctrine and individual falsification, and should be taught truthfully, clearly, without blameable practices, passion, or a wish to please or irritate. By following it, practitioners cease actions, destroy Karman, stop the current of Samsâra, cross misery and transmigration, and move toward peace, Liberation, or the end of mundane existence. Those who ignore or do not know it do not attain these ends and are beyond right faith; those who learn, consider, and reveal it are to be honoured.

Sutrakritanga (Sûtrakritâṅga), Book I / First Book. / First Lecture, Called the Doctrine. / First Chapter. .. Fourth Chapter.

The true Law is associated with knowledge, self-control, restraint, and freedom from Karman. Those who ignore it or follow false doctrines do not reach the end of transmigration or misery.

Second Lecture, Called the Destruction of Karman. / First Chapter. .. Third Chapter.

The Law teaches perfect knowledge, nonviolence, control, detachment, austerities, and right conduct. Following it enables practitioners to cease from actions, destroy Karman, cross Samsâra, and seek liberation.

Third Lecture, Called the Knowledge of Troubles / First Chapter. .. Fourth Chapter.

The Law teaches renunciation, control, nonviolence, truthfulness, restraint from taking what is not freely given, and freedom from sexual pleasure and property. Following it leads toward peace and final liberation.

Sixth Lecture, Called Praise of Mahâvîra.

The Law of the Ginas was proclaimed by the omniscient sage Kâsyapa and is described as the highest Law. Mahâvîra taught it with true knowledge, arguments, and demonstrations, and people who hear and believe it may end their mundane existence or become like Indra.

Seventh Lecture, Called Description of the Wicked.

The Law is considered by the wise man who understands that harming beings is sinful. It teaches restraint, abstention from violence, control, and freedom from passions.

Eighth Lecture, Called on Exertion.

The Law requires a person rich in control not to kill living beings, take what is not freely given, or speak falsely or treacherously. It also requires guarding against transgression by words and thoughts and subduing the senses.

Ninth Lecture, Called the Law.

The Law is preached by Mahâvîra and consists of teachings on non-injury, renunciation, restraint, proper speech, conduct, and liberation.

Tenth Lecture, Called Carefulness.

The Law teaches careful conduct, control, sound judgment, non-injury, truth, and freedom from worldly ties. A wise man or sage should consider it well and expound its true doctrine.

Eleventh Lecture, Called the Path.

The pure, complete, and unparalleled Law is expounded by one who guards his soul, subdues his senses, stops the current of Samsâra, and is free from Âsravas. Those who do not know it are not truly awakened and are beyond right faith.

Twelfth Lecture, Called the Creed.

A man who has learned it by himself or from others and has well considered it makes it manifest like a light. Such a man should always be honoured.

Thirteenth Lecture, Called the Real Truth.

The Law is learned from teachers and should be taught according to truth for the benefit of men. It should not be falsified according to individual opinion, and it should be preached without blameable practices, passion, or a desire to please or irritate others.

Fourteenth Lecture, Called the Nirgrantha.

The Law is mastered through instruction, investigation, and practice. A monk should explain it clearly, answer well-deliberated questions, and distinguish sin.

Sixteenth Lecture, Called the Song.

The Law is searched for and known by the Nirgrantha.

Law in Uttaradhyayana Sutra

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where Law first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Law is a noble, excellent, true teaching and enduring discipline, possessed by the wise and followed by the virtuous. It is difficult for living beings to hear, believe, fulfil, and practise, even when they have a human body, all five senses, or have been instructed; people may instead follow heretical teachers or embrace unrighteousness. A monk should meditate on it after corporal punishment, practise it with noble conduct, passionlessness, self-restraint, contentment, and attentiveness to chastity, continuing despite hardship, ignorance, and doubts about the Ginas. Its path includes penance, combating passions, non-injury, truth, righteousness, carefulness, patience, austerity, compassion, renunciation, and avoiding lying, taking what is not given, property, women, pride, and deceit. It teaches the consequences of virtue, sin, and transient worldly possessions: turning away leads the great sinner to hell and remorse at death, while following it leads toward gods, perfection, and freedom from rebirth. Nami becomes a Svayamsambuddha in the true Law; Brahmadatta knows it but does not follow it, while Kitra hears and practises it. It is a pond for spiritual purification, the only lasting safeguard at death, and an unchangeable, permanent, eternal Law proclaimed by the Ginas, through which the Siddhas reached perfection.

Second Lecture.

The Law is described as something a monk should meditate on, practice through noble and excellent conduct, and embody while doing no actions and remaining passionless. The monk should continue following the Law despite hardship, ignorance, or doubts about the existence of the Ginas.

Third Lecture.

The Law is difficult to hear, believe in, and fulfill. Those who hear and believe in it may practice penances, combat their passions, abstain from killing living beings, and stand firmly in it.

Fifth Lecture.

The Law is transgressed by the fool who embraces unrighteousness, leading to remorse at the hour of death. A faithful man should practise the rules of conduct for householders and not neglect the Pôsaha fast.

Seventh Lecture.

The Law teaches the consequences of virtue and sin. Following the true Law leads toward the state of gods, while turning away from it leads the great sinner to hell.

Ninth Lecture.

The true Law is the teaching in which Nami becomes a Svayamsambuddha. Nami's answers identify self-control, patience, carefulness, content, truth, austerity, and freedom from worldly ties with the path of the wise.

Tenth Lecture.

The best Law is difficult to encounter even for one who possesses all five organs of sense. People follow heretical teachers, and even those instructed in the right Law rarely believe in it or practise it.

Twelfth Lecture

Harikêsa says that the Law is his pond and teaches that living under self-restraint, avoiding injury, lying, taking what is not given, property, women, pride, and deceit is the proper path.

Thirteenth Lecture.

The Law is known by Brahmadatta, but he does not follow its counsel. Kitra has heard and practiced it, urging compassion, right conduct, and renunciation.

Fourteenth Lecture.

The Law is presented as the only lasting safeguard at death and as the path that prevents rebirth. Those who act according to it gain profit from the passing days and eventually reach perfection.

Fifteenth Lecture.

The Law is adopted by one who intends to live as a monk.

Sixteenth Lecture.

The Law is portrayed as the discipline in which a monk should remain steadfast, content, restrained, and attentive to the duties of chastity. It is called unchangeable, permanent, and eternal; the Ginas proclaimed it, and through it the Siddhas reached perfection.

Eighteenth Lecture.

The Law teaches that living beings and worldly possessions are transient, that sinful actions have consequences, and that one should practise penance, righteousness, and self-control. Sañgaya was filled with desire for purity after being taught the Law.

Abstract written by a model

The Law is a dharma practiced to avoid future suffering, become free from Karman, and reach Nirvâna. Its highest form is that of Kâsyapa, or Rishabha, and it requires self-control, penance, right conduct, faith, knowledge, meditation, equanimity, chastity, non-violence, truthfulness, non-possession, and freedom from attachment. It is a car that may yoke bad pupils, yet it inspires liberation, turns the soul from worldly pleasures toward houseless life, and through study and sincere practice destroys ignorance and Karman. It is loved and meditated upon with the Lêsyâs, Samitis, and Guptis.
