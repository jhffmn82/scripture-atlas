Abstract written by a model

In Chuang Tzŭ, Perfect Man is a spiritually transcendent person who embodies freedom from self, attachment, and worldly limitations. In Chuang Tzŭ, Sages of old are divine, passive beings whose virtue and hidden influence bring harmony, protection, and people toward TAO. In Chuang Tzŭ, Sage is the ideal person united with God and the Source, transcending distinctions and following natural order. In Chuang Tzŭ, the Sage is an uncrowned virtuous King who governs through natural influence, inaction, and accord with TAO.

Perfect Man in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where Perfect Man first appears

Abstract written by a model

Perfect Man is a spiritual being and a man in outward appearance, characterized by freedom from self, care, calculation, plans, anxiety, and attachment to outcomes. Beyond the limits of the external world, he transcends life and death and remains unaffected by extremes that harm ordinary people: a scorched ocean, frozen Milky Way, thunder-riven mountains, storms, fire, water, frost, sun, birds, and beasts. The pure men of old embody this way by accepting life, death, birth, and their allotted parts without love, hatred, or rejoicing, while remaining cheerful, grave, patient for the end, and in harmony with all creation. Perfect Man employs the mind as a mirror, receiving without retaining or refusing, and triumphs over matter without injury. Though unconditioned and inwardly divine, he undertakes affairs, accommodates matter, adapts to the external world, and tolerates others without clinging or losing purity. He possesses TAO, venerates ONE, confines himself to essentials, and finds peace by treating ceremonies and music as adventitious. His doctrine is unspoken; he guides like a shadow or echo and performs nothing beyond gazing at the universe. His virtue arises naturally, through discrimination between safety and danger rather than deliberate cultivation.

The Perfect Man is described as a spiritual being unaffected by heat, cold, thunder, storms, or what is bad for ordinary people. He passes beyond the external world, where death and life have no victory over him.

CHAPTER VI.

The pure men of old acted without calculation, plans, or attachment to success and failure. They accepted life and death without love or hatred, played their allotted parts cheerfully, and harmonized with all creation.

CHAPTER VII.

He employs his mind as a mirror: he grasps nothing, refuses nothing, receives without keeping, and triumphs over matter without injuring himself.

CHAPTER XI.

The perfect man is distinguished not by outward bodily difference but by seeing what is not to be seen and by spiritual freedom. His doctrine responds like a shadow to form or an echo to sound, guiding humanity without plans or entanglement.

CHAPTER XIII.

The perfect man places himself outside the universe and beyond all creation, where his soul is free from care. He apprehends TAO, remains in accord with virtue, and treats charity, duty, ceremonies, and music as adventitious.

CHAPTER XVII.

The man of perfect virtue cannot be harmed by fire, water, frost, sun, birds, or beasts because he discriminates between safety and danger. He remains happy in prosperous and adverse circumstances and is cautious about what he accepts and discards.

CHAPTER XXI.

The man of perfect virtue is described as outwardly human but inwardly divine, unconditioned yet able to engage with conditioned things, and pure yet tolerant of others. His virtue arises naturally rather than through deliberate cultivation.

CHAPTER XXII.

The Sage teaches a doctrine that does not find expression in words. The true Sage performs nothing beyond gazing at the universe, adapts to external circumstances without violating or clinging to relationships, and finds salvation in addition without gain and diminution without loss.

Sages of old in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where Sages of old first appears

Abstract written by a model

Sages of old are a group of divine yet passive beings whose essential quality is complete virtue through repose, simplicity, and accord with God and nature. They first obtain TAO for themselves and then for others, preserve pure spirituality, dwell in Non-Existence, and let their knowledge stop at the unknowable. Though inert and ignoring action, they exert a pervasive, protective influence: they ward off corruption, thrive crops, instil peace, benefit all people without distinction, and can protect those who speak against wrongdoing. Their influence remains concealed; they take no credit, establish no name, seek, lose, and reject nothing. Beginning in self-attainment and passivity, they move beyond conventional human concerns, poverty, rank, emoluments, honour, blame, plans, and artificial distinctions, until they treat all things as one. Divine to human beings but ordinary to God, they are at one with God and man, ride the glory of the sky, fulfil their destiny according to nature, rejoice in creation, and bring others toward TAO. They float through life and rest in death.

9 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER I.

The divine man ignores action and is described as inert, yet his influence fills creation, protects him from natural dangers, and causes crops to thrive.

CHAPTER IV.

They first obtained TAO for themselves before obtaining it for others. Their teachings can serve as a protective authority when a person speaks against wrongdoing.

Nan Po Tzŭ K'uei

Divine men are described as divine in relation to human beings but ordinary in relation to God. The meanest being in heaven is said to be better than the best being on earth.

Chiang Lü Mien went to see Chi Ch'ê,

The divine man rides upon the glory of the sky until his form cannot be discerned, fulfilling his destiny and acting according to his nature. He is at one with God and man, for whom affairs cease to exist and all things return to their original state.

CHAPTER XV.

The Sage possesses self-esteem without self-conceit, retirement without solitude, and health without hygiene. The Sage is passive and at repose, makes no plans, is unaffected by honour or blame, and floats through life to rest in death.

CHAPTER XVI.

The Sages may have lived outside mountains and forests, but their Virtue would still have remained hidden because the age was unsuitable. They preserved themselves by not exhausting the empire or Virtue with knowledge and by reverting to natural instincts.

CHAPTER XXIII.

The Sage abides where the tenuity of mortality shares the formless. He devotes himself to the natural and neglects the artificial, and his knowledge stops at the unknowable. His practice may bring no reputation, yet it throws a halo around the meanest.

Kuan Chung being at the point of death, Duke Huan went to see him.

The true Sage embraces the universe, benefits all without respect to persons, takes no credit for realities, and establishes no name for what has been done. The Sage seeks nothing, loses nothing, rejects nothing, and accords with nature.

CHAPTER XXV.

The true Sage influences others through concealed virtue, causing people to forget poverty, rank, and emoluments. He treats all things as one, remains unchanged amid action, ignores conventional distinctions, and moves in harmony with his generation.

Sage in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where Sage first appears

Abstract written by a model

Sage is the true or ideal man: one with God, essence, reality, and the Source, whose Unity remains intact through ten thousand years and even the universe’s passing. He ignores reputation, vulgar right and wrong, rank, precedence, and distinctions of this and that; contraries are identical to him. Adapting to Heaven, human beings, and environmental change, he transfers himself into the position of things viewed, keeps knowledge within himself rather than arguing, and blends all into one harmonious whole. Though turned out by God as a man, he soars beyond the cardinal points and can pass beyond worldly distinctions, the external world, and personal existence. In government he first rejects inquiry, then teaches inaction and natural order without self, following the ancient TAO instead of imposing a partial system.

the Sage in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where the Sage first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Sage is a man of complete virtue, an uncrowned King whose goodness covers the empire and influences all creation without self-awareness, deliberate display, or trace. He follows TAO, looks to God, cultivates virtue, remains natural and passive, and acts through inaction rather than plans or interference. In retirement when the empire lacks TAO, he avoids wealth, fame, power, and personal glory; when governing, he lets officials perform their functions, employs ability, hears the people, and keeps the empire at peace through natural influence. Repose gives him mental equilibrium and makes his mind a mirror of the universe; he can see light in darkness and hear harmony in stillness. He excels in great things, seeks refuge in God, gains freedom from harm, and dies for the world. After a thousand years of the weary world, he enters the kingdom of God and is secure in eternity.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VII.

His goodness covers the whole empire and influences all creation, while he himself seems unaware of it and leaves no conscious trace.

CHAPTER XI.

The Sage looks up to God, perfects virtue, follows TAO, identifies with charity, extends duty, responds to ceremony, undertakes affairs, applies law, relies on fellow-men, and accommodates himself to matter. His action is ultimately inaction rather than premeditated interference.

CHAPTER XII.

The true Sage follows TAO, cultivates virtue in retirement when necessary, and remains in harmony with all things. He does not strive for wealth, fame, success, or personal glory, and he can perceive light in darkness and harmony in stillness.

Chiang Lü Mien went to see Chi Ch'ê,

The Sage governs by allowing officials to perform their functions, employing ability, hearing the people's voice, and keeping the empire at peace. The Sage's government influences people naturally, while the Sage blushes when a filial son must administer medicine to a sick father.

CHAPTER XIII.

The Sage operates ceaselessly through repose, and his mind becomes a mirror of the universe. He cultivates essentials, remains free from care, and does not join struggles for power.

The walrus envies the centipede;

The Sages are associated with excelling in great things rather than in every small thing.

CHAPTER XIX.

The Sage seeks refuge in God and is therefore free from harm caused by objective existences. The Sage also pursues natural intelligence and virtue rather than artificial cunning.
