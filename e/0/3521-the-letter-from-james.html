The Letter from James

Abstract written by a model

This document is the Letter from James, a five-chapter exhortation written by James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ, to the twelve tribes in the Dispersion. Its central concern is that faith must show itself in conduct, and the people who matter most are the scattered believers addressed as brothers, set against the rich, the proud, and those who misuse speech or favor the wealthy. Chapter 1 opens by urging readers to count trials as joy because testing produces endurance, to ask God for wisdom without doubting, and to recognize that temptation comes from one's own lust, not from God, while every good gift comes from the Father of lights. It ends by calling them to be swift to hear, slow to speak and slow to anger, doers of the word and not only hearers, and to practice pure religion by visiting orphans and widows and staying unstained by the world. Chapter 2 forbids partiality, illustrated by the man in a gold ring favored over the poor man, and says partiality breaks the royal law to love one's neighbor. It then argues that faith without works is dead, citing the believing demons, Abraham, and Rahab, and concludes that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone. Chapter 3 warns against becoming teachers too readily and turns to the tongue, small like a bit or rudder yet able to set a forest ablaze, untameable, and wrong when it both blesses God and curses people. It contrasts earthly wisdom of jealousy and ambition with wisdom from above, pure, peaceable, and merciful. Chapter 4 traces quarrels to pleasures and lusts, calls friendship with the world hostility toward God, and commands submission to God, resisting the devil, drawing near to God, and humbling oneself. It forbids judging a brother and rebukes confident business planning, reminding readers life is a vapor and they should say 'If the Lord wills.' Chapter 5 warns the rich that their corroded wealth and withheld wages will testify against them, then urges patience until the Lord's coming, citing the farmer, the prophets, and Job. It forbids oaths and closes with prayer: for the suffering, for the sick anointed by elders, for mutual confession, with Elijah as an example and the hope of turning back a wandering sinner.

The Letter from James / Chapter 1

James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ, writes to the twelve tribes in the Dispersion. He urges them to count trials as joy because the testing of their faith produces endurance, and to ask God for wisdom in faith without doubting. He contrasts the humble and the rich, warns that temptation comes from a person's own lust and not from God, and says lust conceives sin, which produces death. He reminds them that every good gift comes from the Father of lights, who gave birth to them by the word of truth. He closes by telling them to be swift to hear, slow to speak and slow to anger, to be doers of the word and not only hearers, and to practice pure religion by visiting the fatherless and widows and keeping unstained by the world.

Chapter 2

Chapter 2 addresses the letter's readers, called brothers, and forbids them to hold the faith of the glorious Lord Jesus Christ with partiality. It illustrates this with a man in a gold ring and fine clothing who is given a good seat while a poor man in filthy clothing is told to stand or sit by a footstool, and it says God chose the poor to be rich in faith while the rich oppress and drag the readers into court. Partiality breaks the royal law, 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself,' and whoever stumbles in one point of the law is guilty of all, so the readers must speak and act as people judged by the law of freedom, since judgment is without mercy to those who show none. The chapter then argues that faith without works is dead, using the example of a brother or sister left unfed with only kind words, the demons who believe and shudder, Abraham who offered Isaac, and Rahab the prostitute who received the messengers. It concludes that a man is justified by works and not only by faith, as the body without the spirit is dead.

Chapter 3

The author addresses his brothers, warning that not many should become teachers because teachers face heavier judgment and everyone stumbles in many ways. He argues that the tongue, though a small member like a horse's bit or a ship's rudder, boasts great things and can set a whole forest ablaze, defiling the whole body. Mankind has tamed every kind of animal, bird, creeping thing, and sea creature, but nobody can tame the tongue, a restless evil full of deadly poison that both blesses God and curses people made in God's image. Using the images of a spring, a fig tree, and a vine, the author says blessing and cursing should not come from the same mouth. He closes by contrasting earthly wisdom, marked by jealousy and selfish ambition, with wisdom from above, which is pure, peaceable, and full of mercy, and whose fruit of righteousness is sown in peace by peacemakers.

Chapter 4

Chapter 4 addresses readers whose quarrels and fights arise from their pleasures and lusts, and who fail to receive because they ask with wrong motives. It calls them adulterers and adulteresses, warning that friendship with the world is hostility toward God, who resists the proud and gives grace to the humble. It then commands the readers to submit to God, resist the devil, draw near to God, cleanse their hands, purify their hearts, mourn, and humble themselves so that the Lord will exalt them. It forbids speaking against and judging a brother, since only one lawgiver is able to save and destroy. Finally, it rebukes those who confidently plan to travel, trade, and profit for a year, reminding them that life is a vapor, that they should say 'If the Lord wills,' and that knowing the good and not doing it is sin.

Chapter 5

Chapter 5 opens by warning the rich to weep and howl over coming miseries: their riches are corrupted, their gold and silver corroded, and the wages they withheld by fraud from the laborers who mowed their fields cry out to the Lord of Armies. The rich are accused of living in luxury and of condemning and murdering the righteous one, who does not resist them. The text then turns to the brothers, urging patience until the coming of the Lord, as a farmer waits for the rains, and pointing to the prophets and Job as examples of endurance. It forbids swearing oaths, telling them to let their yes be yes and their no be no. It closes by directing the suffering to pray, the sick to call the elders to pray and anoint them with oil, and all to confess sins and pray for one another, citing Elijah's prayer about rain and the saving of a sinner who wanders from the truth.
