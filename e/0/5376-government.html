Abstract written by a model

In The Table-Talk of the Prophet Mohammad (Part 2), Government is described as a trust from God whose holder will be questioned on the Day of Resurrection unless worthy, just, and good. In The Second Book of Nephi, government rests on a child’s shoulder and is forever ordered on David’s throne and kingdom through judgment and justice. In Chuang Tzŭ, government is criticized for interfering with natural dispositions and is said to be properly achieved through natural care and inaction. In The Analects of Confucius, Book XII (Yen Yuan), government is presented as rectification through leaders’ correctness, avoidance of killing, and desire for what is good. In The Analects of Confucius, Book XIII: Tsze-Lu, government is presented as leadership through personal example, labor for the people’s affairs, and the appointment of virtuous and talented officials. In Kojiki (Records of Ancient Matters), Volume III (Emperor Nin-toku to Empress Sui-ko), the government of the Empire is assumed by Princes Ohoke and Woke after Ohoke cedes the Empire to Woke.

government in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where government first appears

Abstract written by a model

Government governs the empire but is repeatedly criticized for interfering with natural dispositions and natural human life. It makes the mistake of artisans when it imposes artificial systems, and its rewards, punishments, coercion, and management disturb natural conditions, corrupt virtue, and produce dissatisfaction and disorder. It was introduced as Virtue declined, contributing to lost integrity. Its proper course is natural care: prevent harm, abstain from aggression, preserve inward sincerity, and rest in inaction. Government is achieved when people follow natural order without self. Though it is presented as a sequence descending from TAO and GOD through public functions to punishments, ceremonies and laws are the lowest governmental form and serve rulers through the ruled.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VII.

It is achieved when mental and physical energy are resolved into abstraction and inaction, and when people follow natural order without admitting the element of self.

CHAPTER IX.

The government of the empire is said to make the same mistake as Poh Loh by imposing artificial systems on natural human life. It is discussed as harmful when it departs from natural instincts and Tao.

CHAPTER XI.

Government is blamed for disturbing natural conditions, corrupting virtue, and producing disorder through rewards, punishments, coercion, and interference. The unit teaches that government should rest in inaction and avoid managing people as if they were mere things.

CHAPTER XIII.

Government is perfected by following the sequence from TAO and GOD through charity, public functions, forms, capacities, distinctions, and punishments. Ceremonies and laws are described as the lowest form of government and as instruments used by the ruled in service of rulers.

CHAPTER XVI.

Government was introduced as Virtue declined, alongside systems of moral reform. Its development contributed to the loss of natural adaptation and original integrity.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Government is compared with caring for horses and is said to require only that no harm come to those governed. The boy rejects elaborate governmental action, while Hsü Wu Kuei advises rulers to abstain from aggression and preserve inward sincerity.

government in The Analects of Confucius, Book XIII: Tsze-Lu

Confucius · the work’s page · read it where government first appears

Abstract written by a model

Government is presented as leadership through personal example and labor for the people’s affairs. It calls for employing the various officers, pardoning small faults, and raising men of virtue and talents to office. Its effectiveness depends chiefly on the correct personal conduct of a prince or minister: with it, orders need not be issued; without it, government is not effective. If the Master were employed, it could be perfected in three years. Good government makes those nearby happy and attracts those far away. It unfolds further as practical counsel: do not desire quick results or attend to small advantages, but act thoroughly and focus on great affairs. By contrast, men of the present day who engage in government are compared to pecks and hampers and are not worth taking into account.
