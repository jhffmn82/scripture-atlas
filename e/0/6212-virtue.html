Abstract written by a model

In Chuang Tzŭ, Virtue is a moral quality and spiritual grace exemplifying TAO, cultivated inwardly and expressed through natural, passive influence. In Chuang Tzŭ, TÊ is perfect spiritual integrity arising from spontaneity and natural virtue, preserved through sincerity and harmed by artificiality and passion. In The Analects of Confucius, Book XV (Wei Ling Kung), virtue is a vital personal responsibility that the determined scholar preserves even at the cost of life. In Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou), virtue is one of the two courses Confucius says can spread over the four seas like rushing water.

Virtue in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where Virtue first appears

Abstract written by a model

Virtue is a moral quality, inward and spiritual grace, and the exemplification of TAO. Genuine virtue proceeds from God, connects God and man, and enables things to be what they are. Its fullest form illuminates more excellently than suns and influences others passively yet irresistibly, without preaching, argument, power, or outward display. It is natural, spontaneous, unconscious, and expressed through inaction; in the wise ruler it is modeled upon the universe and guided by TAO. Cultivating the heart and nature leads beings back to virtue, toward an unconditioned alignment with the beginning of all things. Complete virtue appears outwardly without obligation, brings inward harmony and peaceful acceptance of changing conditions, and is necessary for an empire’s stability. It may be shared as true wisdom, inspire confidence, and support reputation, yet pursuit of fame, wealth, superiority, or intentional display damages it. Ill-regulated virtue ends in reputation and desire for fame. Dissatisfaction, discontent, interference with natural dispositions, outward-looking intention, emotions such as hate and ambition, and the five sources of injury obstruct it. When such influences do not run riot, the mind becomes ordered, reposeful, clear, unconditioned, and capable of inaction. Thus virtue unfolds from divine connection and inner cultivation to natural influence, while warning that deliberate striving and personal reward undermine it.

Virtue is weakened by dissatisfaction, discontent, and interference with natural dispositions. Without virtue, the empire cannot have stability.

CHAPTER XIII.

Virtue is associated with spontaneous action and with the proper ordering of essentials and accidentals. The wise ruler’s virtue is modeled upon the universe, guided by TAO, and expressed through inaction.

CHAPTER XIV.

Virtue is said to establish itself naturally, without undue effort. True virtue does nothing yet surpasses Yao and Shun, and its influence may extend through ten thousand generations without being recognized.

CHAPTER XXII.

Virtue is described as the exemplification of TAO. It cannot be reached directly, though it may beautify a person and is contrasted with charity, duty to one's neighbour, and ceremonies.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Virtue is entangled by hate, ambition, joy, anger, sorrow, and pleasure, and it is obstructed by rejecting, adopting, receiving, giving, knowledge, and ability. When these influences are not allowed to run riot, the mind becomes ordered, reposeful, clear, unconditioned, and capable of inaction. Virtue is also associated with spontaneous emotion arising from the real self.

TÊ in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where TÊ first appears

Abstract written by a model

TÊ is a state of spiritual integrity: a perfect, harmonious, all-embracing virtue of spontaneity that abides in the natural. Perfect virtue belongs to the Sage, who remains passive and at repose, without sorrow, anxiety, attachment to distinctions, or injury to others; through natural influence such a person may govern without aggression. TÊ is glorified by Life, while TAO is its sovereign lord; spontaneous emotion is called passive virtue, and emotion not evoked externally is active virtue. It is injured by trifles, lost through love and hate, and obstructed by passion, artificial moral standards, fame, coercion, government, goodness, art, and learning. Once all-embracing and giving rise to charity, Virtue declines through historical ages as people are led from TAO and natural instinct. It is preserved by sincerity that responds to circumstances without aggression.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

Chiang Lü Mien went to see Chi Ch'ê,

Perfect virtue is associated with governing by natural influence and with a person who has no thought in repose, no anxiety in action, and no attachment to distinctions of right and wrong or good and bad. Such a person has wealth and food without knowing their source and is followed by people who cling to him like lost children.

CHAPTER XV.

Virtue is complete when the Sage remains passive and at repose, free from sorrow, anxiety, and foul influences. Perfect virtue is also associated with a heart unconscious of sorrow and happiness.

CHAPTER XVI.

Virtue is harmony and was once all-embracing, giving rise to charity. As Virtue declined, coercion, government, goodness, art, and learning led man away from TAO and natural instinct.

CHAPTER XVII.

Virtue abides in the natural and develops from knowledge of the natural and the artificial. It should not be sacrificed to fame, and perfect virtue is associated with freedom from injury by external circumstances.

CHAPTER XXIII.

TÊ is the virtue of spontaneity and is glorified through life. TAO is its sovereign lord, while life manifests it. Emotion that is spontaneous is called virtue passive, and emotion not evoked by the external is called virtue active.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Natural virtue is obstructed when a ruler gives free play to passion or imposes artificial moral standards. Hsü Wu Kuei advises cultivating the sincerity within one's breast so it responds to circumstances without aggression.

virtue in The Analects of Confucius, Book XV (Wei Ling Kung)

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where virtue first appears

Abstract written by a model

Virtue is a topic known by only a few, yet more important to man than water or fire. Every man bears personal responsibility for it and must not yield its performance even to a teacher. The determined scholar and man of virtue preserve it complete, even at the cost of life. It enables a man to hold fast what he has gained; without enough, he loses it. Specious words confound virtue, and no one has been seen to love it as much as beauty. Though vital, its course has not been seen to cause a man’s death.
