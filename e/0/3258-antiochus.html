King Antiochus in The First Book of the Maccabees

author unknown · about 99 BC · the work’s page · read it where King Antiochus first appears

Abstract written by a model

King Antiochus Epiphanes is a ruthless ruler, described as a sinful root from Alexander's servants, son of King Antiochus and a former hostage at Rome. He began reigning in the 137th year of the Greeks from his royal city Antioch. He planned to rule both kingdoms, invaded Egypt, and defeated Ptolemy with a vast army. He then plundered Jerusalem and its temple, decreed that all become one people, appointed overseers, sent a tribute collector to Judah's cities, and enforced pagan worship on pain of death. Angered by Judas's fame, he found his treasury depleted by the dissension he caused, left Lysias in charge with orders to destroy Israel's strength and settle foreigners there, and went to Persia for money. Failing to pillage a rich city in Elymais, he fled to Babylon. Hearing of defeats in Judah, he fell sick with grief, admitted his evils at Jerusalem caused his misfortune, appointed Philip guardian of his young son Eupator, and died in the 149th year.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 1

Antiochus Epiphanes, son of King Antiochus and a former hostage at Rome, reigned from the 137th year of the Greeks. He invaded Egypt, defeated Ptolemy, then returned to plunder Jerusalem and its temple. Later he decreed that all his kingdom become one people, forsake their own laws, and obey on pain of death, and he enforced pagan worship in Judah.

Chapter 3

Antiochus is filled with indignation at Judas's fame and gathers all his forces, paying them a year in advance. Finding his treasury depleted and tribute small because of the unrest he caused, he goes to Persia to collect money, leaving Lysias in charge and taking half the forces. He leaves Antioch in the one hundred forty seventh year and crosses the Euphrates.

Chapter 6

King Antiochus fails to pillage a rich city in Elymais, Persia, and flees to Babylon. Hearing of the defeats in Judah, he falls sick with grief, admits his evils at Jerusalem brought this on him, names Philip to oversee his kingdom and son, and dies in the 149th year.

Antiochus in The Second Book of the Maccabees

author unknown · about 124 BC · the work’s page · read it where Antiochus first appears

Abstract written by a model

Antiochus is a king (also described as a prince and general) who succeeds Seleucus and rules from Antioch. The records portray him as arrogant, cruel, corruptible, a murderer and blasphemer. Early in his reign he assents to Jason's offer, is received at Jerusalem, then accepts Menelaus's higher bid for the high priesthood. He weeps over Onias's death and executes Andronicus at the place of the crime, yet is persuaded to acquit Menelaus and condemn his three accusers. On his second invasion of Egypt, believing Judea in revolt, he takes Jerusalem by force, orders a massacre, and plunders the temple of 1800 talents with Menelaus as guide, unaware that the Lord's anger over the people's sins was why he succeeded. He appoints governors Philip at Jerusalem, Andronicus at Gerizim and Menelaus, and sends Apollonius with 22,000 men. He then commands the seizure and torture of seven brothers and their mother; the youngest he tries to win with oaths of wealth and honor before treating him worse. The brothers warn of God's judgment. Driven from Persepolis after trying to rob a temple, he vows to make Jerusalem a graveyard, is struck with incurable bowel pain and a fall from his chariot, repents, writes a letter to the Jews, and dies in a strange land among the mountains. Another account has priests killing him by treachery in Nanaea's temple in Persia. His son succeeds him.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

5 sections; each heading opens the text

The Second Book of the Maccabees / Chapter 1

He came into the temple of Nanaea in Persia with an army and a few friends, on the pretense of marrying her so as to take a large part of the treasures as a dowry. The priests locked him in and killed him, cutting him and his company to pieces and throwing their heads to the people outside.

Chapter 4

Antiochus succeeded Seleucus as king, assented to Jason's offer, visited Jerusalem where Jason received him with torches and shouting, and later accepted Menelaus's higher bid for the high priesthood. He wept over Onias's death and had Andronicus stripped and executed at the place of the crime. He nevertheless was persuaded by Ptolemy son of Dorymenes to acquit Menelaus and sentence the three accusers to death.

Chapter 5

Antiochus made his second invasion of Egypt, then, thinking Judea in revolt, took Jerusalem by force, ordered a massacre, and plundered the temple of eighteen hundred talents with Menelaus as guide. He was arrogant and unaware that the Lord's anger over the people's sins was why he succeeded. He left governors (Philip, Andronicus, Menelaus) and sent Apollonius with an army against the Jews.

Chapter 7

Antiochus, the king, commands the seizure and torture of the seven brothers and their mother, becoming enraged at the spokesman and ordering pans and caldrons heated. He is astonished by the third brother's soul, then tries to win the youngest with oaths of wealth, honor, and friendship and presses the mother to persuade him. When this fails, he handles the youngest worse than the others, and the brothers warn that he will face God's judgment and punishment.

Chapter 9

Antiochus is driven out of Persia after trying to rob a temple at Persepolis, and in his anger vows to make Jerusalem a graveyard of Jews. Struck by God with a bowel affliction and a fall from his chariot, he rots in life, repents with promises to the Jews and the sanctuary, writes them a letter, and dies in a strange land among the mountains.
