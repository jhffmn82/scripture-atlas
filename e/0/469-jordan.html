Abstract written by a model

In Numbers, the Jordan is the boundary of the land sought by Reuben and Gad, which they agree their armed men will cross before the LORD. In The Book of Joshua, the Jordan overflows its banks, marks the boundary between eastern and western Israel, is crossed on dry ground, and holds twelve memorial stones. In The Book of Judges, the Gileadites hold the fords of the Jordan, where Ephraimite fugitives are tested and killed if they fail the pronunciation test. In The Second Book of Samuel, David and his people cross the Jordan by morning, followed afterward by Absalom and all the men of Israel. In The Second Book of Kings, the Jordan is where trees are cut, its waters part for Elijah and Elisha, and Naaman washes seven times and is healed. In Psalms, the Jordan is driven back at the Exodus and asked why it turned back. In The Gospel According to Matthew, the Jordan is where John baptizes confessing people and Jesus is baptized as the heavens open over him.

Jordan in The Book of Joshua

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where Jordan first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Jordan is a river that overflows all its banks at harvest time and serves as a boundary between the eastern tribes (Reuben, Gad, half of Manasseh) and the rest of Israel. In Joshua 1, Joshua and the people are told to cross it within three days. In chapter 3, as the priests' feet touch its edge, its waters stand in a heap far upstream at Adam, leaving dry ground. In chapter 4, the people cross, twelve stones are taken from and set in its midst, and the waters return over its banks. In chapter 22, it remains the dividing line, and the eastern tribes fear it will be said the river leaves them no portion in the LORD.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

The Book of Joshua / Chapter 1

The Jordan is the river that Joshua and all the people are told to cross into the land. Within three days the people are to pass over it. Moses had given the Reubenites, Gadites, and half-tribe of Manasseh land beyond the Jordan toward the sunrise.

Chapter 3

The Jordan is overflowing all its banks, as it does all the time of harvest. When the priests' feet dip into its edge, its waters coming down from above stand in one heap a great way off at Adam, near Zarethan, and the waters toward the Salt Sea are wholly cut off, leaving dry ground for the crossing.

Chapter 4

The Jordan's waters stayed cut off before the ark while the priests stood in its middle and the people crossed on dry land. Twelve stones were taken from its middle and twelve others were set up where the priests stood. When the priests came up, its waters returned to their place and went over all its banks as before.

Chapter 22

The Jordan is the boundary between the eastern tribes' land and the rest of Israel's land. The altar is built in the region by the Jordan, and the eastern tribes fear later generations will say the river leaves them no portion in the LORD.

Jordan in The Second Book of Kings

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where Jordan first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Jordan is a river in Israel, where wood-cutting trees grow and beams were cut. It first parts for Elijah's mantle, then for Elisha, who invokes the LORD. Later Elisha sends Naaman to wash there seven times; after scorning Israel's waters, he obeys and is healed.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 2

The Jordan is divided by Elijah's mantle-strike so he and Elisha cross on dry ground. After Elijah is taken, Elisha strikes it with the same mantle, invoking the LORD, the God of Elijah, and it divides again for him.

Chapter 5

The Jordan is the river in which Elisha instructs Naaman to wash seven times. Naaman at first scorns Israel's waters in favor of Abanah and Pharpar, the rivers of Damascus, but then dips himself seven times in the Jordan and is cleansed, his flesh restored like a little child's.
