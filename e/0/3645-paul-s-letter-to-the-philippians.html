Paul’s Letter to the Philippians

Abstract written by a model

This document is a four-chapter letter from Paul, writing with Timothy, to the saints, overseers and servants at Philippi. It is pastoral and affectionate, combining thanks, personal news and exhortation, and its recurring themes are joy, unity, humility and steadfast faith in Christ despite suffering. Chapter 1 opens with thanks for the Philippians' partnership in the Good News and a prayer that their love will grow in knowledge and discernment. Paul explains that his imprisonment has advanced the gospel, reaching the palace guard and emboldening other brothers, and he rejoices that Christ is proclaimed even by those motivated by envy. He is torn between dying to be with Christ and remaining for their sake, expects to remain, and urges them to stand firm and not fear adversaries. Chapter 2 calls them to unity and humility, using Christ as the model: he emptied himself, took a servant's form, died on the cross, and was exalted by God as Lord. Paul tells them to work out their salvation and shine as lights, and says he is glad even if poured out as a sacrifice. He hopes to send Timothy and is sending Epaphroditus, who nearly died for Christ's work, asking that he be received with honor. Chapter 3 warns against the dogs, evil workers and false circumcision. Paul lists his own credentials, including his Pharisee background and blamelessness under the law, but counts them a loss for Christ. He wants to know Christ, share his sufferings and attain the resurrection, pressing on toward the prize and asking them to imitate him. He contrasts enemies of the cross, whose end is destruction, with believers whose citizenship is in heaven. Chapter 4 urges them to stand firm and asks Euodia and Syntyche to agree. Paul commands rejoicing, gentleness, prayer instead of anxiety, and thinking on what is true and lovely, promising God's peace. He thanks them for their gift, brought through Epaphroditus, says he has learned contentment and can do all things through Christ, promises that God will supply every need, and closes with greetings, including from Caesar's household, and a blessing of grace.

Chapter 1

Paul, writing with Timothy, greets the saints in Christ Jesus at Philippi along with the overseers and servants, and thanks God for their partnership in the Good News. He prays that their love will abound in knowledge and discernment so they may be sincere and filled with the fruits of righteousness. Paul explains that his bonds have advanced the Good News, becoming known to the palace guard and emboldening most of the brothers, while some preach Christ from envy and others from good will, and he rejoices that Christ is proclaimed either way. He is torn between dying to be with Christ, which is far better, and remaining in the flesh for the Philippians' sake, and he expects to remain. He urges them to live worthily of the Good News, stand firm in one spirit, and not be frightened by adversaries, since they have been granted both belief and suffering on Christ's behalf.

Chapter 2

In this chapter the author urges the Philippian believers to be united, humble, and concerned for others, holding up Christ Jesus as the model. Christ, existing in the form of God, emptied himself, took the form of a servant, and became obedient to death on the cross, after which God exalted him so that every knee will bow and every tongue confess him as Lord. The author tells the believers to work out their salvation with fear and trembling, noting that God works in them, and to act without complaining so they shine as lights in a crooked generation. He says he is glad even if poured out as a sacrifice for their faith. He hopes to send Timothy soon, praising his proven service, and is sending Epaphroditus, who nearly died for Christ's work, asking that they receive him with joy and honor.

Chapter 3

In Chapter 3 the writer, who is Paul, urges his brothers to rejoice in the Lord and warns them against the dogs, the evil workers, and the false circumcision. He lists his own credentials in the flesh, including circumcision on the eighth day, the tribe of Benjamin, being a Pharisee, and being found blameless under the law, but counts them all a loss for the sake of Christ. He desires a righteousness from God through faith, to know Christ, the power of his resurrection and the fellowship of his sufferings, and to attain the resurrection from the dead. Paul says he has not yet obtained it but presses on toward the goal for the prize of God's high calling in Christ Jesus, and he asks the brothers to imitate him. He contrasts those who live as enemies of the cross, whose end is destruction, with believers whose citizenship is in heaven, where Christ will change their bodies to be like his glorious body.

Chapter 4

In Chapter 4, the author urges the beloved brothers to stand firm in the Lord and exhorts Euodia and Syntyche to think the same way, asking a true partner to help them. He commands the readers to rejoice always, be gentle, avoid anxiety by praying with thanksgiving, and think on whatever is true, honorable, just, pure, lovely, and of good report, promising that the peace of God will guard their hearts. He thanks the Philippians for reviving their concern for him, explaining that he has learned to be content in every circumstance and can do all things through Christ who strengthens him. He recalls that they alone shared with him in giving and receiving, even in Thessalonica, and that through Epaphroditus he received their gift as a pleasing sacrifice to God. He promises that God will supply every need of theirs, and closes with glory to God, greetings from the brothers and all the saints, especially Caesar's household, and a blessing of grace.
