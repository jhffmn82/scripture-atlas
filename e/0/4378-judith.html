Judith

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

This is the Book of Judith, sixteen chapters telling how the Israelite town of Bethulia is saved from an Assyrian invasion by a widow named Judith. Chapter 1 sets the stage: Nebuchadnezzar, king of the Assyrians at Nineveh, defeats and kills Arphaxad of the Medes after western peoples ignore his call for help, and he vows vengeance on them. In chapters 2 and 3 he sends his chief captain Holofernes with a vast army; it ravages nations, receives submissions from coastal peoples, and destroys their gods and groves so that Nebuchadnezzar alone is worshipped. Chapter 4 shows Judea fearing for Jerusalem and the temple; the high priest Joakim orders the hill passes at Bethulia held, and all Israel fasts and prays in sackcloth. In chapters 5 and 6 Achior, leader of the Ammonites, tells Holofernes that Israel can be beaten only if it has sinned against its God. Holofernes mocks him, declares Nebuchadnezzar the only god, and sends Achior to Bethulia, where the rulers Ozias, Chabris and Charmis welcome him. Chapter 7 has Holofernes besiege the town and cut off its water; after thirty-four days the thirsty people demand surrender, and Ozias asks for five more days. In chapter 8 Judith, a wealthy, devout widow, rebukes the rulers for testing God and announces a deed to be remembered through all generations. She prays in chapter 9 for God to strike the enemy by the hand of a woman. In chapters 10 to 12 she dresses beautifully, goes with her maid to the Assyrian camp, and wins Holofernes's trust with a false plan to lead him through Judea. She stays three days, then dines at his feast, where he drinks heavily. In chapter 13 she beheads the drunken Holofernes with his own sword and carries the head back to Bethulia, where Ozias blesses her. In chapter 14 the head is hung on the wall, Achior sees it and converts, is circumcised and joins Israel, and the Assyrians, finding Holofernes dead, fall into panic. Chapter 15 describes their flight, the pursuit and slaughter by Israel, the plunder of the camp, and Joakim's blessing; Holofernes's belongings are given to Judith, and she leads the women in dance. Chapter 16 closes with her song of thanksgiving, the worship and offerings at Jerusalem, and her dedication of Holofernes's goods to the Lord. She returns to Bethulia, stays unmarried, frees her maid, and dies at 105, buried with her husband Manasses, and Israel is left unthreatened in her lifetime and long after.

Judith / Chapter 1

Chapter 1 of Judith introduces Nebuchadnezzar, king of the Assyrians in Nineveh, and Arphaxad, king of the Medes in Ecbatana, whose city is described as fortified with massive walls, towers and gates. Nebuchadnezzar makes war on Arphaxad on the plain near Ragau and summons peoples from Persia, the west, Cilicia, Damascus, Samaria, Jerusalem and Egypt to join him. Those peoples disregard his summons and send his messengers away in disgrace, so Nebuchadnezzar swears by his throne and kingdom to take vengeance on them. In the seventeenth year he defeats Arphaxad's army, captures Ecbatana, plunders it, and kills Arphaxad with darts in the mountains of Ragau. He then returns to Nineveh with his multitude of soldiers and banquets for one hundred twenty days.

Chapter 2

In the eighteenth year, Nebuchadnezzar king of the Assyrians gathers his servants and great men, and they decree the destruction of all flesh that did not follow his word. He commissions Holofernes, his chief captain, to lead a vast army against the western country, demanding earth and water and ordering that resisters be slain and plundered. Holofernes musters 120,000 footmen and 12,000 mounted archers, with huge supplies of animals, rations, gold and silver, and sets out from Nineveh with a countless company of nations. His army destroys Put and Lud, crosses the Euphrates, ravages Mesopotamia, Cilicia, Midian and the plain of Damascus, and spreads fear among the coastal peoples of Sidon, Tyre, Sur, Ocina, Jemnaan, Azotus and Ascalon.

Chapter 3

Messengers are sent to Holofernes with words of peace, declaring that they are servants of Nebuchadnezzar the great king and offering him their dwellings, country, fields, flocks, herds and cities to use as he pleases. Holofernes comes down toward the sea coast with his army, sets garrisons in the high cities, and takes chosen men from them as allies. The people receive him with garlands, dances and timbrels. Despite this welcome, he casts down their borders and cuts down their sacred groves, having been given the task of destroying all the gods of the land so that all nations would worship Nebuchadnezzar only. He then moves toward Esdraelon near Dotaea, opposite the great ridge of Judea, and encamps between Geba and Scythopolis for a whole month to gather the baggage of his army.

Chapter 4

The children of Israel in Judea hear of all that Holofernes, chief captain of Nebuchadnezzar king of the Assyrians, has done to the nations, and they fear for Jerusalem and the temple, which was recently re-sanctified after the captivity. They send word through Samaria and nearby places, occupy the mountaintops, fortify villages, and store supplies. Joakim the high priest writes to those in Bethulia and Betomesthaim to seize the ascents of the hill country, since the narrow approach there makes it easy to stop an invader, and the people and the senate obey. All Israel, including women, children, cattle, and servants, puts on sackcloth, prostrates before the temple, and cries to God not to give their families and sanctuary over to the nations. The Lord hears them, and the people keep fasting many days while Joakim and the priests, in sackcloth, offer the continual burnt offering and the people's vows and gifts.

Chapter 5

Holofernes, chief captain of the army of Asshur, learns that the children of Israel have fortified the hill country and prepared for war, and he angrily demands that the princes of Moab, the captains of Ammon, and the governors of the sea coast explain who these people are. Achior, leader of the children of Ammon, answers by recounting Israel's history: their descent from the Chaldeans, their worship of the God of heaven, their sojourns in Mesopotamia, Canaan, and Egypt, their deliverance through the Red Sea, and their conquest of the hill country. He explains that they prospered while they did not sin and were destroyed and taken captive when they left God's way, and that they have now returned to their God and resettled Jerusalem. Achior advises Holofernes to attack only if the people have sinned, and otherwise to pass by, lest their God defend them. The people around the tent complain, and Holofernes' great men and those of Moab and the sea coast say Achior should be cut to pieces, claiming Israel has no power or might and will be prey for the army.

Chapter 6

After the council uproar subsides, Holofernes, chief captain of Asshur's army, mocks Achior and the Moabites for prophesying that Israel's God will defend Israel, declares that Nebuchadnezzar is the only god, and vows that Israel will be destroyed. He orders his servants to take Achior to Bethulia and hand him over to the children of Israel, so that Achior will share their fate. The servants bring Achior to the springs below Bethulia, where slingers drive them off, and they bind him and leave him at the foot of the hill. The Israelites free him and bring him to the city's rulers, Ozias, Chabris, and Charmis, and Achior reports Holofernes' words to the assembly. The people worship and pray to God, comfort and praise Achior, and Ozias hosts him and the elders at a feast while they call on God all night.

Chapter 7

Holofernes orders his huge army to move against Bethulia, where it camps in the valley and seizes the passes. The children of Israel are terrified and keep watch all night. Holofernes inspects the ascents and springs, and the rulers of Esau, Moab and the sea coast advise him to besiege the city and cut off its water rather than fight. He agrees, and the Ammonites, Assyrians and Esau's children take the springs and surround the city. After thirty-four days the cisterns are dry and the people, faint with thirst, demand that Ozias surrender; he asks for five more days for God to show mercy, and promises to yield if no help comes.

Chapter 8

Judith, a wealthy and devout widow of Bethulia whose husband Manasses died during barley harvest, hears that Ozias has sworn to deliver the city to the Assyrians after five days if God does not help. She summons Ozias, Chabris, and Charmis and rebukes them for testing God and putting themselves in his place, urging the people to wait for God's salvation and to see the trial as discipline like that of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Ozias praises her wisdom but says the thirsty people forced the oath and asks her to pray for rain. Judith replies that she will do a deed remembered through all generations, instructs them to stand at the gate that night, and forbids them to inquire about it. Ozias and the rulers bless her and return to their stations.

Chapter 9

Judith falls on her face, puts ashes on her head, and uncovers her sackcloth as the evening incense is offered at Jerusalem, then cries out to the Lord. She recalls how the Lord gave her father Simeon a sword to avenge a violated virgin, and asks to be heard as a widow. She describes the Assyrians as proud, trusting in horses, riders, footmen, shield, spear, bow, and sling, and as intending to defile the sanctuary and destroy the horn of the altar. She asks God to break their strength, to strike servant and prince by the deceit of her lips, and to use the hand of a woman. She closes by asking that every nation and tribe know that no one but the Lord protects the race of Israel.

Chapter 10

After finishing her prayer, Judith removes her widow's garments and sackcloth, bathes, and dresses in her finest clothing and jewelry to make herself very beautiful. With her maid, carrying wine, oil, roasted grain, figs, and bread, she goes to the gate of Bethulia, where Ozias, Chabris, and Charmis bless her and have the gate opened. In the valley an Assyrian watch stops her, and she says she is a Hebrew fleeing because her people are about to be consumed, and that she will show Holofernes a way to win the hill country. Struck by her beauty, the Assyrians send a hundred men to escort her to the tent of Holofernes, and the whole camp gathers around her. Holofernes comes out with silver lamps before him, and Judith bows down to him before his servants raise her up.

Chapter 11

Holofernes reassures Judith that she will be safe and well treated if she has chosen to serve Nebuchadnezzar, and asks why she fled her people. Judith flatters him and claims that her people can only be defeated if they sin against their God. She says they have run short of food and water and mean to eat livestock and the sanctified first fruits, tithes of wine and oil, and that they have sent to Jerusalem for the elders' permission. She claims that once they do this they will be delivered to Holofernes, and she offers to stay, pray at night in the valley for a sign of their sin, and then lead him through Judea to Jerusalem. Holofernes and his servants are pleased and amazed by her beauty and wisdom, and Holofernes promises that if she does as she says, her God will be his God and she will dwell in Nebuchadnezzar's palace.

Chapter 12

Holofernes has Judith brought to where his silver vessels are set and offers her his own food and wine, but she declines, saying her own provisions will suffice until the Lord works through her hand. She stays in the camp three days, going out each night to the valley of Bethulia to wash at the fountain and pray that God direct her way. On the fourth day Holofernes holds a feast for his servants only and sends Bagoas the eunuch to persuade her to join them. Judith agrees, dresses in her finery, and sits on fleeces laid out beside Holofernes, who is captivated and has desired her since first seeing her. She eats and drinks what her servant prepared, and Holofernes, delighted, drinks more wine than he ever had in a single day.

Chapter 13

In the evening, after a long feast, Bagoas shuts the tent and the servants leave Judith alone with Holofernes, who lies drunk on his bed. Judith prays to the Lord God, takes Holofernes' sword from the bedpost, and cuts off his head with two blows to the neck. She gives the head to her maid, who puts it in the food bag, and the two pass through the camp as if going to prayer and reach the gates of Bethulia. Judith announces God's victory to the watchmen and the gathered people, shows the head and the canopy, and says Holofernes did not defile her. The people worship God, and Ozias blesses Judith above all women and praises her for not sparing her life for her people.

Chapter 14

Judith tells her kindred to hang Holofernes' head on the city wall and instructs them to arm themselves at dawn and feign a descent toward the Assyrian watch, so that the Assyrians will rouse their captains, fail to find Holofernes, flee, and be pursued across Israel. She has Achior the Ammonite summoned; he faints at the sight of the head, then bows to her, hears her account of her deeds, and comes to believe in God, is circumcised, and joins the house of Israel. At morning the people hang the head on the wall and go out in bands to the mountain ascents. The Assyrians send word to their leaders, who come to Holofernes' tent, and Bagoas enters and finds Holofernes dead and headless on the threshold. Bagoas finds Judith gone, cries out that a Hebrew woman has shamed the house of King Nebuchadnezzar, and the Assyrian camp falls into uproar and mourning.

Chapter 15

Those in the enemy tents hear what has happened, are seized with fear, and flee across the plain and hill country. Ozias sends word to Betomasthaim, Bebai, Chobai, Chola, and every border of Israel, and the children of Israel, joined by people from Jerusalem, Gilead, and Galilee, pursue and slaughter the enemy past Damascus. The people of Bethulia plunder the camp of Asshur and are greatly enriched, and the villages and cities of the hill country and plain also take many spoils. Joakim the high priest and the elders from Jerusalem come to see Judith, bless her as the glory of Israel, and the people answer 'Amen.' After thirty days of plunder, Holofernes' tent and furnishings are given to Judith, and she leads the women of Israel in a dance with olive garlands while the armed men follow singing.

Chapter 16

Judith leads all Israel in a song of thanksgiving, recounting how the Lord destroyed Asshur's vast army by the hand of a woman, as she used her beauty and a deception to make the enemy leader weak and strike his neck with the sword. The song praises the Lord as creator and merciful to those who fear him, and warns that nations rising against Israel will suffer judgment. On reaching Jerusalem, the people worship, are purified, and offer burnt offerings, free will offerings, and gifts, while Judith dedicates Holofernes' belongings and the canopy to the Lord. After three months of feasting, Judith returns to Bethulia, remains unmarried and honored, frees her maid, and dies at 105, buried in the cave of her husband Manasses and mourned for seven days. No one frightened Israel during her days or for a long time after.
