Abstract written by a model

In The Tao Teh King, or The Tao and Its Characteristics, Tao is the enduring, unnamed yet nameable way that originates, nourishes, forms, and completes all things while acting without self-display or control. In The Tao Teh King, or The Tao and Its Characteristics, the Great Tao (Way or Method) ceased to be observed. In The Tao Teh King, or The Tao and Its Characteristics, the Way of Heaven lowers what is high, raises what is low, diminishes superabundance, supplements deficiency, and always stands with the good man. In Chuang Tzŭ, TAO is the universal, eternal, formless, unconditioned principle that gives rise to and pervades all things while remaining beyond material existence and deliberate action. In Chuang Tzŭ, The Way is another name for TAO in Lao Tzŭ's teachings, a metaphorical clue to all things human and divine rather than a road men walk. In Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), the way preserves mental constitution, nourishes nature, cultivates character, and serves Heaven without distraction by death or longevity.

Lao-Tse · the work’s page · read it where Tao first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tao is the enduring, unchanging, unnamed yet nameable way or course: undefined, complete, formless, still, alone, all-pervading, inexhaustible, and existing before Heaven and Earth. Conceived without a name, it originates heaven and earth; conceived with a name, it is the mother of all things. It is unseen, unheard, untouchable, indeterminable, and indescribable, the Form of the Formless and Semblance of the Invisible, yet it acts ceaselessly and does not pass away. Tao produces, nourishes, forms, completes, matures, maintains, clothes, and overspreads all things, while claiming no possession, credit, ability, lordship, or control. Its operation is mysterious, proceeding by contraries and marked by weakness; it does nothing for the sake of doing, so that nothing is left undone. It may seem insipid, hidden, humble, incomplete, or inferior, but skilfully supplies what all things need and makes them complete. Tao is the standard for personal conduct and government: agreement with it brings attainment, long endurance, and protection from decay; disregarding it brings an end, mistrust, and war. It is nursed within oneself and extends through family, neighbourhood, state, and kingdom. Valued as a nursing-mother and the most honoured of things, it is sought through diminishing doing, silence, inward harmony, humility, kindness, and action without self-display or contention.

TAO in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where TAO first appears

Abstract written by a model

TAO is the universal, eternal, formless, unconditioned principle, the Way, order, source, and clue to all things human and divine. Existing before heaven and earth without beginning or end, it pervades, covers, supports, nourishes, and produces all creation while remaining beyond material existence, fixed names, speech, form, and deliberate action. It gives rise to form, life, spirituality, fullness and emptiness, renewal and decay, beginnings and ends; from its perspective opposites such as life and death, success and failure, greatness and smallness, value and worthlessness lose their fixed distinction. What matters most is unity with this indivisible ONE. TAO cannot be secured by knowledge, worldly arts, skill, moral schemes, artificial speech, reputation, profit, or self-conscious striving. It is obscured by limited understanding, distinctions, conflict, obstruction, and the fragmentation of its unity. Its approach requires repose, stillness, purity, simplicity, fasting of the heart, spontaneity, inaction, inward endowment, outward correctness, and freedom from personal wisdom and ambition. Though it cannot be fully conveyed, named, given, or received through external instruction, it is known naturally and is everywhere. The account unfolds from TAO as a metaphorical Way and hidden principle, through its operation in natural action, Sagehood, self-culture, government, and the transformations of life and death. It contrasts TAO with charity, duty, technical skill, worldly learning, and divided doctrines; presents it as the basis of invisible government and the sovereign lord of TÊ; then increasingly stresses its absolute, omnipresent, paradoxical character. It ends by warning that specialists and thinkers such as Hui Tzŭ may investigate creation yet fail to conclude in TAO, whose unified perspective resists fragmentation.

TAO is presented as the domain containing a fixed point beyond the opposing conditions of worldly life. It enables one to move above mortality, remain without special effort, and harmonize with surroundings. Its expression through TÊ is concealed, purposeless, and unconcerned with merit or fame.

CHAPTER XXI.

TAO cannot be reached by superficial workers or by those who value worldly distinctions, and it is not adequately conveyed through speech or formal morality. It enables people to remain unmoved by change, rank, gain, loss, life, and death, and is associated with perfect goodness, happiness, and inner freedom.

CHAPTER XXII.

TAO cannot be brought, reached, heard, seen, spoken, or given a name, and it is not attained through knowledge. It is omnipresent and exists in all things, while causing fullness and emptiness, renewal and decay, beginning and ending, accumulation and dispersion without being any of them. Its operation is spontaneous, and the unit associates attainment of it with inaction, silence, and non-discussion.

CHAPTER XXIII.

TAO operates without a visible agent, remains the same through different spheres of action, and can be divided without ceasing to be entire. It is infinite as Space and Time, unconditioned, and the source from which existence and all subdivisions proceed. TAO is the sovereign lord of TÊ, and following it requires spontaneity, inaction, and freedom from the twenty-four disturbances and obstructions.

CHAPTER XXIV.

TAO is presented as something that cannot be reached through fixed moral schemes, cleverness, or demonstrations of unusual powers. The boy knows where TAO abides, and Lu Chü's account of tuned lutes illustrates TAO as harmony rather than mere technical effect.
