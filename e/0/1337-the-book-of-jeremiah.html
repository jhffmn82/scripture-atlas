The Book of Jeremiah

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

This is the book of Jeremiah, 52 chapters of prophecy and narrative centered on Jeremiah, son of Hilkiah, a priest from Anathoth called by the LORD before birth to be prophet to the nations. Despite protesting he is a child, he is made a fortified city and given the LORD's words, ministering under Josiah, Jehoiakim, and Zedekiah until Jerusalem's fall. The opening chapters charge Judah with abandoning the LORD, the spring of living waters, for Baal and idols, likening Israel to an unfaithful wife. Repeated calls to return are paired with warnings of an invader from the north, false prophets crying 'Peace, peace,' and condemnation of temple complacency, child burning at Topheth, and injustice. Jeremiah is forbidden to intercede, laments his own persecution, and enacts signs: the ruined linen belt, the potter's clay, the broken container. Kings Shallum, Jehoiakim, and Coniah are condemned, while promises appear of a righteous Branch for David, a regathered remnant, a new covenant written on hearts, and the restored city. The story turns to conflict and siege: Pashhur puts Jeremiah in the stocks, Jehoiakim burns the scroll Baruch wrote, and Hananiah's false two-year promise ends in his death. Jeremiah urges submission to Nebuchadnezzar, writes to exiles to seek Babylon's peace for seventy years, and buys a field at Anathoth as a sign of future restoration. Under Zedekiah he is imprisoned, thrown into a dungeon, and rescued by Ebedmelech. Jerusalem falls, Zedekiah is blinded, and the temple is burned. Gedaliah is made governor but murdered by Ishmael; Johanan and the remnant ignore the LORD's word through Jeremiah and flee to Egypt, taking him and Baruch along, where they keep worshiping the queen of the sky and are warned of judgment. After a word to Baruch, oracles against the nations follow: Egypt, Philistines, Moab, Ammon, Edom, Damascus, Kedar, Hazor, Elam, and at length Babylon, whose doom Seraiah is to read and sink in the Euphrates. The book ends with a historical account of Jerusalem's destruction, the deportations, and Jehoiachin's release from prison by Evilmerodach.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

52 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 1

Chapter 1 introduces Jeremiah, son of Hilkiah, a priest from Anathoth in Benjamin, whose prophetic ministry spans the reigns of Josiah, Jehoiakim, and Zedekiah until Jerusalem's captivity in the fifth month. The LORD tells Jeremiah that he knew and sanctified him before birth and appointed him a prophet to the nations. Jeremiah protests that he is a child who cannot speak, but the LORD commands him to go where sent, touches his mouth, and puts his words there, setting him over nations and kingdoms to uproot, tear down, destroy, overthrow, build, and plant. Through the visions of an almond branch and a boiling cauldron tipping from the north, the LORD declares he watches over his word and will bring evil from the north on the inhabitants of the land. The LORD says he will call the northern kingdoms to besiege Jerusalem and Judah's cities because of idolatry, and he makes Jeremiah a fortified city, iron pillar, and bronze walls, promising that his opponents will fight him but not prevail.

Chapter 2

In Jeremiah 2 the LORD sends a message to Jerusalem, the house of Jacob, and Israel, recalling Israel's early devotion as a bride in the wilderness and holiness to him. He charges that the fathers, priests, law-handlers, rulers, and prophets abandoned him for Baal and worthless things, trading their glory for what doesn't profit. He says his people committed two evils: forsaking him, the spring of living waters, and digging broken cisterns. Israel has suffered ruin and captivity, yet seeks help from Egypt and Assyria, and denies guilt while pursuing idols under every green tree. The LORD announces he will contend and judge them, saying the gods they trust cannot save them and that they will be ashamed of Egypt as they were of Assyria.

Chapter 3

In Chapter 3, the LORD appeals to unfaithful Israel and Judah using the image of a divorced wife who has gone to other men, accusing the people of polluting the land with prostitution and wickedness. In the days of King Josiah, the LORD recounts that backsliding Israel committed adultery on every high mountain and under every green tree and was given a certificate of divorce, yet treacherous Judah saw this and also played the prostitute, returning only in pretense. The LORD commands a proclamation toward the north inviting Israel to return, acknowledge her iniquity, and receive mercy, promising to bring a remnant to Zion, give shepherds after his heart, and call Jerusalem 'The LORD's Throne' where all nations gather. He foresees Judah and Israel walking together out of the north to the inherited land. The chapter closes with a voice on the bare heights weeping and confessing, as the people admit that salvation is in the LORD alone and lie down in shame for sinning from their youth.

Chapter 4

In this chapter the LORD calls Israel to return to him and put away abominations, and urges the men of Judah and Jerusalem to break up fallow ground and circumcise their hearts lest his wrath burn like fire. He announces evil and great destruction coming from the north, described as a lion and destroyer of nations, and the people are told to blow the trumpet, flee to fortified cities, and mourn in sackcloth. The prophet protests that the people were deceived with promises of peace, laments his anguish at the sound of war, and sees a vision of the earth returned to waste and void. The LORD declares the people foolish and skilled at evil, says the land will be desolate though he will not make a full end, and that he will not relent. Jerusalem, pictured as a woman adorning herself in vain, is despised by her lovers and cries out like a woman in labor before murderers.

Chapter 5

In Chapter 5, the LORD sends a search through the streets of Jerusalem for a single just person who seeks truth, and finds the people, both poor and great, have refused correction, sworn falsely, and broken the yoke. The LORD declares that the houses of Israel and Judah have acted treacherously, committing adultery, denying him, and trusting in false prophets who say no evil will come. He announces judgment: a lion, wolf, and leopard, and then a mighty, ancient nation of unknown language that will devour harvests, flocks, and fortified cities. Even so, he promises not to make a full end of them, and says that as they served foreign gods in their land, they will serve strangers in a land not theirs. The chapter closes by condemning wicked men who trap others and grow rich through deceit while ignoring the fatherless and needy, and by lamenting that prophets prophesy falsely, priests rule by their own authority, and the people love it so.

Chapter 6

Chapter 6 warns the children of Benjamin to flee Jerusalem because evil is coming from the north. The LORD of Armies commands that trees be cut down and a siege mound built against Jerusalem, the city to be visited because it is filled with oppression, violence, and wickedness. The speaker laments that the people's ears are uncircumcised, that prophets and priests deal falsely and cry 'Peace, peace!' when there is no peace, and that the people refused the good old paths and the watchmen's trumpet. The LORD declares their offerings unacceptable and describes a cruel people from the north, armed with bow and spear and riding horses, who will come against the daughter of Zion. The chapter ends with the people mourning in terror and the LORD calling them rejected silver, since refining cannot remove their wickedness.

Chapter 7

The LORD commands Jeremiah to stand at the gate of the LORD's house and tell the people of Judah to amend their ways rather than trust in the slogan 'the LORD's temple.' The LORD condemns their theft, murder, adultery, false swearing, and worship of Baal and other gods, calls the temple a den of robbers, and warns that he will treat it as he treated Shiloh. He forbids Jeremiah to intercede for the people, denounces their cakes for the queen of the sky and their rejection of the prophets, and says they have done worse than their fathers. The chapter ends with the abomination of Topheth, where children are burned, and with the prediction that it will become the valley of Slaughter, the dead will be unburied food for birds and animals, and the land will become a waste with all joy silenced.

Chapter 8

Chapter 8 records the LORD's judgment on the people of Jerusalem and Judah for persistent backsliding and deceit. The bones of kings, princes, priests, prophets, and inhabitants will be dug from their graves and spread before the sun, moon, and army of the sky they worshiped, and survivors in exile will prefer death to life. The LORD contrasts his people's refusal to return with the stork, turtledove, swallow, and crane that know their seasons, and condemns the scribes' false pen, the wise men, and the prophets and priests who cry 'Peace, peace' when there is no peace. An invading enemy, heard from Dan with snorting horses, will devour the land, and the LORD will send unchARMable serpents among them. The speaker laments the people's hurt and asks why there is no healing when there is balm and a physician in Gilead.

Chapter 9

In Chapter 9 the speaker laments the slain of the daughter of his people and wishes to flee from a people he calls adulterers and treacherous men. The LORD describes a society full of lies, slander, and betrayal among neighbors and brothers, who refuse to know him, and he declares he will melt and test them and punish them. He announces that Jerusalem will become heaps and the cities of Judah a desolation, because the people forsook his law and followed their own stubbornness and the Baals. He promises wormwood, poisoned water, scattering among the nations, and the sword, and calls for mourning women to wail as death enters windows and palaces. The chapter closes by saying that the wise, mighty, and rich should not glory in those things but in knowing the LORD, who exercises loving kindness, justice, and righteousness, and by announcing punishment on Egypt, Judah, Edom, Ammon, Moab, and all Israel, who are uncircumcised in flesh or heart.

Chapter 10

The LORD addresses the house of Israel, warning them not to learn the ways of the nations or fear the signs of the sky, since the nations' idols are only wood decked with silver and gold, unable to speak, move, do good or do evil. In contrast, the LORD is the true, living God and everlasting King, who made the earth by his power and controls the weather, and the gods who did not make the heavens and earth will perish. The text then turns to the people under siege, who are told to gather their wares because the LORD will sling out the inhabitants of the land. A lamenting voice mourns a serious wound, a destroyed tent, and lost children, blaming the shepherds who did not inquire of the LORD, and announces news of a commotion from the north that will make Judah's cities a desolation. The speaker admits that man cannot direct his own steps, asks the LORD to correct him gently, and asks that wrath be poured out on the nations that devoured Jacob.

Chapter 11

The LORD commands Jeremiah to proclaim the words of the covenant to the men of Judah and the inhabitants of Jerusalem, recalling the covenant made with their fathers when he brought them out of Egypt. Because Judah and Israel broke it by following other gods and Baal, the LORD announces unavoidable evil, forbids Jeremiah to pray for the people, and says their gods will not save them. Jeremiah learns that people are plotting to kill him and, likening himself to a gentle lamb led to slaughter, entrusts his cause to the LORD. The LORD answers by pronouncing punishment on the men of Anathoth who seek Jeremiah's life, saying their young men will die by the sword, their children by famine, and no remnant will remain.

Chapter 12

In Chapter 12 a speaker complains to the LORD that the wicked prosper and are at ease, honoring God with their mouths but not their hearts, and asks that they be pulled out like sheep for slaughter. He also asks how long the land will mourn. The LORD answers that the speaker must prepare for harder trials, since even his brothers and his father's house have betrayed him. The LORD then laments that he has forsaken his house and given his beloved heritage to her enemies, so that many shepherds have made his vineyard a desolate wilderness. Finally the LORD declares he will pluck up his evil neighbors who touch Israel's inheritance and then have compassion on them, restoring them if they learn to swear by his name, but destroying any nation that will not listen.

Chapter 13

In Chapter 13 the LORD commands the narrator to buy a linen belt, wear it, and later hide it in a cleft of rock by the Euphrates. After many days he digs it up and finds it ruined and profitable for nothing. The LORD explains that this is how he will ruin the pride of Judah and Jerusalem, whose evil people refuse to hear him and follow other gods, though he had made Israel and Judah cling to him as a belt clings to a waist. The narrator is told to announce that all will be filled with drunkenness and dashed against one another without pity, and he urges the people to humble themselves before darkness comes. The chapter ends with the king and queen mother warned of lost crowns, Judah carried away captive from the north, and Jerusalem rebuked for its iniquity, abominations, and shame.

Chapter 14

Chapter 14 opens with the LORD's word to Jeremiah concerning a drought that devastates Judah and Jerusalem: nobles' children find empty cisterns, plowmen cover their heads, and the doe and wild donkeys fail for lack of grass. The people confess their sins and plead for the LORD to act for his name's sake and not leave them, but the LORD says they have loved to wander and refuses to accept them, forbidding Jeremiah to pray for them. Jeremiah objects that the prophets promise assured peace, and the LORD answers that these prophets speak lies he never sent, and that they and their hearers will die by sword and famine. Jeremiah is told to weep for the slain and famine-stricken, and the chapter ends with a communal prayer acknowledging wickedness, asking the LORD not to break his covenant, and trusting him as the one who can send rain.

Chapter 15

In Jeremiah 15 the LORD tells Jeremiah that even if Moses and Samuel interceded, he would not relent toward Judah's people, and he decrees death, sword, famine, and captivity for them, with the sword, dogs, birds, and animals as four destroyers, because of Manasseh's deeds in Jerusalem. He describes Jerusalem's loss of children, multiplied widows, and sudden terror, and says he is weary of showing compassion because the people rejected him and did not return. Jeremiah laments being a man of strife whom everyone curses, asks the LORD to avenge him, recalls delighting in the LORD's words, and complains that his pain is perpetual and that the LORD seems like a deceitful brook. The LORD responds that if Jeremiah returns and separates the precious from the vile he will be as the LORD's mouth, and promises to make him a fortified bronze wall and to deliver and redeem him from the wicked.

Chapter 16

The LORD commands the prophet, addressed as 'you', not to marry, have children, or attend houses of mourning or feasting in this place, as a sign that the children, parents, great and small will die by sword and famine, unlamented and unburied. The LORD declares he will end the voices of mirth, bridegroom and bride, and has withdrawn his peace, loving kindness and mercy from this people. When the people ask why, the prophet is to answer that they and their fathers forsook the LORD, served other gods, and that the present generation has done worse by following their stubborn evil hearts. Therefore the LORD will cast them into a land they and their fathers have not known, where they will serve other gods, and will repay their iniquity double for polluting his land. Yet he promises a future in which he is known as the one who brought Israel back from the north and all countries, restoring them to their land, and the speaker declares that the nations will come to the LORD and renounce their fathers' worthless gods.

Chapter 17

Chapter 17 opens with the LORD declaring that the sin of Judah is engraved on their hearts and altars, and that Judah will lose its treasures and heritage and serve enemies in an unknown land. It contrasts the cursed man who trusts in man, who is like a desert bush, with the blessed man who trusts in the LORD, who is like a tree planted by water. The LORD says he searches the heart and repays each person according to their deeds. The speaker prays for healing and refuge, complains that people mock him about the LORD's word, and asks that his persecutors be destroyed. The LORD then sends him to the gates of Jerusalem to tell the kings and people to keep the Sabbath holy and carry no burden, promising lasting blessing for obedience and a devouring, unquenched fire in the gates for disobedience.

Chapter 18

The LORD tells Jeremiah to go to the potter's house, where he watches the potter remake a marred clay vessel into another one. The LORD explains that, like clay in the potter's hand, the house of Israel is in his hand, and that any nation he threatens with destruction may be spared if it turns from evil, while any nation he promises to build may lose that good if it disobeys. Jeremiah is to tell the men of Judah and the inhabitants of Jerusalem to turn from their evil ways, but they answer that it is in vain and that they will follow their own plans. The LORD condemns Israel for forgetting him, burning incense to false gods, and leaving the ancient paths, and says he will scatter them before the enemy. Jeremiah's opponents plot to strike him with the tongue and ignore his words, and Jeremiah prays that the LORD hear him, remember that he spoke good for them, and deal with them in anger without forgiving their sin.

Chapter 19

The LORD commands Jeremiah to buy a potter's earthen container and take elders of the people and of the priests to the valley of the son of Hinnom, by the entry of the gate Harsith. There Jeremiah is to proclaim judgment on the kings of Judah and the inhabitants of Jerusalem for forsaking the LORD, burning incense to other gods, shedding innocent blood, and burning their children to Baal. The LORD declares that the place will be renamed the valley of Slaughter, that Judah's counsel will be made void, that people will die by the sword and be food for birds and animals, and that the city will become an astonishment and a hissing, with siege-time cannibalism. Jeremiah is to break the container before the men with him as a sign that the LORD will break the people and city beyond repair, making Jerusalem's houses like Topheth. Jeremiah then returns from Topheth, stands in the court of the LORD's house, and tells all the people that the LORD will bring all the pronounced evil on the city and its towns because they have stiffened their necks and refused to hear.

Chapter 20

Pashhur, the priest and chief officer in the LORD's house, hears Jeremiah prophesying, strikes him, and puts him in the stocks at the upper gate of Benjamin. When released the next day, Jeremiah renames Pashhur Magormissabib and declares that the LORD will give Judah and its treasures to the king of Babylon, and that Pashhur and his household will die in Babylonian captivity. Jeremiah then laments that the LORD has overpowered him, that he is mocked all day, and that he cannot hold back the LORD's word, which burns in his bones. He recounts the plots of his familiar friends, expresses confidence that the LORD will make his persecutors stumble, and calls on the LORD to avenge him. The chapter closes with a call to praise the LORD, followed by Jeremiah cursing the day of his birth and asking why he came out of the womb to see sorrow and shame.

Chapter 21

King Zedekiah sends Pashhur son of Malchijah and Zephaniah son of Maaseiah to ask Jeremiah to inquire of the LORD, hoping the LORD will make Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon withdraw. Jeremiah answers that the LORD himself will fight against Jerusalem, turn back its defenders' weapons, and strike the city with pestilence. Afterward Zedekiah, his servants, and the survivors will be delivered into Nebuchadnezzar's hand and killed without mercy. The people are offered the way of life, which is to go out to the Chaldeans, or the way of death, which is to stay in the city. The LORD also tells the House of David to execute justice or face a fire of wrath, and he declares he is against the city's overconfident inhabitants.

Chapter 22

The LORD sends a message to the house of the king of Judah, calling it to execute justice and righteousness and warning that disobedience will make the house a desolation, with nations later explaining the ruin as punishment for abandoning the covenant. The chapter then addresses specific kings: Shallum son of Josiah will die in captivity and never return, and Jehoiakim son of Josiah is condemned for building his house by injustice and unpaid labor, in contrast to his father who did justice and judged the cause of the poor. Jehoiakim is told he will be buried like a donkey and cast out beyond Jerusalem's gates. Coniah son of Jehoiakim is declared to be given into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon and cast out with his mother to a foreign land where he will die. The LORD finally commands that Coniah be recorded as childless, with none of his offspring prospering on David's throne.

Chapter 23

In Jeremiah 23 the LORD pronounces woe on the shepherds who have scattered and driven away his flock, and promises to gather the remnant, set up faithful shepherds, and raise a righteous Branch for David who will reign wisely and be called 'The LORD our righteousness.' The chapter then turns to the prophets and priests of Samaria and Jerusalem, whom the LORD accuses of profanity, adultery, lies, and encouraging evildoers, likening Jerusalem to Sodom and Gomorrah and promising them wormwood and poisoned water. The LORD warns the people not to heed prophets who speak visions of their own hearts and promise peace, since they were not sent and have not stood in his council. He declares that he sees everywhere, fills heaven and earth, and is against those who tell lying dreams and steal or invent his words, contrasting straw with wheat and likening his word to fire and a hammer. The chapter ends with a ban on the phrase 'the message from the LORD', because people have perverted the words of the living God, and the LORD threatens to forget and cast off those who use it, bringing everlasting reproach and shame.

Chapter 24

The LORD shows Jeremiah two baskets of figs set before the LORD's temple, after Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon has carried Jeconiah, the princes of Judah, and the craftsmen and smiths captive to Babylon. One basket holds very good, first-ripe figs and the other holds figs so bad they cannot be eaten. The LORD explains that the good figs stand for the captives of Judah sent to the land of the Chaldeans, whom he will regard as good, bring back to the land, build and plant, and give a heart to know him. The bad figs stand for Zedekiah king of Judah, his princes, the remnant of Jerusalem, and those living in Egypt, whom the LORD will give up to be scattered among the kingdoms as a reproach, proverb, taunt, and curse. He will send sword, famine, and pestilence among them until they are consumed from the land he gave to them and their fathers.

Chapter 25

In the fourth year of Jehoiakim, king of Judah, Jeremiah tells all Judah and Jerusalem that for twenty-three years the LORD's word and his prophets have called them to turn from evil and other gods, but they have not listened. Therefore the LORD will bring the families of the north and Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, his servant, against Judah and the surrounding nations, who will serve Babylon seventy years. After seventy years the LORD will punish Babylon and make the land of the Chaldeans desolate forever. Jeremiah is told to take the cup of the wine of wrath and make all the nations drink it, from Jerusalem and Egypt to the kings of the north, with the king of Sheshach drinking last. The chapter ends with the LORD roaring in judgment against all the inhabitants of the earth, the slain lying unburied, and the shepherds wailing as their pasture is destroyed.

Chapter 26

In the early reign of Jehoiakim of Judah, the LORD commands Jeremiah to stand in the LORD's house and warn the people that, unless they listen, the house will become like Shiloh and the city a curse to all nations. The priests, prophets, and people seize Jeremiah and demand his death, but the princes of Judah come up from the king's house and hear the case. Jeremiah urges them to amend their ways and warns that killing him would bring innocent blood on them, and the princes and people rule that he is not worthy of death. Elders cite Micah the Morashtite, who prophesied against Zion under Hezekiah and was not killed, while Jehoiakim had Uriah son of Shemaiah killed after retrieving him from Egypt. Ahikam son of Shaphan protects Jeremiah from being handed over to the people.

Chapter 27

In Jeremiah 27, the LORD tells Jeremiah to make bonds and bars, wear them on his neck, and send word to the kings of Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon through their messengers in Jerusalem. The message is that the LORD, creator of the earth, has given all these lands and the animals to Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon, whom all nations will serve until his own land's time comes. Nations that refuse to put their necks under Babylon's yoke will be punished with sword, famine, and pestilence, while those that submit will remain in their land. Jeremiah repeats this to Zedekiah king of Judah, and to the priests and people, warning them not to listen to prophets, diviners, and others who say they will not serve Babylon or that the temple vessels will soon return. The LORD says the remaining vessels in the temple, the king's house, and Jerusalem will also be carried to Babylon, where they will stay until the LORD visits them and restores them to Jerusalem.

Chapter 28

In the fourth year of Zedekiah's reign, the prophet Hananiah the son of Azzur declares in the LORD's house that the LORD has broken the yoke of the king of Babylon and that within two years the temple vessels and Jeconiah with all the captives will return. Jeremiah answers 'Amen' but warns that a prophet of peace is only known as true when his word comes to pass. Hananiah then takes the bar from Jeremiah's neck and breaks it, repeating his two-year promise. The LORD tells Jeremiah that wooden bars have been replaced by iron ones, meaning all the nations will serve Nebuchadnezzar. Jeremiah tells Hananiah the LORD has not sent him and that he will die that year, and Hananiah dies in the seventh month.

Chapter 29

Jeremiah the prophet sends a letter from Jerusalem, carried by Elasah and Gemariah, to the exiles whom Nebuchadnezzar took to Babylon. In it the LORD tells them to build houses, plant gardens, marry, multiply, and seek the peace of Babylon, warning them not to trust their prophets and diviners, and promising a return after seventy years. The LORD also declares judgment of sword, famine, and pestilence on the king and the people remaining in Jerusalem, who are likened to rotten figs. He announces that Ahab son of Kolaiah and Zedekiah son of Maaseiah, who prophesy lies, will be delivered to Nebuchadnezzar and killed. Shemaiah the Nehelamite sends letters urging the priests to rebuke Jeremiah, and the LORD pronounces punishment on Shemaiah and his offspring for speaking rebellion.

Chapter 30

In Jeremiah 30, the LORD tells Jeremiah to write all his words in a book and promises to reverse the captivity of Israel and Judah and return them to the land given to their fathers. He describes a terrifying day, the time of Jacob's trouble, from which Jacob will be saved, when he will break the yoke of foreign rule and raise up David their king. The LORD explains that Israel's wound is incurable and that he inflicted it because of the greatness of their iniquity, with their former lovers forgetting them. He promises nonetheless to devour and plunder those who devoured and plundered Israel, to heal Israel, and to rebuild its city and multiply its people. The chapter closes with the promise that they will be his people, and that his fierce anger will burst on the wicked and not return until he has accomplished his purposes.

Chapter 31

In Jeremiah 31, the LORD promises to be God of all the families of Israel and to rebuild, regather, and ransom Jacob/Israel from the north and the uttermost parts of the earth, turning mourning into joy. Rachel weeps in Ramah for her children, but the LORD tells her to stop weeping because they will return, and he expresses mercy and yearning for repentant Ephraim, his dear son. The LORD announces that he will sow the houses of Israel and Judah, watch over them to build and plant, and end the proverb about sour grapes so that each dies for his own iniquity. He promises a new covenant with Israel and Judah, unlike the one made when he brought their fathers out of Egypt, by writing his law on their hearts and forgiving their iniquity. He guarantees Israel's permanence as long as the sun, moon, stars, and heavens endure, and foretells Jerusalem rebuilt and holy to the LORD, never to be plucked up again.

Chapter 32

In the tenth year of Zedekiah, with Babylon's army besieging Jerusalem, Jeremiah is imprisoned in the court of the guard by King Zedekiah for prophesying that the city and the king will fall to Babylon. At the LORD's word, Jeremiah's cousin Hanamel offers him the field at Anathoth, and Jeremiah buys it for seventeen shekels of silver, signs and seals the deed before witnesses, and gives it to Baruch to store in an earthen vessel as a sign that fields will again be bought in the land. Jeremiah then prays, praising the LORD's power and history with Israel and puzzling over the command to buy land when the city is about to fall to the Chaldeans. The LORD answers that nothing is too hard for him, confirms that Jerusalem will be burned because of Judah's and Israel's idolatry and disobedience, and then promises to regather the people, make an everlasting covenant with them, and reverse their captivity so that fields are bought again throughout the land.

Chapter 33

The LORD speaks to Jeremiah a second time while he is still locked up in the court of the guard. He promises that Jerusalem and Judah, though now broken down and desolate from the Chaldeans' attack, will receive health, healing, cleansing, and pardon. The LORD says the voices of joy, bridegroom and bride, and thanksgiving will return, flocks will again be counted in the cities, and the captivity will be reversed as at the first. He promises a Branch of righteousness to grow up to David, and that David will never lack a man on the throne nor the Levitical priests a man to offer sacrifices. He ties these promises to his covenant of day and night, saying that only if it could be broken could his covenant with David and the Levites fail, and he rebukes those who say he has cast off the two families.

Chapter 34

While Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon and his army fight against Jerusalem and the remaining cities of Judah, the LORD sends Jeremiah to tell King Zedekiah that the city will be burned and that he will be taken to Babylon, though he will die in peace. The chapter then recounts how Zedekiah made a covenant with the people of Jerusalem to free their Hebrew male and female servants, and how the princes and people first obeyed but then took the servants back. The LORD recalls his covenant with their fathers at the exodus from Egypt requiring release of Hebrew servants after seven years, and charges the people with profaning his name by reversing their release. As punishment he proclaims a 'liberty' to the sword, pestilence, and famine, and gives the covenant-breakers, including Zedekiah and his princes, into the hands of their enemies. He declares that the Babylonian army will return to take and burn Jerusalem and make the cities of Judah a desolation.

Chapter 35

In the days of Jehoiakim son of Josiah, king of Judah, the LORD tells Jeremiah to bring the Rechabites into the LORD's house and offer them wine. Jeremiah gathers Jaazaniah, his brothers, his sons, and the whole house of the Rechabites into a room and sets bowls and cups of wine before them. They refuse, saying their father Jonadab son of Rechab commanded them never to drink wine, build houses, sow seed, or plant vineyards, but to live in tents, and that they came to Jerusalem only for fear of the Babylonian and Syrian armies. The LORD uses their faithfulness as a contrast, telling Jeremiah to ask the men of Judah and inhabitants of Jerusalem why they do not listen to him, and announces that he will bring on them all the evil he has pronounced. Jeremiah then tells the Rechabites that because they obeyed Jonadab, Jonadab will never lack a man to stand before the LORD.

Chapter 36

In the fourth year of Jehoiakim, the LORD told Jeremiah to write all his words against Israel, Judah, and the nations on a scroll, hoping Judah would repent. Jeremiah dictated it to Baruch the son of Neriah and sent him to read it in the LORD's house on a fast day, since Jeremiah was restricted. After Baruch read it in the fifth year, the princes heard it, feared, told Baruch and Jeremiah to hide, and reported it to the king. Jehoiakim had Jehudi read the scroll, cut it up with a penknife and burned it in the brazier, showing no fear, and ordered the arrest of Baruch and Jeremiah, but the LORD hid them. The LORD then had Jeremiah write a second scroll with Baruch and pronounced judgment on Jehoiakim: no heir on David's throne, and his body cast out to heat and frost.

Chapter 37

Zedekiah, son of Josiah, reigns in place of Coniah, but neither he nor his people heed the LORD's words through Jeremiah. Zedekiah sends Jehucal and Zephaniah to ask Jeremiah to pray for them, while Pharaoh's army's approach causes the Chaldeans to withdraw from Jerusalem. The LORD tells Jeremiah that Pharaoh's army will return to Egypt and the Chaldeans will come back, take the city, and burn it. When Jeremiah goes toward Benjamin, the guard captain Irijah seizes him as a defector, and the princes beat him and imprison him in the house of Jonathan the scribe. Zedekiah secretly consults him, hears that he will be delivered to Babylon's king, and moves Jeremiah to the court of the guard, where he is given daily bread until the city's bread runs out.

Chapter 38

Jeremiah tells the people that those who stay in Jerusalem will die while those who go out to the Chaldeans will live, and that the city will be given to the king of Babylon. Offended princes, including Shephatiah, Gedaliah, Jucal, and Pashhur, ask King Zedekiah to put him to death, and Zedekiah yields them control of him; they lower Jeremiah into Malchijah's muddy dungeon. Ebedmelech the Ethiopian, a eunuch, appeals to the king, and Zedekiah orders him to take thirty men and lift Jeremiah out, which he does using cords and rags. Zedekiah then secretly questions Jeremiah, who urges him to surrender to the Babylonian princes to save his life, house, and city, but Zedekiah fears the Jews who defected. Zedekiah instructs Jeremiah to conceal their talk from the princes, and Jeremiah remains in the court of the guard until Jerusalem is taken.

Chapter 39

In the ninth year of Zedekiah's reign, Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon and his whole army besieged Jerusalem, and in the eleventh year a breach was made in the city and the Babylonian princes took seats in the middle gate. Zedekiah and his men of war fled by night toward the Arabah, but the Chaldean army overtook him in the plains of Jericho and brought him to Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah, where his sons and the nobles of Judah were killed and he was blinded and bound in fetters for transport to Babylon. The Chaldeans burned the king's house and the people's houses and broke down Jerusalem's walls, while Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard carried the remaining people captive to Babylon and left some of the poor in Judah with vineyards and fields. Nebuchadnezzar ordered that Jeremiah be cared for and not harmed, so Nebuzaradan and other officers took him from the court of the guard and committed him to Gedaliah, and he lived among the people. The chapter closes with a word the LORD had given Jeremiah earlier, promising Ebedmelech the Ethiopian that he would be delivered and not fall by the sword because he trusted in the LORD.

Chapter 40

Nebuzaradan, captain of the guard, releases Jeremiah from his chains at Ramah and tells him the LORD brought disaster on Judah because of its sin. He offers Jeremiah the choice to go to Babylon or stay, and sends him to Gedaliah the son of Ahikam, whom the king of Babylon made governor, at Mizpah. Gedaliah assures the captains of the forces and their men that serving the Chaldeans will bring them well-being, and Jews scattered in Moab, Ammon, Edom and other lands return and gather abundant wine and summer fruits. Johanan the son of Kareah and the captains warn Gedaliah that Baalis, king of Ammon, has sent Ishmael the son of Nethaniah to kill him. Gedaliah does not believe them and forbids Johanan's offer to kill Ishmael secretly.

Chapter 41

In the seventh month, Ishmael the son of Nethaniah, a royal descendant, came with ten men to Mizpah, ate with Gedaliah the son of Ahikam, and then killed him along with the Jews and Chaldean soldiers there. Two days later he lured eighty mourning pilgrims from Shechem, Shiloh, and Samaria into the city, killed them, and threw their bodies into the pit Asa had made, sparing ten who offered hidden stores of food. Ishmael took the remaining people of Mizpah, including the king's daughters, captive and set off toward the Ammonites. Johanan the son of Kareah and the captains heard of it, pursued him, and caught up at the great waters in Gibeon, where the captives rejoined Johanan while Ishmael escaped with eight men to the Ammonites. Johanan led the recovered remnant to Geruth Chimham near Bethlehem, intending to go to Egypt out of fear of the Chaldeans.

Chapter 42

Johanan the son of Kareah, the captains of the forces, and all the people come to Jeremiah the prophet and ask him to pray to the LORD for guidance, promising to obey whatever answer comes. After ten days the LORD's word comes to Jeremiah, who tells them that if they stay in this land the LORD will build and plant them and protect them from the king of Babylon. If they instead go to Egypt seeking to escape war and hunger, the sword, famine, and pestilence will overtake them there and they will die. Jeremiah warns that they have dealt deceitfully with their own souls by asking for the word while not intending to obey it, and he repeats that they will die in the place where they wish to live.

Chapter 43

After Jeremiah finishes delivering the LORD's words, Azariah, Johanan the son of Kareah, and the proud men accuse him of speaking falsely and claim Baruch the son of Neriah has turned him against them to hand them over to the Chaldeans. Johanan and the captains disobey the LORD's voice and take the whole remnant of Judah, including Jeremiah and Baruch, into Egypt, where they arrive at Tahpanhes. There the LORD tells Jeremiah to hide great stones in the brickwork at the entry of Pharaoh's house in sight of the men of Judah. He explains that he will bring Nebuchadnezzar, his servant, to set his throne on those stones. Nebuchadnezzar will strike Egypt with death, captivity, and the sword, burn the houses of Egypt's gods, and break the pillars of Beth Shemesh.

Chapter 44

Jeremiah delivers the word of the LORD of Armies, the God of Israel, to all the Jews living in Egypt at Migdol, Tahpanhes, Memphis, and Pathros. He recalls that Jerusalem and the cities of Judah are desolate because of idolatrous incense burning, despite repeated warnings from the prophets, and warns that the Judah remnant in Egypt will be consumed by sword, famine, and pestilence. The men and women refuse to listen, vowing to keep burning incense and pouring drink offerings to the queen of the sky, claiming prosperity came with it and hardship came when they stopped. Jeremiah answers that the LORD remembered their incense and brought the desolation, and he tells them to go ahead and keep their vows, while the LORD swears that his name will no longer be invoked by the men of Judah in Egypt. The LORD gives a sign: he will give Pharaoh Hophra into the hand of his enemies, as he gave Zedekiah to Nebuchadnezzar, and only a few escapees will return to Judah.

Chapter 45

Chapter 45 records a message that Jeremiah the prophet spoke to Baruch the son of Neriah in the fourth year of Jehoiakim the son of Josiah, king of Judah, when Baruch was writing Jeremiah's words in a book. The LORD, the God of Israel, quotes Baruch's complaint that He has added sorrow to his pain and that he is weary with groaning and finds no rest. The LORD declares that He will break down what He has built and pluck up what He has planted throughout the whole land. He asks whether Baruch seeks great things for himself and tells him not to, since evil will come on all flesh. The LORD promises that Baruch will escape with his life wherever he goes.

Chapter 46

Chapter 46 is the LORD's word to Jeremiah the prophet concerning the nations, beginning with Egypt. It describes the defeat of Pharaoh Necoh's army at Carchemish by the Euphrates, struck by Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon in the fourth year of Jehoiakim of Judah. Egypt's boast of rising like the Nile is answered with a day of vengeance for the LORD, no healing for Egypt, and a coming invasion by Nebuchadnezzar's innumerable army from the north that will devastate Memphis and other cities and hand Egypt, Amon of No, and Pharaoh over to captivity. The LORD adds that Egypt will afterwards be inhabited as in the days of old. He then comforts Jacob his servant, promising to save him from captivity, to be with him, and to correct him in measure without leaving him unpunished.

Chapter 47

Chapter 47 records the LORD's word to Jeremiah the prophet concerning the Philistines, given before Pharaoh struck Gaza. The LORD describes waters rising out of the north that become an overflowing stream flooding the land, its cities and inhabitants, so that men cry out and all wail. The noise of the hoofs, chariots, and wheels of the invader's strong ones makes fathers too feeble to look back for their children. The day is coming to destroy all the Philistines and cut off every remaining helper from Tyre and Sidon, and Gaza and Ashkelon are described as bald and brought to nothing. The chapter ends with an address to the sword of the LORD, which asks how long until it is quiet, and is answered that it cannot rest because the LORD has commanded it against Ashkelon and the seashore.

Chapter 48

Chapter 48 is the LORD of Armies' oracle against Moab, announcing the destruction of its cities, including Nebo, Kiriathaim, Heshbon, Dibon, and Kerioth. Moab is judged for trusting in its works and treasures, for its long complacency, and for its pride and for magnifying itself against the LORD. Its god Chemosh will go into captivity with his priests and princes, and a destroyer will come on every city so that none escapes. The LORD himself laments for Moab's vineyards and people, even as he promises terror, the pit, and the snare, and an end to its being a people. The chapter closes with a promise that the LORD will reverse Moab's captivity in the latter days.

Chapter 49

Jeremiah 49 records the LORD's oracles of judgment against several nations. Ammon, whose god Malcam occupies Gad, will see Rabbah become a desolate heap, yet its captivity is later reversed. Edom, including Teman, Dedan and Bozrah, is stripped bare, made small, and brought down from its heights like Sodom and Gomorrah. Damascus, Kedar, and Hazor are threatened with terror, flight and desolation, with Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon named as the agent against Kedar and Hazor. Elam is to have its bow broken and be scattered to all the winds, but its captivity will be reversed in the latter days.

Chapter 50

Chapter 50 records the word the LORD spoke through Jeremiah the prophet against Babylon and the land of the Chaldeans. A nation from the north, with a company of great nations and many kings, is stirred up to take Babylon, make it desolate, and destroy its idols, its warriors, its wise men and its waters, because it has been proud against the LORD and plundered his heritage. Israel and Judah, described as lost sheep misled by their shepherds and devoured by Assyria and Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon, are told to flee Babylon, return weeping to seek the LORD in an everlasting covenant, and be pardoned as a remnant. The LORD, their strong Redeemer, will plead their cause and bring Israel back to Carmel, Bashan, Ephraim and Gilead. The chapter ends with Babylon's king losing heart and the earth trembling at the noise of Babylon's capture.

Chapter 51

In Jeremiah 51 the LORD declares that he will destroy Babylon, the cup that made the nations drunk, by sending destroyers and stirring up the kings of the Medes. Israel and Judah are told they are not forsaken, and his people are urged to flee Babylon and declare the LORD's work in Zion. The chapter contrasts the LORD, creator of the earth, with the worthless idols of goldsmiths, and calls Babylon his battle ax before turning the weapon against her. Zion and Jerusalem cry out against Nebuchadnezzar's violence, and the LORD promises vengeance, drying up her sea and judging Bel. Jeremiah then commands Seraiah to read his book of Babylon's doom there, bind a stone to it, and cast it into the Euphrates as a sign that Babylon will sink and not rise.

Chapter 52

Chapter 52 recounts the fall of Jerusalem under Zedekiah, who did evil, rebelled against the king of Babylon, and was captured after Nebuchadnezzar besieged the city from the ninth to the eleventh year of his reign. Zedekiah's sons and the princes of Judah were killed, Zedekiah was blinded and imprisoned in Babylon, and Nebuzaradan burned the LORD's house, the king's house and all the great houses, and broke down the walls. The Chaldeans broke up and carried off the temple's bronze pillars, sea, bases and vessels, and Nebuzaradan took leading priests and officials to Riblah, where the king of Babylon put them to death. The chapter lists the captives carried away in three deportations, totaling four thousand six hundred. It ends with Evilmerodach king of Babylon releasing Jehoiachin from prison in the thirty-seventh year of his captivity, treating him kindly and providing for him daily until his death.
