Abstract written by a model

In The Acts of the Apostles, Paul preaches Jesus as the Christ, travels on mission, is imprisoned, appeals to Caesar, and preaches in Rome. In Paul’s Letter to the Romans, Paul writes as a servant, called apostle, and apostle to the Gentiles, explaining his mission and concern for Israel. In Paul’s Letter to the Romans, the narrator describes his struggle with sin, cries out for deliverance, and thanks God through Jesus Christ. In Paul’s First Letter to the Corinthians, Paul writes as an apostle, founder, father, servant, and steward who instructs and corrects the Corinthian believers. In Paul’s First Letter to the Corinthians, Paul presents himself as an apostle who preaches Christ, teaches by the Spirit, delivers traditions, and urges steadfastness. In 2 Corinthians, Paul writes as an apostle and pastor defending his sincerity, authority, conduct, and love for the Corinthians. In Paul’s Letter to the Galatians, Paul defends his divine apostolic commission, recounts his history, and urges the Galatians to live by faith and the Spirit. In Paul’s Letter to the Ephesians, Paul writes as an apostle, prisoner, servant, and ambassador in chains who urges the Ephesians to live worthily and pray for boldness.

In Paul’s Letter to the Philippians, Paul writes as an imprisoned servant who encourages unity, humility, rejoicing, contentment, and faith in Christ. In Paul’s Letter to the Colossians, Paul writes as an apostle and servant who labors for the Colossians, warns them against deception, and asks them to remember his chains. In 1 Thessalonians, Paul writes with Silvanus and Timothy as a gentle apostle who preached to and encouraged the Thessalonian believers. In Paul’s Second Letter to the Thessalonians, Paul writes as an apostle who commends perseverance, requests prayer, commands discipline, and authenticates the letter by hand. In 1 Timothy, Paul writes as an apostle and former persecutor who received mercy, was appointed to service, and entrusted with the Good News. In 2 Timothy, Paul writes as an imprisoned apostle and teacher who endures suffering, instructs Timothy, and awaits a crown of righteousness. In Titus, Paul writes as a servant and apostle who instructs Titus to set the Cretan assemblies in order and silence deceivers. In Paul’s Letter to Philemon, Paul writes as a prisoner appealing to Philemon for Onesimus and promising to repay Onesimus’s debt.

Paul in The Acts of the Apostles

author unknown · about AD 80 to 90 · the work’s page · read it where Paul first appears

Abstract written by a model

Paul, also called Saul, is a Jew born in Tarsus in Cilicia, raised in Jerusalem at the feet of Gamaliel, a Pharisee son of Pharisees, and a Roman citizen by birth. He first appears as a young man at whose feet garments were laid. He consented to Stephen's death and ravaged the assembly, dragging men and women to prison with the chief priests' authority. Near Damascus a light and the voice of Jesus stopped him; he was blinded, healed and baptized. Chosen as a vessel to bear Jesus' name before nations, kings and Israel, he proclaimed Jesus as the Christ in synagogues, escaped a plot in a basket, and was brought by Barnabas to the apostles, then sent to Tarsus. Barnabas fetched him to Antioch, where they taught for a year. The Holy Spirit separated him with Barnabas for the Gentile mission. He blinded Elymas, turned to the Gentiles after Jewish opposition, was called Mercury at Lystra, and was stoned yet rose. After a dispute over Mark he separated from Barnabas and chose Silas, strengthening assemblies in Syria and Cilicia. His journeys took him to Philippi, where he was jailed and invoked Roman status, then Thessalonica, Athens, and Corinth, where he made tents and taught a year and a half. At Ephesus he taught in the school of Tyrannus for two years, with special miracles through his hands, and planned Jerusalem and then Rome. After a riot and a plot, he passed through Macedonia and Greece, preached until midnight at Troas, and told the Ephesian elders he went bound by the Spirit to Jerusalem, supporting himself by manual labor and coveting nothing. In Jerusalem he was seized in the temple, chained, and defended himself in Hebrew, asserting his citizenship. Accused by Tertullus, he was held two years under Felix at Caesarea, professing he served God according to the Way and hoped in the resurrection. He appealed to Caesar and defended himself before Agrippa. The voyage to Rome brought an angel's assurance that he must stand before Caesar. On Malta a viper bite left him unharmed, natives called him a god, and he healed Publius's father. The records end with him in Rome, guarded by a soldier, living two years in his rented house preaching God's Kingdom with all boldness and without hindrance.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

Chapter 8

Saul consented to Stephen's death. He ravaged the assembly, entering every house and dragging men and women off to prison.

Chapter 9

Saul, breathing threats and slaughter, sought letters to bring followers of the Way bound to Jerusalem, but was stopped near Damascus by a light and the voice of Jesus, was blinded for three days, and was healed and baptized. He then proclaimed Christ in the synagogues, confounded the Jews, escaped a plot by being lowered in a basket through the wall, and was brought by Barnabas to the apostles in Jerusalem. After the Hellenists sought to kill him, the brothers sent him to Tarsus.

Chapter 11

Barnabas goes to Tarsus to look for Saul, finds him, and brings him to Antioch. They teach many people there for a year, and Saul then helps carry relief to the elders in Judea.

Chapter 13

Also called Saul, Paul is separated with Barnabas by the Holy Spirit's direction, strikes the sorcerer Elymas with blindness at Paphos, and preaches in the synagogue at Antioch of Pisidia. After Jewish opposition he and Barnabas turn to the Gentiles, and are then thrown out of the region and go to Iconium.

Chapter 14

Paul speaks in Iconium, flees to Lystra, and heals a lame man there, after which he is called Mercury as chief speaker. He pleads with the crowd not to sacrifice to him, is then stoned and dragged out of Lystra as if dead, yet rises and goes to Derbe. He and Barnabas later strengthen disciples, appoint elders, and report back at Antioch.

Chapter 15

Paul disputes with the men from Judea in Antioch and is appointed to go to Jerusalem, where he reports what God did among the Gentiles. Later he refuses to take Mark, whom he says withdrew in Pamphylia, so he separates from Barnabas, chooses Silas, and travels through Syria and Cilicia strengthening the assemblies.

Chapter 16

Paul has Timothy circumcised and take part in the journey, and he receives a vision at Troas of a man of Macedonia asking for help. At Philippi he commands a spirit out of a girl, is beaten and jailed with Silas, tells the jailer to believe in the Lord Jesus, and insists that the magistrates come themselves to release him because he is a Roman.

Chapter 17

Paul came to Thessalonica and reasoned from the Scriptures in the synagogue for three Sabbaths, proclaiming that Jesus is the Christ who had to suffer and rise. Driven out by a mob, he went to Beroea, then was escorted to Athens, where he was provoked by the idols, debated in the synagogue and marketplace, and spoke at the Areopagus. Some mocked him, some wanted to hear more, and a few believed.

Chapter 18

Paul leaves Athens for Corinth, works as a tent maker with Aquila and Priscilla, and reasons in the synagogue every Sabbath. After the Jews oppose him he declares he will go to the Gentiles, stays a year and six months teaching, and is encouraged by the Lord in a vision. He is brought before Gallio, then shaves his head at Cenchreae for a vow, sails with Priscilla and Aquila, leaves them at Ephesus, promises to return if God wills, and travels via Caesarea and Antioch through Galatia and Phrygia strengthening disciples.

Chapter 19

Paul comes to Ephesus, explains John's baptism to certain disciples, and lays hands on them so the Holy Spirit comes. He speaks in the synagogue for three months and then in the school of Tyrannus for two years, with special miracles done by his hands, and he determines to go through Macedonia and Achaia to Jerusalem and then Rome. When the riot breaks out, the disciples and some Asiarchs keep him from entering the theater.

Chapter 20

Paul left for Macedonia and Greece, stayed three months, and changed his route to return through Macedonia after a plot by Jews. At Troas he preached until midnight, embraced the fallen Eutychus and said his life was in him, then continued talking until daybreak before departing. He bypassed Ephesus to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost, called the Ephesian elders to Miletus, and said he goes bound by the Spirit to Jerusalem, where bonds and afflictions await him.

Chapter 21

Paul travels by ship to Tyre, Caesarea, and Jerusalem despite warnings through the Spirit and Agabus that he will be bound, saying he is ready to be bound and to die for the name of the Lord Jesus. In Jerusalem he reports God's work among the Gentiles, follows the elders' advice to undergo purification and pay for four men under a vow, and is seized in the temple, beaten, and chained. He tells the commanding officer he is a Jew from Tarsus in Cilicia and is allowed to speak to the people in Hebrew.

Chapter 22

Paul defends himself in Hebrew before the Jerusalem crowd, telling of his Jewish upbringing, his persecution of the Way, and his conversion and commission to the Gentiles. After the crowd demands his death, he is bound for scourging but asserts his Roman citizenship, and the next day he is freed from his bonds and set before the council.

Chapter 23

Paul tells the council he has lived in good conscience before God, rebukes Ananias after being struck, and apologizes upon learning Ananias is high priest. He declares himself a Pharisee judged over the resurrection, which divides the council, and is rescued by soldiers. The Lord tells him he must testify in Rome; he exposes the plot through his nephew and is taken to Caesarea, where Felix orders him kept in Herod's palace.

Chapter 24

Paul is accused before Felix by Tertullus and the Jewish leaders, and defends himself by saying he came to Jerusalem only twelve days earlier to worship and bring gifts and offerings for the needy. He confesses that he serves God according to the Way and hopes in the resurrection, and says he was judged before the council over that one point. He is kept in custody with privileges, speaks to Felix and Drusilla about faith in Christ Jesus, and is left in bonds after two years.

Chapter 25

Paul is kept in custody at Caesarea and, at his hearing, denies having sinned against the Jewish law, the temple, or Caesar. He refuses to be tried in Jerusalem, says he does not refuse to die if guilty, and appeals to Caesar. He is later brought before King Agrippa and the assembled men at Festus's command.

Chapter 26

Paul makes his defense before King Agrippa, recounting his Pharisee upbringing, his persecution of the saints, and his conversion and commissioning on the road to Damascus. He says he obeyed the heavenly vision, preached repentance to Jews and Gentiles, and was seized by the Jews in the temple. He rejects Festus's charge of madness, presses Agrippa on his belief in the prophets, and wishes all his hearers might become as he is, except for his bonds.

Chapter 27

Paul is delivered with other prisoners to the centurion Julius and sails toward Italy, receiving kind treatment at Sidon. He warns that the voyage will bring loss of cargo, ship and lives, is ignored, and later reports an angel's message that he must stand before Caesar and that God has granted him all who sail with him. He tells the centurion that the sailors must stay in the ship, urges all to eat, gives thanks over bread, and is spared when the centurion stops the soldiers from killing the prisoners.

Chapter 28

Paul gathers sticks on Malta, is bitten by a viper, shakes it into the fire and is unharmed, so the natives call him a god. He heals Publius's father and other sick islanders, then travels to Rome, where he is allowed to stay with a guarding soldier. He tells the Jewish leaders he is bound for the hope of Israel, testifies about God's Kingdom and Jesus, quotes Isaiah, and stays two years in his rented house preaching with all boldness and without hindrance.

He plans to carry a contribution to Jerusalem, then go to Spain by way of Rome, and asks prayer for deliverance from the disobedient in Judea. Paul in Paul’s First Letter to the Corinthians

author unknown · AD 53 to 54 · the work’s page · read it where Paul first appears

Abstract written by a model

Paul is an apostle of Jesus Christ, called through the will of God, who writes to the Corinthian believers together with his companion Sosthenes, described as a brother. The records present him as a founding figure and father to the Corinthians: he planted them as a servant of God, laid a foundation as a wise master builder by the grace given him, and became their father in Christ Jesus through the Good News. He is also a fellow worker of God, a servant of Christ and steward of God's mysteries alongside Apollos, and he defends his apostleship by noting he has seen Jesus Christ as Lord and that the Corinthians are his work in the Lord. The letter opens with thanks and a plea to end divisions. Paul says Christ sent him to preach the Good News, not to baptize, so he baptized only Crispus, Gaius and the household of Stephanas. He preaches Christ crucified, not in wisdom of words. He fed the Corinthians milk rather than solid food, and says the boasting in men is wrong, though all things, including Paul, are theirs. What matters most is his conduct and self-understanding. He does not judge himself, since the Lord is his judge. He endures hunger, thirst, nakedness, beatings and homelessness, works with his own hands, and answers cursing and persecution with blessing and endurance. He has an apostolic right to financial support but does not use it, preaching without charge and under the necessity of a stewardship entrusted to him. Though free from all, he makes himself a servant to all, becoming as Jews, those under the law, those without law, and the weak, to gain them. He disciplines his body to avoid disqualification and seeks the profit of the many that they may be saved. He is unmarried and wishes others were like him, distinguishing his own judgment from the Lord's command, and thinks he has God's Spirit. He also judges a case of sexual sin within the assembly, though not outsiders. The letter moves toward plans. He sends Timothy to remind the Corinthians of his ways, which he teaches in every assembly, and says he will come shortly if the Lord is willing, asking whether they want him to come with a rod or in love and gentleness. He will stay at Ephesus until Pentecost, where a great and effective door is open despite many adversaries, then pass through Macedonia to visit them. He gives instructions on the collection, commends Timothy and the house of Stephanas, and urges Apollos to visit. He closes with a greeting written in his own hand and love for the Corinthians in Christ Jesus.

Paul in 2 Corinthians

Paul · AD 55 to 56 · the work’s page · read it where Paul first appears

Abstract written by a model

Paul is an apostle of Christ Jesus by God's will, writing with Timothy to the assembly of God at Corinth. The records present him as a pastor defending his sincerity, authority and conduct to a community in which critics call him lowly in presence, bold only when absent, weak in body and unskilled in speech. Most important is his relationship with the Corinthians: he loves them, bears anxiety for all the assemblies, feels godly jealousy for them as a bride promised to one husband, Christ, and calls himself a fellow worker who works for their joy rather than controlling their faith. He trusts in God who raises the dead and hopes to be delivered still. He boasts of a clear conscience, of the Corinthians mutually, and of Achaia's readiness to Macedonia. He claims his word in letters matches his deed in person, and he takes approval from the Lord, not self-commendation. The account unfolds from the opening, where Paul describes comfort in affliction, despair of life in Asia, his changed travel plans, and his sparing the Corinthians by not coming. He writes in sorrow and tears, urges forgiveness of an offender, and is relieved in Macedonia when Titus arrives with good news of their repentance, so he no longer regrets his letter. He then turns to the collection for the saints, sending Titus and brothers, avoiding blame in administering it, and urging cheerful, bountiful giving. Next he defends his authority against critics: he walks in the flesh but does not war by it, keeps within the sphere God appointed reaching to Corinth, and hopes to preach beyond. He asserts that he is not inferior to the best apostles, a Hebrew, Israelite and offspring of Abraham, and a servant of Christ more than his rivals, recounting hardships and his escape from Damascus in a basket. Reluctantly boasting, he speaks of a man caught up to Paradise and a thorn in the flesh, glorying in weakness so Christ's power rests on him. He performed signs and wonders, was no financial burden, and will spend and be spent for their souls. He ends by fearing strife and disorder on his coming third visit, mourning those who have not repented, warning he will not spare them, exhorting, blessing, and noting his authority is for building up, not tearing down.

Abstract written by a model

Paul is the author of the letter to the Galatians, presenting himself as an apostle commissioned not by men but through Jesus Christ and God the Father, writing with the brothers who are with him. Abstract written by a model
