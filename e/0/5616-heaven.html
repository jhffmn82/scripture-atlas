Abstract written by a model

In Psalms, the heavens are called to praise the LORD, having been created at his command and established forever. In Psalms, the heavens are described as the work of the LORD’s fingers, containing the moon and stars and prompting wonder about humanity. In The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach, or Ecclesiasticus, the sky is described as the pride of the heavenly heights, encircled by the rainbow and containing the stars. In Revelation, heaven is the setting of the woman, the dragon, and their war, where the dragon and his angels lose their place. In The Analects of Confucius, Book V (Kung-Ye Ch'Ang), the way of Heaven is discussed as something that cannot be heard. In The Analects of Confucius, Book VI (Yung Yey), Heaven is invoked to reject the Master if he has acted improperly. In The Analects of Confucius, Book IX (Tsze Han), Heaven is described as preserving the cause of truth from perishing. In The Analects of Confucius, Book IX (Tsze Han), the appointments of Heaven are named as a subject about which the Master seldom spoke.

In The Doctrine of the Mean, Heaven is described as the enduring source of Nature, sincerity, appointments, nourishment, and transformation, containing and nourishing all things. In Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang), Heaven produces inferior people and appoints rulers and teachers, while determining the accomplishment of great results. In Mencius, Book II (Gong Sun Chou), Heaven sends calamities, confers benevolence, grants opportunities, and determines whether the kingdom will receive tranquillity and good order. In Mencius, Book V (Wan Zhang), Heaven produces people so that those informed earlier may instruct those informed later, and provides places, offices, and emoluments. In Mencius, Book VI (Gaozi), Heaven gives people faculties, relations, and laws, and prepares them for great office through hardship. In Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), Heaven is known through knowing and nourishing one’s nature and is associated with appointed events and natural allotments. In Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), the appointment of Heaven is described as existing for everything and including natural feelings and righteousness in moral relations. In Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), Heaven is used as a comparison for the broad, flowing influence of the superior man.

Heaven in The Doctrine of the Mean

James Legge · about 449 BC · the work’s page · read it where Heaven first appears

Abstract written by a model

Heaven is a vast, enduring source of Nature, sincerity, appointments, nourishment, and transforming operations. It confers what is called the Nature and makes appointments that the superior man quietly awaits without murmuring. It is bountiful to beings according to their qualities: nourishing what flourishes, overthrowing what is ready to fall, and granting dignity and the throne as favors. Large, substantial, high, brilliant, far-reaching, and long-enduring, it contains the sun, moon, stars, and constellations of the zodiac, overspreading all things. Its singleness of virtue makes it Heaven. With Earth, it provides the mode of operation against which the Ruler’s institutions are presented; knowing this harmony shows knowledge of Heaven. It supports, contains, overshadows, and curtains all things, and is great because all are nourished together without injuring one another. Finally, it measures the ideal ruler and individual, whose sincerity may know its transforming and nurturing operations.
