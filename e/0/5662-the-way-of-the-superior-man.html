the course of the Mean in The Doctrine of the Mean

James Legge · about 449 BC · the work’s page · read it where the course of the Mean first appears

Abstract written by a model

The course of the Mean is embodied and followed by the superior man, opposed by the mean man. Though people may choose it, they cannot keep it for a full month or attain it, even through difficult feats. The superior man accords with it without regret while unknown and unregarded.

the way of the superior man in The Doctrine of the Mean

James Legge · about 449 BC · the work’s page · read it where the way of the superior man first appears

Abstract written by a model

The way of the superior man is the Path of duty: an accordance with Nature whose regulation is Instruction and which cannot be left even briefly. It is a near, human path grounded in common consciousness and the ordinary intercourse of men and women, yet it reaches secretly, widely, and far through Heaven and earth beyond even the sage’s complete knowledge or practice. It unfolds by beginning with what is near and low, like traveling or ascending, rather than abandoning the right path halfway. In its fullest course, it is abundant like overflowing water, nourishing all things and rising to heaven’s height. Complete in scope, it includes the three hundred rules of ceremony and three thousand rules of demeanor, becoming fact only through perfect virtue when the proper man appears.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

The Doctrine of the Mean / Chapter 1

The Path of duty is an accordance with Nature, and its regulation is called Instruction. It may not be left for an instant.

Chapter 11

The right path is what the good man tries to follow before abandoning it halfway.

Chapter 12

It reaches wide and far while remaining secret. Its simple elements may be found in the intercourse of common men and women, while its utmost reaches shine through Heaven and earth. Even the sage cannot fully know or practice it.

Chapter 13

The path is not far from man and is connected with the common indications of consciousness. A course far from those indications cannot be considered the path.

Chapter 15

The way of the superior man is compared to traveling and ascending: one first traverses the space that is near and begins from the lower ground.

Chapter 27

The path proper to the Sage sends forth and nourishes all things like overflowing water and rises to the height of heaven. It embraces the three hundred rules of ceremony and the three thousand rules of demeanor, but it is trodden only when the proper man appears.
