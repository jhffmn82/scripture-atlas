Abstract written by a model

Haman, son of Hammedatha, is described in the Greek Esther records as a Bougean (Bugaean), and in the king's and Mordecai's letters as a Macedonian, an alien to the Persians. He is the central antagonist: a man highly honored and exalted by King Ahasuerus, set above all the king's friends, holding the next place to the king, second post and chief ruler in the kingdom. A Haman is also listed among the seven chamberlains who were sent to bring in Vashti. He is called a slanderer, wicked, arrogant, and the enemy of the Jews, of Mordecai, and of Esther's people. The story begins with Haman, honored in the king's sight, seeking to harm Mordecai and his people because of the executed chamberlains. In chapter 3 he is enraged when Mordecai refuses to bow, plots the destruction of all the Jews, casts lots that fall on the fourteenth day of Adar, accuses the Jews before the king as a nation with differing laws, and promises ten thousand talents of silver into the treasury. He receives the king's ring and commands the writing of the decrees sent to the provinces. Mordecai and Esther pray against him; Esther asks that the counsel of Israel's enemies be turned back on themselves and says she has not eaten at his table. In chapter 5 he is invited with the king to Esther's feast and leaves glad, but seeing Mordecai in the court enrages him again. He tells his wife Zeresh and his friends of his wealth and honor, says none of it pleases him while Mordecai remains, and, pleased by their advice, has gallows prepared for hanging Mordecai. In chapter 6 he comes to ask for Mordecai's hanging, but, believing the king wishes to honor himself, proposes the robe, horse and proclamation, and is then ordered to do all of it for Mordecai. He goes home mourning with his head covered, Zeresh and his friends tell him he will assuredly fall, and he is hurried to Esther's banquet. In chapter 7 Esther names him as the wicked enemy. Terrified, he begs her for mercy and falls on her couch, which the king takes as an assault. He is hanged on the gallows he had prepared for Mordecai. His possessions go to Esther and his ring to Mordecai. The king's letter says he was hanged with his whole family at the gates of Susa, and Mordecai's letter says his calamities fell on himself and he was hanged with his children, his ten sons. Finally, Mordecai's dream interpretation identifies Haman as one of the two serpents, the other being Mordecai.

Esther (Greek) / Chapter 1

Haman, son of Hammedatha the Bougean, was honored in the king's sight and sought to harm Mordecai and his people because of the executed chamberlains. A Haman is also listed among the seven chamberlains whom the king sent on the seventh day to bring in Vashti.

Chapter 3

Haman is exalted by the king above all his friends, and he is enraged when Mordecai does not bow to him. He plots to destroy all the Jews, casts lots that fall on the fourteenth day of Adar, accuses the Jews before the king as a nation with differing laws, and offers ten thousand talents of silver. Receiving the king's ring, he commands the writing of the decrees sent to the provinces.

Chapter 4

Haman is described as holding the next place to the king and as having spoken against the Jews to cause their death. He promised the king ten thousand talents to be paid into the treasury so that he might destroy the Jews. Mordecai's prayer calls him arrogant, and Esther prays that the counsel of those against Israel be turned against themselves and that an example be made of the one who began to injure them. She also says she has not eaten at his table.

Chapter 5

Haman is invited with the king to Esther's feast and leaves glad and merry. Seeing Mordecai in the court enrages him, and he tells his friends and wife Zeresh of his wealth, his honor, and his exclusive invitation, saying these things do not please him while Mordecai remains. He is pleased by their advice to hang Mordecai and has the gallows prepared.

Chapter 6

Haman comes to the court to ask the king to hang Mordecai on the gallows he prepared, but is asked what should be done for the man the king wishes to honor. Believing it is himself, he proposes the robe, horse and proclamation, and is then ordered to do all of it for Mordecai, which he does. He goes home mourning with his head covered, is told by Zeresh and his friends that he will assuredly fall, and is rushed by the chamberlains to Esther's banquet.

Chapter 7

Haman is named by Esther as the wicked enemy and is terrified before the king and queen. He begs Esther for mercy and falls on her couch, which the king takes as an assault. He is then hanged on the gallows he had prepared for Mordecai.

Chapter 8

Haman's possessions are given to Esther and his ring is taken from him and given to Mordecai. The king's letter calls him a Macedonian, son of Hammedatha, an alien to the Persians, who plotted against the king, Mordecai, Esther, and the Jews and to transfer the Persian dominion to the Macedonians. The letter states he was hanged with his whole family at the gates of Susa.

Chapter 9

Haman is identified as the son of Hammedatha, called the Bugaean and, in Mordecai's letter, the Macedonian, and as the enemy of the Jews. The letter recounts how he decreed and cast lots to destroy them and sought Mordecai's hanging. His calamities fell on himself, and he was hanged along with his children.

Chapter 10

Haman is identified in Mordecai's interpretation of the dream as one of the two serpents, the other being Mordecai.
