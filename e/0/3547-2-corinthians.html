Abstract written by a model

This document is Paul's Second Letter to the Corinthians, thirteen chapters in which Paul, with Timothy and co-workers, writes to the assembly at Corinth and the saints in Achaia. The most important figures are Paul, the Corinthian believers, Titus, and Christ, with God as the source of comfort and power. It opens by blessing God, who comforts Paul in affliction so he can comfort others, and Paul defends his changed travel plans, insisting God's promises are 'Yes' in Christ. Chapters 2 and 7 deal with a painful earlier letter, a person who caused sorrow and should now be forgiven, and Paul's anxiety at Troas until Titus arrived in Macedonia with good news; Paul distinguishes godly sorrow leading to repentance from worldly sorrow. Chapters 3 to 5 reflect on the ministry itself: the Corinthians are Christ's living letter, the new covenant of the Spirit surpasses the glory of Moses' covenant, the ministers carry treasure in clay vessels, and the earthly tent contrasts with an eternal house. All will face Christ's judgment seat, and believers are new creations given the ministry of reconciliation as ambassadors for Christ. Chapter 6 urges the Corinthians to receive grace now, lists the ministers' hardships and paradoxes, asks them to open their hearts, and warns against being unequally yoked with unbelievers. Chapters 8 and 9 turn to the collection for the saints, holding up the generous but poor Macedonians and Christ's own becoming poor as examples, sending Titus and two brothers, and teaching that God loves a cheerful giver and that giving produces thanksgiving. Chapters 10 to 13 form a defense of Paul's apostolic authority against critics and self-commending rivals he calls false apostles. He boasts reluctantly, listing his labors, sufferings, and his escape from Damascus in a basket, and describes being caught up to the third heaven. He recounts his thorn in the flesh, to which the Lord answered that grace is sufficient and power is perfected in weakness. He announces a third visit, fears finding strife and unrepented sin, and warns he will not spare offenders. He urges the Corinthians to test themselves, closing with calls to rejoice, be of one mind, live in peace, greetings from the saints, and a benediction naming the Lord Jesus Christ, God, and the Holy Spirit.

Paul’s Second Letter to the Corinthians / Chapter 1

In 2 Corinthians 1, Paul and Timothy greet the assembly of God at Corinth and all the saints in Achaia, blessing God as the Father of mercies and God of all comfort. Paul explains that God comforts him in affliction so he can comfort others, and he describes an affliction in Asia in which he and his companions despaired of life, learning to trust in God who raises the dead. He asks for the Corinthians' prayers and defends his conduct as holy and sincere. He then explains his changed travel plans, insisting he was not fickle, because God's promises are "Yes" in Jesus Christ. He says he did not come to Corinth in order to spare them, and that he and his companions are fellow workers for their joy rather than controllers of their faith.

Chapter 2

In this chapter the author, writing in the first person, explains that he decided not to come to the recipients again in sorrow, and that he wrote to them out of much affliction and many tears to show his abundant love, not to grieve them. He says that the one who caused sorrow has grieved all of them in part, that the punishment inflicted by the many is sufficient, and that they should now forgive and comfort that person and confirm their love for him lest he be swallowed up by excessive sorrow. He says he forgives whomever they forgive, in the presence of Christ, so that Satan gains no advantage, since they are not ignorant of his schemes. He recounts that at Troas, despite an open door in the Lord, he had no relief because he did not find Titus his brother, and so he went on into Macedonia. He closes by thanking God who leads them in triumph in Christ and spreads the sweet aroma of his knowledge, being a stench of death to those who perish and a sweet aroma of life to those being saved, and he says they speak sincerely in Christ and do not peddle the word of God.

Chapter 3

In this chapter the writer, speaking for himself and his co-workers, asks whether they need letters of commendation and answers that the Corinthian believers themselves are Christ's letter, written by the Spirit of the living God on hearts of flesh. He says their sufficiency comes from God, who made them servants of a new covenant of the Spirit rather than of the letter. He contrasts the service written on stones, which came with a glory so bright that the children of Israel could not look at Moses' face, with the Spirit's service, which has much more glory and remains. Moses put a veil on his face, and the same veil still lies on the hearts of those who read the old covenant, until someone turns to the Lord. Where the Spirit of the Lord is there is liberty, and believers with unveiled faces are transformed into the Lord's image from glory to glory.

Chapter 4

In this chapter the ministers explain that, having received mercy through their ministry, they do not lose heart, and they have renounced shameful, deceitful ways in favor of openly stating the truth. They say the Good News is veiled only to those who are dying, whose minds the god of this world has blinded so they cannot see the light of the glory of Christ. They preach Christ Jesus as Lord and themselves as servants of the believers, and they describe carrying the treasure of God's light in clay vessels, so that the greatness of the power is from God. Though pressed, perplexed, pursued and struck down, they are not crushed, forsaken or destroyed, and they always carry the dying of Jesus so that his life may be revealed in them. They hold to the hope that God, who raised the Lord Jesus, will raise them and present them with the believers, and they look at unseen eternal things rather than seen temporal ones.

Chapter 5

The speaker, writing for himself and his fellow ministers, contrasts the earthly tent of the body with a house from God, eternal in the heavens, and says they groan to be clothed with it so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life. God has given the Spirit as a down payment, so they are confident, walk by faith, and aim to please the Lord whether at home in the body or absent from it. All must be revealed before the judgment seat of Christ to receive according to what they have done in the body. The love of Christ compels them because one died for all, so that the living should live for him who died and rose. Anyone in Christ is a new creation, and God, who reconciled us through Christ, has given the ministry of reconciliation, so the ministers act as ambassadors urging people to be reconciled to God, since he made the sinless one to be sin so that we might become the righteousness of God.

Chapter 6

The speaker, writing on behalf of himself and his co-workers, urges the Corinthians not to receive God's grace in vain, citing a saying of God that now is the acceptable time and the day of salvation. He and his co-workers describe how they commend themselves as servants of God by giving no cause for stumbling, enduring afflictions, beatings, imprisonments, and fastings, and by living in pureness, knowledge, kindness, the Holy Spirit, and sincere love. They present paradoxes of their ministry: dying yet living, sorrowful yet rejoicing, poor yet making many rich. The speaker says his heart is open to the Corinthians, who are restricted by their own affections rather than by him, and asks them to open their hearts in return. He then tells them not to be unequally yoked with unbelievers, contrasting righteousness with iniquity, light with darkness, Christ with Belial, and the temple of God with idols, and closes with God's promise to be a Father to those who come out and are separate.

Chapter 7

The speaker urges the beloved recipients to cleanse themselves and open their hearts, asserting that he and his companions wronged, corrupted, and took advantage of no one. He describes affliction in Macedonia, with fightings outside and fear inside, until God comforted them through the coming of Titus, who brought news of the recipients' longing, mourning, and zeal. The speaker explains that his earlier letter grieved them only for a while, and that he rejoices because their grief led to repentance, distinguishing godly sorrow that leads to salvation from worldly sorrow that produces death. He says he wrote so that their earnest care for him would be revealed before God, and that Titus was refreshed by them all. The speaker's boasts about them to Titus proved true, and he closes by rejoicing in his confidence concerning them.

Chapter 8

The writer, speaking to the brothers, holds up the assemblies of Macedonia as an example: despite severe affliction and deep poverty they gave generously, beyond their power and of their own accord, first giving themselves to the Lord. Because of this, the writer urged Titus to complete among the addressees the grace of giving he had begun. The addressees, who abound in faith, utterance, knowledge and love, are urged to abound in this grace too, following the example of Jesus Christ, who became poor so they might become rich. Giving should match what one has, aiming at equality so that present abundance and later abundance supply each other's lack. Titus and two other brothers are sent to administer the gift honorably, and the addressees are asked to show proof of their love to them.

Chapter 9

In Chapter 9 the writer, Paul, tells the believers in Achaia that he need not write at length about the service to the saints, since he knows their readiness and has boasted of it to those in Macedonia. He sends the brothers ahead so that the promised gift will be ready and so that his boasting is not shown to be empty if Macedonians come with him and find Achaia unprepared. He teaches that sowing sparingly brings a sparing harvest, sowing bountifully a bountiful one, and that each should give willingly because God loves a cheerful giver. He explains that God supplies and multiplies seed and the fruits of righteousness. The service of giving meets the saints' needs and produces thanksgiving to God, and the saints will glorify God and yearn for the givers; the chapter closes with thanks to God for his unspeakable gift.

Chapter 10

In this chapter Paul appeals to the Corinthian audience by the humility and gentleness of Christ, saying he is lowly in person but bold when absent, and he hopes not to have to be bold against critics who think he walks according to the flesh. He explains that his weapons of warfare are mighty before God for throwing down strongholds, imaginations and every high thing raised against the knowledge of God, and for bringing every thought captive to Christ. He defends his authority, given by the Lord for building up, against the charge that his letters are strong but his presence and speech are weak, insisting that he is the same in deed as in word. He contrasts himself with self-commending rivals who measure themselves by themselves, saying he boasts only within the limits God appointed, which reach to Corinth. He hopes to preach beyond them as their faith grows, and concludes that one should boast in the Lord, since the one the Lord commends is approved.

Chapter 11

In Chapter 11 Paul expresses godly jealousy for the Corinthians, whom he promised to one husband, Christ, and fears they will be led astray from simple devotion to Christ as the serpent deceived Eve. He defends his standing against the very best apostles, reminding them that he preached God's Good News free of charge, supported by brothers from Macedonia, and will keep doing so. He denounces false apostles as deceitful workers who masquerade as apostles of Christ, just as Satan masquerades as an angel of light. Reluctantly boasting as a fool, he lists his Hebrew heritage and his labors, prisons, beatings, stoning, shipwrecks, perils, hardships and daily anxiety for all the assemblies. He ends by choosing to boast of his weakness, recalling his escape from the governor under King Aretas in Damascus by being let down in a basket through a window in the wall.

Chapter 12

In this chapter the speaker, who is Paul, reluctantly turns to visions and revelations of the Lord and describes a man in Christ who was caught up into the third heaven and Paradise fourteen years earlier and heard unspeakable words. Paul says he will boast only of such a man and of his own weaknesses, and that to keep him from being exalted excessively he was given a thorn in the flesh, a messenger of Satan. He begged the Lord three times for its removal, and the Lord answered that his grace is sufficient and his power is made perfect in weakness. Paul then defends his apostleship to the Corinthian believers, reminding them of the signs and wonders worked among them and that he was never a financial burden to them, and he says he is ready to come a third time. He closes by fearing that he will find strife, factions and other disorders among them, and that he will mourn for those who have sinned and not repented of uncleanness, sexual immorality and lustfulness.

Chapter 13

In the closing chapter of the letter, Paul tells the Corinthian believers that he is coming to them a third time and warns those who have sinned that he will not spare them when he arrives. He says they are seeking proof that Christ speaks in him, and notes that Christ was crucified in weakness yet lives by God's power, as Paul and his companions are weak in him yet will live by that power toward them. Paul urges the Corinthians to examine and test themselves to see whether they are in the faith and whether Jesus Christ is in them. He prays that they do what is honorable and become perfect, and explains that he writes while absent so that he need not deal sharply when present, using the authority the Lord gave him for building up. He ends with final exhortations to rejoice, be perfected, be of one mind and live in peace, greetings from all the saints, and a benediction naming the Lord Jesus Christ, God, and the Holy Spirit.
