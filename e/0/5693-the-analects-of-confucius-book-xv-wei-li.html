The Analects of Confucius, Book XV (Wei Ling Kung)

Abstract written by a model

This record is Book XV of The Analects of Confucius, a sequence of teachings and incidents centered on the Master’s ideals of virtue, learning, judgment, government, and the superior man. It opens with the Duke Ling of Wei seeking military advice; Confucius declines, saying his learning concerns sacrificial vessels rather than war, and leaves. In Chan, hardship tests his followers, and he explains that the superior man can endure want without descending into license. Across the book, Confucius teaches that his learning is unified by an all-pervading principle and repeatedly defines moral character. The superior man makes righteousness central, joins it with propriety, humility, and sincerity, seeks within himself, bears concern for his own ability rather than others’ recognition, remains dignified without wrangling, and preserves virtue even at the cost of life. Virtue is presented as more necessary than water or fire, a responsibility no one should surrender even to a teacher, and something to be valued above food, wealth, and fear of poverty. Students including Tsze-kung, Tsze-chang, Yen Yuan, and Yu ask questions about conduct, virtue, and rule. The Master recommends truthful speech, careful action, reciprocity, never doing to others what one would not want done to oneself, and serving worthy officers and befriending virtuous scholars. He advises rulers to follow proper models, avoid licentious songs and specious talkers, govern with dignity, and move people according to propriety. The later teachings warn against shallow cleverness, misleading words, impatience, unexamined public opinion, failure to reform faults, and thought without learning. Confucius urges careful judgment of both people and words, praise grounded in examination, and foresight about distant consequences. He laments vanished practices of historical honesty and generosity, insists that teaching make no class distinctions, and says language need only convey meaning. The book ends with a practical display of considerate guidance: when the blind Music-master Mien visits, Confucius identifies the path, mat, and those present, explaining that this is the proper rule for leading the blind.

The Analects of Confucius, Book XV (Wei Ling Kung) / CHAP. I. 1. The Duke Ling of Wei asked Confucius about

The Duke Ling of Wei asks Confucius about tactics. Confucius says he has heard about sacrificial vessels but has not learned military matters, and he departs the next day. In Chan, provisions are exhausted and Confucius's followers become too ill to rise; Tsze-lu questions whether the superior man must endure such hardship, and Confucius explains that the superior man may endure want while the mean man gives way to unbridled license.

CHAP. II. 1. The Master said, 'Ts'ze, you think, I suppose, that

The Master asks Ts'ze-kung whether he thinks the Master learns many things and keeps them in memory. Tsze-kung agrees, while allowing that it may not be so. The Master explains that he seeks a unity all-pervading.

CHAP. III. The Master said, 'Yu, those who know virtue are

The Master addresses Yu. He says that those who know virtue are few.

CHAP. IV. The Master said, 'May not Shun be instanced as

The Master asks whether Shun may be cited as an example of governing efficiently without exertion. He says that Shun did nothing except gravely and reverently occupy his royal seat. The unit concerns Shun’s efficient rule and The Master’s description of it.

CHAP. V. 1. Tsze-chang asked how a man should conduct

Tsze-chang asks the Master how a man should conduct himself so as to be appreciated everywhere. The Master teaches that sincere and truthful words and honourable and careful actions are respected even among rude tribes, while their opposites are not appreciated even in one’s neighborhood. He instructs the man to keep these principles constantly before him and carry them into practice. Tsze-chang writes the counsels on the end of his sash.

CHAP. VI. 1. The Master said, 'Truly straightforward was the

The Master contrasts historiographer Yu and Chu Po-yu under good and bad government. When good government prevailed in his State, historiographer Yu was like an arrow, and when bad government prevailed, he was also like an arrow. Chu Po-yu is praised as a superior man who holds office under good government and keeps his principles in his breast under bad government.

CHAP. VII. The Master said, 'When a man may be spoken

The Master teaches about when a man should or should not be spoken with. Speaking to a man who may be spoken with is proper, while failing to speak to him is an error concerning the man. Speaking to a man who may not be spoken with is an error concerning the words, and the wise avoid both kinds of error.

CHAP. VIII. The Master said, 'The determined scholar and the

The Master discusses the determined scholar and the man of virtue. They will not seek to live at the expense of injuring their virtue. They will even sacrifice their lives to preserve their virtue complete.

CHAP. IX. Tsze-kung asked about the practice of virtue. The

Tsze-kung asks the Master about the practice of virtue. The Master compares virtuous practice to a mechanic sharpening his tools before working. He advises people living in any state to serve its most worthy great officers and befriend its most virtuous scholars.

CHAP. X. 1. Yen Yuan asked how the government of a country

Yen Yuan asks the Master how the government of a country should be administered. The Master recommends following the seasons of Hsia, riding in the state carriage of Yin, and wearing the ceremonial cap of Chau. He also advises using the Shao with its pantomimes. He says to banish the songs of Chang and keep far from specious talkers because the songs are licentious and the talkers are dangerous.

CHAP. XI. The Master said, 'If a man take no thought about

The Master teaches that a man should think about what is distant. He warns that failing to do so will bring sorrow near at hand. The unit concerns the relationship between distant thought and near sorrow.

CHAP. XII. The Master said, 'It is all over! I have not seen one

The Master says that it is all over. He states that he has not seen one who loves virtue as he loves beauty. The statement concerns the rarity of loving virtue as deeply as beauty.

CHAP. XIII. The Master said, 'Was not Tsang Wan like one

The Master asks whether Tsang Wan was like one who had stolen his situation. He says Tsang Wan knew the virtue and talents of Hui of Liu-hsia. Despite this knowledge, Tsang Wan did not procure that Hui of Liu-hsia should stand with him in court.

CHAP. XIV. The Master said, 'He who requires much from

The Master teaches that a person who requires much from himself and little from others will avoid becoming the object of resentment. The unit concerns self-expectation, expectations of others, and resentment.

CHAP. XV. The Master said, 'When a man is not in the habit of

The Master says that a man who is not in the habit of asking himself what he should think about something is difficult to help. He repeats the question, "What shall I think of this?" to illustrate the needed habit. He concludes that he can do nothing with such a man.

CHAP. XVI. The Master said, 'When a number of people are

The Master describes a group of people who spend a whole day together. Their conversation does not turn on righteousness. Instead, they favor carrying out suggestions based on small shrewdness, which the Master says is a hard case.

CHAP. XVII. The Master said, 'The superior man in everything

The Master describes the qualities of a superior man. The superior man considers righteousness essential in everything and performs it according to the rules of propriety. He brings it forth in humility and completes it with sincerity.

CHAP. XVIII. The Master said, 'The superior man is distressed

The Master says that the superior man is distressed by his lack of ability. The superior man is not distressed when other men do not know him. The unit concerns The Master, the superior man, and the causes of distress.

CHAP. XIX. The Master said, 'The superior man dislikes the

The Master says that the superior man dislikes the thought of his name not being mentioned after his death. The statement concerns the superior man's desire for his name to be remembered beyond his lifetime.

CHAP. XX. The Master said, 'What the superior man seeks, is

The Master states that the superior man seeks within himself. He contrasts this with the mean man, who seeks in others.

CHAP. XXI. The Master said, 'The superior man is dignified,

The Master teaches that the superior man is dignified and does not wrangle. He also teaches that the superior man is sociable but not a partizan.

CHAP. XXII. The Master said, 'The superior man does not

The Master teaches that the superior man does not promote a man merely because of his words. He also teaches that the superior man does not reject good words because of the man who speaks them. The unit concerns how the superior man evaluates men and words.

CHAP. XXIII. Tsze-kung asked, saying, 'Is there one word

Tsze-kung asks the Master whether one word can serve as a rule of practice for all one's life. The Master identifies reciprocity as such a word. He explains it as not doing to others what one does not want done to oneself.

CHAP. XXIV. 1. The Master said, 'In my dealings with men,

The Master reflects on his dealings with men and asks whose evil he blames and whose goodness he praises beyond what is proper. He says that if he sometimes exceeds in praise, his examination of the individual must provide grounds for it. He states that this people supplied the ground why the three dynasties pursued the path of straightforwardness.

CHAP. XXV. The Master said, 'Even in my early days, a

The Master recalls that in his early days, a historiographer would leave a blank in his text. He also recalls that a person who had a horse would lend it to another to ride. He laments that neither of these practices exists anymore.

CHAP. XXVI. The Master said, 'Specious words confound

The Master says that specious words confound virtue. He also says that want of forbearance in small matters confounds great plans. The statement concerns the effects of misleading speech and impatience on virtue and larger plans.

CHAP. XXVII. The Master said, 'When the multitude hate a

The Master says that when the multitude hates a man, the case must be examined. He also says that when the multitude likes a man, the case must be examined. The teaching concerns how to assess the multitude's opinions of a man.

CHAP. XXVIII. The Master said, 'A man can enlarge the

The Master teaches that a man can enlarge the principles he follows. He says that those principles do not enlarge the man. The unit concerns the relationship between a man and his principles.

CHAP. XXIX. The Master said, 'To have faults and not to

The Master teaches that having faults is not the central problem. The real fault is failing to reform those faults. His statement concerns how a person responds to their own faults.

CHAP. XXX. The Master said, 'I have been the whole day

The Master says he spent the whole day without eating and the whole night without sleeping. He was occupied with thinking. He concludes that thinking was of no use and that the better plan is to learn.

CHAP. XXXI. The Master said, 'The object of the superior man

The Master explains that the superior man makes truth his object rather than food. He says that plowing can sometimes bring want, while learning may bring emolument. The superior man worries about failing to attain truth, not about poverty coming upon him.

CHAP. XXXII. 1. The Master said, 'When a man's knowledge is

The Master explains how knowledge, virtue, dignity, and propriety determine whether a man can retain what he gains and earn the people’s respect. A man who has knowledge enough to attain but lacks sufficient virtue to hold what he gains will lose it again. Even when he has knowledge and virtue, he must govern with dignity, and full excellence is not reached if he moves the people contrary to the rules of propriety.

CHAP. XXXIII. The Master said, 'The superior man cannot be

The Master distinguishes between the superior man and the small man. The superior man cannot be known in little matters but may be entrusted with great concerns. The small man may not be entrusted with great concerns but may be known in little matters.

CHAP. XXXIV. The Master said, 'Virtue is more to man than

The Master teaches that virtue is more important to man than water or fire. He says he has seen men die from stepping on water and fire. He has never seen a man die from following the course of virtue.

CHAP. XXXV. The Master said, 'Let every man consider virtue

The Master teaches that every man should regard virtue as his own responsibility. He says that a man must not yield the performance of virtue even to his teacher. The unit concerns the relationship between every man, virtue, and the Master’s instruction.

CHAP. XXXVI. The Master said, 'The superior man is correctly

The Master says that the superior man is correctly firm. He distinguishes correct firmness from being merely firm. The statement concerns the superior man and the quality of his firmness.

CHAP. XXXVII. The Master said, 'A minister, in serving his

The Master teaches about a minister's conduct in serving his prince. He says the minister should reverently discharge his duties. The minister should treat his emolument as a secondary consideration.

CHAP. XXXVIII. The Master said, 'In teaching there should be

The Master states that teaching should have no distinction of classes. The unit concerns the principle of equal treatment in teaching.

CHAP. XXXIX. The Master said, 'Those whose courses are

The Master states that those whose courses are different cannot make plans for one another. The teaching concerns people with differing courses and their inability to devise plans together.

CHAP. XL. The Master said, 'In language it is simply required

The Master states that language has a simple requirement. He says that language must convey the meaning.

CHAP. XLI. 1. The Music-master, Mien, having called upon

The unit concerns the Music-master Mien visiting the Master. The Master guides Mien by identifying the steps and the mat, then informs him who is present after they are seated. Tsze-chang asks whether this is the rule for speaking to the Music-master. The Master confirms that it is the rule for those who lead the blind.
