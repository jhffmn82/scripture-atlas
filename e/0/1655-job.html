Abstract written by a model

In The Book of Job, Job is a blameless and upright man who endures severe trials, maintains his integrity, repents, and is restored. In The Book of Job, Job is accused by Eliphaz of wickedness and urged to return to the Almighty. In The Book of Job, Job is addressed by Eliphaz, Zophar, and Elihu through reminders, accusations, and challenges. In The Book of Ezekiel, Job is named as a righteous man whose righteousness would save only himself. In Surah 21: The Prophets (Al-Anbiyaa), Job cries to Allah about adversity, and Allah removes his affliction and restores his household. In Surah 38: The letter Saad (Saad), Job cries to Allah about affliction, is restored, and is described as steadfast and repentant.

Job in The Book of Job

author unknown · about 574 BC · the work’s page · read it where Job first appears

Abstract written by a model

Job is a blameless, upright, God-fearing man of Uz, called 'my servant' by the LORD. He is the greatest of the children of the east, with seven sons, three daughters, vast livestock, and a very great household. He continually offers burnt offerings for his children. In a series of disasters he loses his livestock, servants, and all his children, yet he worships, blesses the LORD's name, and does not charge God with wrongdoing. Struck with painful sores from head to foot, he sits in ashes, rebukes his wife, and still does not sin with his lips. The records stress that he keeps his integrity throughout. Three friends, Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar, come to him. Job then curses the day of his birth, sighs and groans, and wishes God would crush him, though he takes consolation that he has not denied the Holy One's words. In his replies he calls his friends miserable comforters and deceitful brothers, claims understanding equal to theirs, and insists his cause is righteous. He feels watched, worn out, treated as an enemy, and set in stocks by God. He longs for an umpire, a witness in heaven, and a Redeemer, and expects to see God in his flesh. He wants to reason with God and believes he will come out like gold, yet he is terrified before God and cannot find him. He swears he will hold fast to his righteousness until death. He recalls his former standing: he delivered the poor and fatherless, was eyes to the blind, sat as chief, and was honored by listeners. In his final defense he lists his faithfulness and challenges the Almighty to answer. When the three friends stop answering, Elihu grows angry, quoting Job as claiming he is righteous, and accuses him of rebellion, empty talk and justifying himself rather than God. The LORD then addresses Job as one who darkens counsel by words without knowledge and asks long questions about creation, behemoth and Leviathan. Job calls himself of small account, lays his hand on his mouth, confesses he spoke of things he did not understand, and repents in dust and ashes. The LORD calls him his servant, says he spoke rightly, and accepts his prayer for his three friends. Job is restored with twice his former wealth, has seven sons and three daughters again, lives one hundred forty more years, sees four generations, and dies old and full of days.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

27 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 1

Job is introduced as a blameless, upright, God-fearing man in the land of Uz, with ten children and enormous wealth, who continually offers burnt offerings in case his sons have sinned. After losing his livestock, servants and all his children in a series of disasters, he tears his robe, shaves his head, falls to the ground and worships, blessing the LORD's name. The text states that in all this he did not sin or charge God with wrongdoing.

Chapter 2

Job is described by the LORD as blameless, upright, God-fearing and still maintaining his integrity. Struck with painful sores from head to foot, he sits among the ashes scraping himself with a potsherd. He rebukes his wife, asking whether people should receive good from God and not evil, and does not sin with his lips.

Chapter 3

Job opens his mouth and curses the day of his birth, wishing it and the night of his conception had never been. He asks why he did not die at birth, imagining death as rest among kings, princes, and the weary. He describes himself as sighing and groaning, never at ease, and overtaken by the thing he feared, as trouble comes.

Chapter 6

Job answers his friends, saying his anguish outweighs the sand of the seas and excusing his rash words on that ground. He wishes God would crush him and cut him off, yet takes consolation that he has not denied the words of the Holy One. He asks to be shown his error, accuses his brothers of deceit, and insists his cause is righteous and his tongue free of injustice.

Chapter 7

Job describes his suffering as months of misery and wearisome nights, with flesh clothed in worms and dust and skin that closes and breaks out afresh. He vows not to keep silent and complains to God in the bitterness of his soul, saying he loathes his life and would choose strangling and death. He asks why God watches and burdens him, and why God does not pardon his disobedience, noting that he will soon lie in the dust and be no more.

Chapter 9

Job responds by asking how man can be just with God, and says he could not answer God once in a thousand times. He insists he is blameless yet believes his own mouth would condemn him, and that God would not hold him innocent. He says his days pass swifter than a runner, that he cannot forget his complaint or cheer up, and that washing himself with snow would not help. He longs for an umpire between himself and God, and wishes God's rod and terror removed so he could speak without fear.

Chapter 10

Job, weary of his life, gives free course to his complaint and speaks in bitterness of soul to God. He pleads not to be condemned, maintains that God knows he is not wicked, and says that even if righteous he cannot lift his head for disgrace. He wishes he had died at birth and asks to be left alone for a little comfort before he dies.

Chapter 12

Job answers his friends sarcastically, claiming he has understanding equal to theirs and is not inferior. He laments that though he is just and blameless and called on God, who answered him, he has become a joke to his neighbor. He then affirms that wisdom, might, and control over rulers and nations belong to God.

Chapter 13

Job declares that he has seen and understood all that his friends know and is not inferior to them. He says he wants to reason with God, will maintain his ways even though he expects God to kill him, and believes he is righteous. He asks God to withdraw his hand and terror, to tell him his sins, and laments that he is treated as an enemy and is decaying like a rotten thing.

Chapter 14

The speaker laments human frailty and asks God to look away from man so he may rest like a hireling finishing his day. He wishes to be hidden in Sheol until God's wrath passes, says he would wait all the days of his warfare for release, and imagines answering God's call. He feels watched for his sin and ends in pain, with his soul mourning within him.

Chapter 16

Job answers his friends, calling them miserable comforters and saying he could speak as they do, but would instead strengthen them. He says God has worn him out and broken him, and he mourns in sackcloth with his horn in the dust. He maintains there is no violence in his hands and that his prayer is pure, and he declares that he has a witness in heaven who vouches for him. He pours out tears to God, asks that his right be maintained, and notes that he will soon go the way of no return.

Chapter 19

Job rebukes his friends for tormenting him and says that if he has erred, the error remains with himself. He laments that God has walled up his way, stripped his glory, and that his family, servants, and friends have all turned from him. He pleads for pity, wishes his words were engraved in rock, and declares that he knows his Redeemer lives and that he will see God in his flesh.

Chapter 21

Job answers his friends, asking them to listen and to let that be their consolation, and says his complaint is not to man and that he is impatient. He argues that the wicked often prosper and die at ease, rejecting their counsel, and accuses his friends of plotting to wrong him. He concludes that their comfort is nonsense and their answers remain only falsehood.

Chapter 23

Job answers by wishing he could find God and come to his seat to argue his case in order, confident that God would listen to him. He cannot locate God in any direction, but he insists God knows his way and that he will come out like gold after being tried. He claims he has held fast to God's steps, not turned from his commands, and valued God's words more than necessary food, yet he is terrified and faint of heart before God and was not cut off before the darkness.

Chapter 26

Job answers with sarcasm, asking how the one he addresses has helped the powerless, saved the weak, and counseled the unwise, and whose spirit spoke through him. He then gives a long description of God's power over the dead, the earth, the waters, the heavens, and the sea. He concludes that people hear only a small whisper of God and cannot understand the thunder of his power.

Chapter 27

Job takes up his parable again and swears by the living God, who he says has taken away his right and made his soul bitter, that he will not speak unrighteousness or deceit. He refuses to justify his friends and declares he will hold fast to his righteousness until death. He then promises to teach about God's hand and describes the fate of the wicked.

Chapter 29

Job longs for the past, recalling when God watched over him and his children were around him. He describes himself as a man who delivered the poor, the fatherless, and the widow, wore righteousness and justice like a robe, and was eyes to the blind and feet to the lame. He broke the jaws of the unrighteous, expected to die in his own house, and sat as chief among men, comforting mourners.

Chapter 31

Job speaks his final defense, claiming he has kept a covenant with his eyes and has been faithful, just, generous to servants, the poor, the widow and the fatherless, and hospitable to strangers. He invites severe penalties on himself if any of his claims are false, and challenges the Almighty to answer him. He says he would present the number of his steps to God like a prince, and the chapter closes with the note that his words are ended.

Chapter 32

Job is no longer answered by his three friends because he is righteous in his own eyes. Elihu is angry at him for justifying himself rather than God. Elihu also says that none of the friends convinced Job or answered his words.

Chapter 33

Job is addressed directly and told to listen, to set his words in order and answer, and to hold his peace if he has nothing to say. The speaker quotes Job as having said that he is clean, without disobedience, innocent, and that God finds occasions against him, counts him an enemy, puts his feet in the stocks, and marks his paths. He is told he is not just in this and is offered the chance to be justified or taught wisdom.

Chapter 34

Job is quoted by Elihu as having said that he is righteous, that God has taken away his right, that he is called a liar despite being without disobedience, and that it profits a man nothing to delight in God. Elihu charges him with drinking scorn like water, walking with wicked men, speaking without wisdom, clapping his hands among them, and adding rebellion to his sin.

Chapter 35

Job is charged by Elihu with saying that his righteousness is more than God's and with asking what profit he has more than if he had sinned. Elihu says Job says he does not see God, and that he speaks empty talk and multiplies words without knowledge.

Chapter 37

Job is addressed directly and told to listen, stand still, and consider the wondrous works of God. He is asked whether he knows how God controls the clouds and lightning, knows the workings of the clouds, or can spread out the sky with God. These questions imply that he cannot.

Chapter 38

Job is addressed by the LORD as one who darkens counsel by words without knowledge and is told to brace himself like a man. He is met with a long series of questions about creation, the sea, light, weather, the stars, and animals, none of which he answers in this unit, and the questions imply he was not present and lacks the knowledge and power they describe.

Chapter 40

Job answers the LORD by saying he is of small account, lays his hand on his mouth, and says he has spoken once and twice but will proceed no further. He is then told to brace himself, is questioned about annulling the LORD's judgment and condemning him to be justified, and is challenged to show an arm and voice like God's. He is directed to consider behemoth.

Chapter 41

Job is addressed with a series of rhetorical questions about whether he could catch, bind, sell, or fight Leviathan. He is told to lay his hand on the creature, remember the battle, and do so no more, and that even the sight of Leviathan casts one down. He is thereby challenged to consider who can stand before the speaker.

Chapter 42

Job answers the LORD, confessing that he spoke of things he did not understand, and repents in dust and ashes. The LORD calls him his servant, says he spoke rightly, and accepts his prayer for his three friends. He is restored with twice his former wealth, has seven sons and three daughters, lives one hundred forty more years, sees four generations, and dies old and full of days.

Job (the addressee of Eliphaz) in The Book of Job

author unknown · about 574 BC · the work’s page · read it where Job (the addressee of Eliphaz) first appears

Abstract written by a model

Job is a man addressed in successive speeches by Eliphaz, Zophar, and Elihu, with a final unit in which an unnamed listener is questioned about wild animals. Eliphaz first recalls that Job once instructed many and strengthened the weak, yet now faints under trouble, and he points to Job's piety and integrity as his confidence. Zophar quotes him as claiming pure doctrine and cleanness, calls him a boaster, says he deserves more punishment than God exacts, and urges him to set his heart right and put away iniquity, promising security and restoration. Eliphaz later charges him with vain words, hindering devotion, and speaking against God. Elihu says he is full of the judgment of the wicked, warns against wrath and bribes, says God would have drawn him out of distress, and tells him to magnify God's work. In the last unit, he is asked whether he understands or controls wild creatures. Over time, the address shifts from reminder to accusation to challenge.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

5 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 4

Job is addressed by Eliphaz as one who has instructed many and strengthened weak hands and feeble knees, but who now faints and is troubled when trouble touches him. Eliphaz asks whether Job's piety and the integrity of his ways are not his confidence and hope.

Chapter 11

The addressee is accused by Zophar of being a man full of talk, of boasting and mocking, and of claiming 'My doctrine is pure. I am clean in your eyes.' Zophar says he deserves more punishment than God exacts, and urges him to set his heart aright, stretch out his hands to God, and put away iniquity. If he does, Zophar promises he will lift his face without spot, forget his misery, be secure, rest in safety, and be courted by many.

Chapter 15

Job, the one Eliphaz addresses, is accused of speaking vain and unprofitable words, doing away with fear, and hindering devotion before God. Eliphaz says his own mouth and lips condemn him, questions whether he is the first man born or holds God's counsel, and charges that his heart carries him away so that he lets words against God leave his mouth.

Chapter 36

The person Elihu addresses is told he is full of the judgment of the wicked, with judgment and justice taking hold of him. He is warned not to let riches entice him to wrath, not to let a large bribe turn him aside, and not to desire the night. Elihu says God would have allured him out of distress into a wide place with a full table, and that he has chosen iniquity rather than affliction. He is also told to remember to magnify God's work.

Chapter 39

Job is not named in this unit. The listener addressed with questions such as 'Do you know...' and 'Can you...' is asked whether he understands or controls the birth, freedom, strength, and flight of wild animals.
