Abstract written by a model

In The Tao Teh King, or The Tao and Its Characteristics, the state is associated with reproach that a person may accept and thereby be hailed as its altars' lord. In Mencius, Book II (Gong Sun Chou), the State is described as secured by principles concerning proper rule and support rather than by the strengths of mountains and rivers. In Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou), the State is described as having the family as its root and as surviving only by chance without principled rulers, lawful ministers, propriety, and learning. In Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou), States are described as flourishing, enduring, decaying, or perishing according to benevolence. In Mencius, Book V (Wan Zhang), the State is described in relation to a prince losing it and a high minister leaving it after repeated remonstrance fails. In Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), the State is described as an object of tranquillity sought and secured by certain ministers.

Abstract written by a model

A State has the family as its root and affects those affected by great families. Without principled rulers, lawful ministers, propriety, and learning, it survives only by chance and perishes quickly when seditious people spring up.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou) / Part I / Chapter 1

A State may be preserved only by fortunate chance when its rulers lack principles and its ministers lack laws governing their duties. It is ruined when superiors disregard propriety and inferiors do not learn.

Chapter 5

The State has the family as its root.

Chapter 6

States flourish, endure, decay, or perish according to benevolence; lacking it, princes lead States to smite themselves first, then suffer destruction by others.
