Yu

person · 17 works · 48 quoted facts · 9 connections
Also called: Ch'iu, Chi Lu, Chi-lu, Chung Yu, Chung-yu, This Yu, Tsai Yu, Tsze-lu, Yen Ch'iu, Zan Yu, the philosopher Yu

Abstract written by a model

In The Analects of Confucius, the philosopher Yu teaches that filial piety and fraternal submission are the root of benevolence and that ease in practising propriety must be regulated by propriety's rules. In The Analects of Confucius, Book V (Kung-Ye Ch'Ang), Tsze-lu is portrayed as daring, generous, suited to military administration, eager to act, and willing to share possessions with friends. In The Analects of Confucius, Book V (Kung-Ye Ch'Ang), Ch'iu is considered employable as governor of a city of a thousand families or a clan of a hundred chariots, though his perfect virtue is uncertain. In The Analects of Confucius, Book V (Kung-Ye Ch'Ang): Tsai Yu is found asleep during the daytime, prompting the Master to compare him to rotten wood and dirty earth. In The Analects of Confucius, Book VI (Yung Yey): Tsze-lu was displeased after the Master visited Nan-tsze. In The Analects of Confucius, Book VI (Yung Yey), Chung-yu is described as decisive, fit for government office, and able to serve as an officer without difficulty. In The Analects of Confucius, Book VI (Yung Yey), Yen Ch'iu is described as a man of various ability fit for government service who delights in the doctrines but says his strength is insufficient. In The Analects of Confucius, Book VIII (T'ai-po), Yu is praised for having no flaw in his character and for his filial piety, simple living, sacrificial elegance, and labor on ditches and water-channels.

Tsze-lu in The Analects of Confucius, Book V (Kung-Ye Ch'Ang)

author unknown · 474 to 220 BC · the work’s page · read it where Tsze-lu first appears

Abstract written by a model

Tsze-lu is portrayed as daring, action-driven, and generous with friends. The Master identifies Yu as Tsze-lu, saying he is fonder of daring but lacks judgment in matters. Tsze-lu may manage military levies in a kingdom of a thousand chariots, though the Master does not affirm his perfect virtue. He fears hearing something new before carrying prior learning into practice. At the Master's side as Chi Lu, he wishes to share chariots, horses, and light fur dresses with friends without displeasure if they spoil them.
