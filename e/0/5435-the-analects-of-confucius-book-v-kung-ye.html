The Analects of Confucius, Book V (Kung-Ye Ch'Ang)

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

This document is a sequence of sayings and judgments by the Master about character, learning, conduct, and fitness for public life. It begins with his confidence in Kung-ye Ch'ang and Nan Yung, expressed through marriages into his family, and praises Tsze-chien’s virtue as connected with virtuous men in Lu. The Master assesses several disciples: Tsze-kung is a gemmed sacrificial utensil but inferior to Hui in understanding; Tsze-lu, Ch'iu, and Ch'ih have evident abilities yet are not confirmed as perfectly virtuous; Ch'i-tiao K'ai pleases him by declining office until ready. He criticizes empty eloquence, impulsiveness, daytime sleep, passion, and unsupported claims of moral achievement, while valuing conduct over words. The sayings also evaluate public figures and exemplary behavior. Kung-wan is honored for learning from inferiors; Tsze-ch'an is humble, respectful, kind, and just; Yen P'ing sustains respect in friendship; and Tsze-wan and Ch'an Wan are called loyal and pure without being declared perfectly virtuous. The Master questions Tsang Wan’s ornamented tortoise house and Wei-shang Kao’s uprightness, praises Po-i and Shu-ch'i for not retaining former wrongs, and rejects flattering speech, affected respect, and concealed resentment. Amid concern that his doctrines make no way, the Master imagines leaving by sea, calls for a return from Ch'an, and urges his ambitious pupils to learn restraint and form. The document closes with wishes voiced by Chi Lu, Yen Yuan, and the Master: generosity, modesty, rest for the aged, sincerity toward friends, and tenderness toward the young. It ends with the Master’s disappointment at people’s failure to recognize their own faults, alongside his insistence that love of learning distinguishes him even from honourable and sincere people.

CHAP. I. 1. The Master said of Kung-ye Ch'ang that he might

The Master said that Kung-ye Ch'ang might be wived, though he had been put in bonds without committing any crime. Accordingly, The Master gave Kung-ye Ch'ang his own daughter to wife. The Master said that Nan Yung would remain in office under good government and escape punishment and disgrace under bad government, and gave him the daughter of his own elder brother to wife.

CHAP. II. The Master said of Tsze-chien, 'Of superior virtue

The Master says that Tsze-chien is indeed a man of superior virtue. He asks how Tsze-chien could have acquired this character if there were not virtuous men in Lu. The statement connects Tsze-chien’s character with the presence of virtuous men in Lu.

CHAP. III. Tsze-kung asked, 'What do you say of me, Ts'ze?

Tsze-kung asks the Master what he says of him. The Master says Tsze-kung is a utensil. When Tsze-kung asks what kind, the Master identifies him as a gemmed sacrificial utensil.

CHAP. IV. 1. Some one said, 'Yung is truly virtuous, but he is

Someone says that Yung is truly virtuous but is not ready with his tongue. The Master questions the value of readiness with the tongue and says that smartnesses of speech often procure hatred. He says he does not know whether Yung is truly virtuous, but sees no reason for him to show readiness of the tongue.

CHAP. V. The Master was wishing Ch'i-tiao K'ai to enter on

The Master wishes Ch'i-tiao K'ai to enter on official employment. Ch'i-tiao K'ai replies that he is not yet able to rest in the assurance of this. The Master is pleased by the reply.

CHAP. VI. The Master said, 'My doctrines make no way. I will

The Master says that his doctrines are not making progress. He proposes getting on a raft and floating about on the sea, and suggests that Yu would accompany him. Tsze-lu is glad to hear this, but the Master says Yu is fonder of daring than he is and does not exercise judgment upon matters.

CHAP. VII. 1. Mang Wu asked about Tsze-lu, whether he was

Mang Wu asks The Master whether Tsze-lu is perfectly virtuous. The Master says Tsze-lu might manage military levies in a kingdom of a thousand chariots, but he does not know whether Tsze-lu is perfectly virtuous. The Master likewise says Ch'iu might govern a city of a thousand families or a clan of a hundred chariots, and Ch'ih might converse with visitors and guests in a court, but he does not know whether either is perfectly virtuous.

CHAP. VII. 1. The Master said to Tsze-kung, 'Which do you

The Master asks Tsze-kung whether he or Hui is superior. Tsze-kung says he dares not compare himself with Hui because Hui understands an entire subject after hearing one point, while Tsze-kung understands only a second point. The Master agrees that Tsze-kung is not equal to Hui.

CHAP. IX. 1. Tsai Yu being asleep during the daytime, the

The unit concerns the Master and Tsai Yu. Seeing Tsai Yu asleep during the daytime, the Master says that rotten wood cannot be carved and dirty earth cannot receive the trowel. The Master says that he once trusted men’s words as evidence of their conduct, but now examines their conduct as well. He says that Tsai Yu taught him to make this change.

CHAP. X. The Master said, 'I have not seen a firm and

The Master says he has not seen a firm and unbending man. Someone replies that Shan Ch'ang is such a man. The Master says Shan Ch'ang is under the influence of his passions and therefore cannot be pronounced firm and unbending.

CHAP. XI. Tsze-kung said, 'What I do not wish men to do to

Tsze-kung says that he does not wish men to do to him what he also wishes not to do to them. The Master responds by addressing Ts'ze and says that he has not attained to that. The unit concerns Tsze-kung's principle and the Master's judgment of his attainment.

CHAP. XII. Tsze-kung said, 'The Master's personal displays of

Tsze-kung says that the Master's personal displays of his principles and ordinary descriptions of them can be heard. He says that the Master's discourses about man's nature and the way of Heaven cannot be heard. The unit concerns what can and cannot be heard from the Master.

CHAP. XIII. When Tsze-lu heard anything, if he had not yet

Tsze-lu hears something but has not yet succeeded in carrying it into practice. He fears that he will hear something else before he can act on what he already heard.

CHAP. XIV. Tsze-kung asked, saying, 'On what ground did

Tsze-kung asks the Master why Kung-wan received the title of Wan. The Master explains that Kung-wan was active and fond of learning. He also says Kung-wan was unashamed to ask and learn from his inferiors. For these reasons, Kung-wan was styled Wan.

CHAP. XV. The Master said of Tsze-ch'an that he had four of

The Master describes Tsze-ch'an as having four characteristics of a superior man. Tsze-ch'an was humble in his conduct and respectful when serving his superiors. He was kind in nourishing the people and just in ordering them.

CHAP. XVI. The Master said, 'Yen P'ing knew well how to

The Master says that Yen P'ing knew well how to maintain friendly intercourse. Although an acquaintance might be long, Yen P'ing showed the same respect as at first. The unit concerns Yen P'ing's consistent respect in maintaining friendly intercourse.

CHAP. XVII. The Master said, 'Tsang Wan kept a large tortoise

The Master discusses Tsang Wan, who kept a large tortoise in a house. Tsang Wan decorated the capitals of the pillars with hills and the small pillars above the beams with representations of duckweed. The Master questions what sort of wisdom this showed.

CHAP. XVIII. 1. Tsze-chang asked, saying, 'The minister Tsze-

Tsze-chang asks the Master about Tsze-wan, who repeatedly entered and left office without showing joy or displeasure and informed each new minister about his conduct of government. The Master calls Tsze-wan loyal but says he does not know whether Tsze-wan was perfectly virtuous. Tsze-chang also asks about Ch'an Wan, who abandoned his forty horses and left successive States after comparing their officials to Ch'ui; the Master calls him pure but does not call him perfectly virtuous.

CHAP. XIX. Chi Wan thought thrice, and then acted. When the

Chi Wan thinks three times before acting. The Master is informed of Chi Wan’s action. The Master says that twice may be sufficient.

CHAP. XX. The Master said, 'When good order prevailed in his

The Master says that when good order prevailed in his country, Ning Wu acted as a wise man. When his country was in disorder, Ning Wu acted as a stupid man. Others may equal Ning Wu's wisdom, but they cannot equal his stupidity.

CHAP. XXI. When the Master was in Ch'an, he said, 'Let me

The Master is in Ch'an and calls out, saying, “Let me return!” He describes the little children of his school as ambitious and too hasty. He says they are accomplished and complete so far but do not know how to restrict and shape themselves.

CHAP. XXII. The Master said, 'Po-i and Shu-ch'i did not keep

The Master discusses Po-i and Shu-ch'i. They did not keep the former wickednesses of men in mind. As a result, few resentments were directed towards them.

CHAP. XXIII. The Master said, 'Who says of Wei-shang Kao

The Master questions whether Wei-shang Kao is truly upright. He recounts that someone begged vinegar from Wei-shang Kao. Wei-shang Kao then begged vinegar from a neighbor and gave it to the man.

CHAP. XXIV. The Master said, 'Fine words, an insinuating

The Master says that fine words, an insinuating appearance, and excessive respect are shameful. He says Tso Ch'iu-ming was ashamed of them, and that he is also ashamed of them. He also says that concealing resentment against a person while appearing friendly is shameful conduct, which Tso Ch'iu-ming and he both reject.

CHAP. XXV. 1. Yen Yuan and Chi Lu being by his side, the

Yen Yuan and Chi Lu are with the Master when he asks each of them to state his wishes. Chi Lu wishes to share his chariots, horses, and light fur dresses with friends without displeasure if they spoil them, while Yen Yuan wishes not to boast of his excellence or display his meritorious deeds. The Master says his wishes are to give the aged rest, show sincerity to friends, and treat the young tenderly.

CHAP. XXVI. The Master said, 'It is all over! I have not yet

The Master declares that everything is over. He says he has not yet seen anyone who could perceive his own faults and inwardly accuse himself.

CHAP. XXVII. The Master said, 'In a hamlet of ten families,

The Master says that in a hamlet of ten families there may be one person as honourable and sincere as he is. He adds that this person may not be as fond of learning. The statement concerns the Master, the hamlet, the honourable and sincere person, and learning.
