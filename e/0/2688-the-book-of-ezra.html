The Book of Ezra

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

The Book of Ezra, in ten chapters, tells of the return of God's people from Babylonian captivity to Jerusalem, the rebuilding of the temple, and the later restoration of the community under Ezra. It opens in Cyrus's first year, when the LORD stirs Cyrus to proclaim that a house be built in Jerusalem. Heads of households from Judah and Benjamin, priests, and Levites go up, neighbors give gifts, and Cyrus returns the vessels Nebuchadnezzar had taken, counting them to Sheshbazzar, who brings them to Jerusalem. Chapter 2 lists the returnees under leaders including Zerubbabel and Jeshua, totaling 42,360, and notes priestly families disqualified for lack of genealogy. In chapter 3 Jeshua and Zerubbabel build the altar, keep the feast of booths, and lay the temple foundation, amid praise and the mingled weeping and shouting of old men who remembered the first house. Chapters 4 to 6 recount opposition and vindication. Adversaries whose offer of help was refused trouble the builders, and under Artaxerxes a letter from Rehum and Shimshai leads to a royal order stopping the work until Darius's second year. Haggai and Zechariah then prophesy, and building resumes. When Tattenai questions the builders and writes to Darius, a search finds Cyrus's decree at Achmetha. Darius reaffirms it, orders Tattenai to support the Jews, and the house is finished in his sixth year, dedicated with joy, and followed by the Passover. From chapter 7 the focus shifts to Ezra, a priest descended from Aaron and a skilled scribe in the law of Moses. In Artaxerxes' seventh year he travels from Babylon to Jerusalem with the king's letter granting silver, gold, supplies, tribute exemptions for temple personnel, and authority to appoint judges. Chapter 8 lists those who went with him, describes recruiting Levites and temple servants at Casiphia, and a fast at Ahava for a safe way. It then records the weighing of the treasure to twelve priestly chiefs, deliverance from enemies, and safe arrival in Jerusalem, where the treasure was delivered to Meremoth. In chapter 9 the princes report intermarriage with surrounding peoples, including by priests and Levites. Ezra tears his garment, mourns, and prays a confession, calling the LORD righteous. In chapter 10 the weeping assembly, prompted by Shecaniah, covenants to put away foreign wives and their children. A summons gathers the exiles in Jerusalem, and despite some opposition, cases are examined from the tenth month to the first month of the next year, and the book closes by listing those found to have married foreign women.

The Book of Ezra / Chapter 1

In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, the LORD stirred Cyrus's spirit to fulfill the word spoken by Jeremiah, and Cyrus issued a written proclamation throughout his kingdom. The proclamation said the LORD, the God of heaven, had given him all the kingdoms and commanded him to build a house in Jerusalem, and it invited any of God's people to go up and build it, with neighbors helping with silver, gold, goods, and animals. The heads of fathers' households of Judah and Benjamin, with the priests and Levites, whose spirits God had stirred, rose up to go and build, and those around them strengthened their hands with gifts. Cyrus also brought out the vessels of the LORD's house, which Nebuchadnezzar had carried off to the house of his gods, and counted them out through Mithredath the treasurer to Sheshbazzar the prince of Judah. The vessels totaled five thousand four hundred, and Sheshbazzar brought them up to Jerusalem when the captives were brought up from Babylon.

Chapter 2

Chapter 2 lists the children of the province who went up out of captivity in Babylon, where Nebuchadnezzar had carried them, and returned to Jerusalem and Judah, each to his own city. They came with leaders including Zerubbabel and Jeshua. The chapter records the numbers of the men of Israel by family and town, then the priests, Levites, singers, gatekeepers, temple servants, and Solomon's servants. Some priestly families who could not prove their genealogy were disqualified, and the governor barred them from the most holy things until a priest could serve with Urim and Thummim. The whole assembly numbered 42,360, besides servants, singers, and animals; some heads of households gave willingly to the work of God's house, and all settled in their cities.

Chapter 3

In the seventh month the people of Israel gathered at Jerusalem as one man, and Jeshua the son of Jozadak and Zerubbabel the son of Shealtiel, with the priests and their relatives, built the altar and offered burnt offerings on it despite fear of the surrounding peoples. They kept the feast of booths and the set offerings, though the temple foundation was not yet laid. They paid masons and carpenters and gave food, drink, and oil to the people of Sidon and Tyre to bring cedar from Lebanon to Joppa under Cyrus's grant. In the second month of the second year, Zerubbabel, Jeshua, the priests, the Levites, and the returned captives began the work and appointed Levites aged twenty and up to oversee it. When the foundation was laid, priests with trumpets and Levites with cymbals praised the LORD, and the people shouted, while the old men who had seen the first house wept so loudly that the weeping and joyful shouting could not be told apart.

Chapter 4

The adversaries of Judah and Benjamin offer to help build the temple, but Zerubbabel, Jeshua, and the heads of fathers' households refuse, citing King Cyrus's command. The people of the land then weaken and trouble the builders and hire counselors against them through the reigns of Cyrus and Darius. In the days of Artaxerxes, Rehum the chancellor, Shimshai the scribe, and their companions write a letter accusing Jerusalem of being a rebellious city that will stop paying tribute if rebuilt. Artaxerxes searches the records, confirms Jerusalem's history of revolt, and orders the building stopped. Rehum, Shimshai, and their companions enforce this by force of arms, and the work on God's house halts until the second year of Darius.

Chapter 5

Haggai and Zechariah prophesy to the Jews in Judah and Jerusalem, and Zerubbabel and Jeshua begin building God's house with the prophets' help. Tattenai, the governor beyond the River, and Shetharbozenai question the builders about who authorized the work and ask for their names, but they are not able to stop it because God's eye is on the elders. Tattenai and his companions write to King Darius, reporting that the work is prospering and relaying the elders' account. The elders say they serve the God of heaven and earth, that the house was destroyed by Nebuchadnezzar after the people provoked God, and that Cyrus decreed its rebuilding and sent the vessels with Sheshbazzar, who laid the foundations. The letter asks Darius to search the treasure house at Babylon for Cyrus's decree and to send his decision.

Chapter 6

King Darius orders a search of the archives in Babylon, and a scroll is found at Achmetha in Media recording Cyrus's decree in his first year that God's house at Jerusalem be rebuilt at royal expense, with its vessels restored. Darius reaffirms the decree, orders Tattenai, Shetharbozenai and their companions to stay away and to supply the Jews' needs, and pronounces penalties on anyone who alters it. Tattenai and his companions comply diligently, and the elders of the Jews build and finish the house through the prophesying of Haggai and Zechariah the son of Iddo, on the third day of Adar in Darius's sixth year. The children of Israel, priests and Levites dedicate the house with joy and many offerings, and set the priests and Levites in their divisions. They then keep the Passover and the feast of unleavened bread for seven days, rejoicing because the LORD had strengthened their hands.

Chapter 7

Chapter 7 introduces Ezra, a priest descended from Aaron through a listed genealogy and a skilled scribe in the law of Moses, who went up from Babylon to Jerusalem in the seventh year of King Artaxerxes of Persia. He left on the first day of the first month and arrived on the first day of the fifth month, because the good hand of his God was on him and he had set his heart to seek, do, and teach the LORD's law. The chapter reproduces Artaxerxes' letter, which permits willing Israelites, priests, and Levites to go with Ezra, sends silver and gold for offerings at the house of God in Jerusalem, and orders the treasurers beyond the River to supply Ezra's needs. The letter also exempts temple personnel from tribute, tells Ezra to appoint magistrates and judges, and prescribes penalties for those who do not keep the law of God and the law of the king. Ezra closes by blessing the LORD for putting this in the king's heart and describes gathering chief men of Israel to go up with him.

Chapter 8

Chapter 8 lists the heads of fathers' households and the males with them who went up from Babylon with the narrator in the reign of Artaxerxes. At the river Ahava the narrator found no sons of Levi among the people, so he sent chief men and teachers to Iddo at Casiphia, and they brought back Sherebiah, Hashabiah, their kin, and 220 temple servants. The narrator proclaimed a fast at Ahava to seek a straight way, since he was ashamed to ask the king for soldiers, and God granted their request. He set apart twelve chiefs of the priests, including Sherebiah and Hashabiah, and weighed to them the silver, gold, and vessels offered for God's house. After leaving Ahava on the twelfth day of the first month, the group was delivered from enemies and bandits, reached Jerusalem, and on the fourth day weighed the treasure into the hand of Meremoth, offered sacrifices, and delivered the king's commissions to the governors.

Chapter 9

The princes report to the narrator that the people of Israel, the priests, and the Levites have not separated from the peoples of the lands and have taken their daughters as wives for themselves and their sons, with the princes and rulers leading in this trespass. The narrator tears his garment and robe, pulls hair from his head and beard, and sits confounded until the evening offering while those who tremble at God's words gather to him. At the evening offering he falls on his knees, spreads his hands to the LORD, and prays a confession of shame over Israel's long guilt. He recalls the LORD's grace in leaving a remnant and giving favor before the kings of Persia, and cites the prophetic commandment against giving or taking daughters in marriage with the peoples of the land. He asks whether they should break the commandments again, and concludes that the LORD is righteous and that no one can stand before him in their guiltiness.

Chapter 10

Ezra prays and weeps before God's house, and a great assembly of Israelites joins in weeping. Shecaniah the son of Jehiel confesses that the people have married foreign women and proposes a covenant to put away these wives and their children according to the law. Ezra makes the chiefs of the priests, the Levites, and all Israel swear to do this, then mourns in the room of Jehohanan. A proclamation summons all the exiles to Jerusalem within three days on penalty of forfeiture, and on the twentieth day of the ninth month they gather in the rain, agree to Ezra's call to separate, and arrange for appointed times and local elders and judges to examine each case, despite opposition from Jonathan, Jahzeiah, Meshullam, and Shabbethai. The examination runs from the first day of the tenth month to the first day of the first month, and the chapter lists the priests, Levites, singers, gatekeepers, and other Israelites found to have taken foreign wives, some of whom had children by them.
