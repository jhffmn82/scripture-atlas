Leviticus

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

Leviticus is a book of laws the LORD gives to Moses for the children of Israel, concluding with the statement that these are the commandments given on Mount Sinai. Its central concern is how a people can live near a holy God: the LORD is holy, so Israel must be holy. The most important figures are the LORD, Moses as mediator, and Aaron and his sons, the priests. The book opens with the offering system: burnt, meal, peace, sin and trespass offerings (chapters 1-5), then the priests' procedures and portions (6-7). Moses consecrates Aaron and his sons with washing, anointing, blood and offerings (8). On the eighth day the LORD's glory appears and fire consumes the offering (9). Soon after, Nadab and Abihu offer strange fire and are devoured; the priests are told to avoid wine at the Tent of Meeting and to distinguish holy from common (10). The focus then moves to purity. Chapters 11-15 cover clean and unclean animals, childbirth, skin disease and mildew in garments and houses, and bodily discharges, with rites of cleansing. Chapter 16 sets out the Day of Atonement: Aaron enters the Most Holy Place, blood is sprinkled on the mercy seat, and a live goat carries Israel's sins into the wilderness. Chapter 17 requires sacrifices to be brought to the Tent of Meeting and forbids eating blood because life is in the blood. Chapters 18-20 address conduct: forbidden sexual relations, Molech worship and mediums, with penalties of death or being cut off, alongside the call to be holy, love one's neighbor and the stranger as oneself, and deal justly. Chapters 21-22 set stricter standards of holiness for priests and for handling holy things. Chapter 23 lists the appointed feasts: Sabbath, Passover, first fruits, the feast of weeks, trumpets, atonement and booths. Chapter 24 covers the lamp, the bread, and the stoning of a man who blasphemed the Name, with laws of equal retribution. Chapter 25 regulates the Sabbath year and the Jubilee, land redemption, and treatment of poor brothers. Chapter 26 gives the covenant of blessings for obedience and escalating curses for disobedience, ending with the promise to remember the covenant with Jacob, Isaac and Abraham if the people confess. Chapter 27 explains valuing and redeeming vows, dedicated things and tithes.

Leviticus / Chapter 1

The LORD calls to Moses from the Tent of Meeting and instructs him to tell the children of Israel how to offer offerings of livestock. Leviticus 1 gives procedures for the burnt offering in three forms: a male without defect from the herd, a male without defect from the flock (sheep or goats), and birds (turtledoves or young pigeons). The offerer brings the animal, lays a hand on its head for atonement, and kills it, while Aaron's sons, the priests, handle the blood and burn the offering on the altar. Each offering is described as an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Chapter 2

Chapter 2 gives instructions for presenting a meal offering to the LORD. The offering is made of fine flour with oil and frankincense, brought to Aaron's sons, the priests, who burn a handful as a memorial on the altar. The chapter covers forms baked in the oven, on a griddle, or in a pan, and the portion left over belongs to Aaron and his sons as a most holy part. Yeast and honey are forbidden in offerings burned for the LORD, salt of the covenant must always be included, and a first fruits offering uses fresh heads of grain parched with fire and crushed, with oil and frankincense.

Chapter 3

Leviticus 3 gives the rules for the sacrifice of peace offerings, which a person may bring from the herd, the flock (a lamb), or as a goat. For each animal the offerer must present it without defect before the LORD, lay his hand on its head, and kill it at the Tent of Meeting, while Aaron's sons, the priests, sprinkle the blood around on the altar. The fat covering the innards, the two kidneys with the fat on them, and the cover on the liver (plus the entire tail fat for a lamb) are taken away and burned on the altar as an offering made by fire, a pleasant aroma to the LORD. The chapter ends by declaring a perpetual statute for all generations and dwellings that the people shall eat neither fat nor blood.

Chapter 4

The LORD instructs Moses to tell the children of Israel how to handle unintentional sins against His commands. Different offenders bring different sin offerings: the anointed priest brings a young bull, the whole congregation brings a young bull with the elders laying hands on it, a ruler brings a male goat, and a common person brings a female goat or lamb. In each case the offerer lays a hand on the animal, it is killed before the LORD, and the priest applies its blood to the altar and burns the fat. The bull's remains for the priest and the congregation are burned outside the camp. The priest makes atonement, and the offender is forgiven.

Chapter 5

Chapter 5 lists situations in which a person incurs guilt: failing to testify after a public adjuration, touching unclean animal carcasses or human uncleanness unknowingly, and swearing rashly. The guilty person must confess and bring an offering to the LORD, with the priest making atonement. The offering scales to means: a female lamb or goat, then two turtledoves or young pigeons, then a tenth of an ephah of fine flour without oil or frankincense. The LORD then tells Moses that unwitting trespass against holy things requires a ram without defect, restitution, and an added fifth given to the priest. Unwitting violation of any of the LORD's commands still brings guilt and requires a ram as a trespass offering, after which the person is forgiven.

Chapter 6

In Leviticus 6, the LORD speaks to Moses about offenses against a neighbor, requiring the guilty person to restore what was taken plus a fifth and bring a ram as a trespass offering, through which the priest makes atonement. The LORD then instructs Moses to command Aaron and his sons on the law of the burnt offering, including the nightly burning on the altar, the priest's removal of ashes, and the perpetual altar fire. The chapter gives the law of the meal offering, of which Aaron's sons eat the remainder unleavened in a holy place, and the special meal offering for the anointed priest, which is wholly burned. Finally, it sets out the law of the sin offering, which is killed where the burnt offering is killed, eaten by the offering priest and the male priests in a holy place, with rules for handling blood-stained garments and cooking vessels, and with the exception that any sin offering whose blood is brought into the Tent of Meeting must be burned.

Chapter 7

Leviticus 7 gives the law of the trespass offering: it is most holy, is killed where the burnt offering is killed, has its blood sprinkled on the altar and its fat burned, and may be eaten by male priests in a holy place. It sets out which offerings belong to the priests, including the skin of the burnt offering and meal offerings, and gives rules for the peace offerings, including thanksgiving, vow and free will offerings, time limits on eating the meat, and the penalty of being cut off for eating it while unclean. The LORD speaks to Moses twice more, forbidding the children of Israel to eat fat of bull, sheep or goat and forbidding any blood, on penalty of being cut off. He also assigns the wave breast and heave right thigh of the peace offerings to Aaron and his sons as a portion forever. The chapter closes by summarizing the laws of the burnt, meal, sin, trespass, consecration and peace offerings, commanded at Mount Sinai.

Chapter 8

The LORD commands Moses to gather Aaron, his sons, the garments, anointing oil, a sin offering bull, two rams, and a basket of unleavened bread, and to assemble the congregation at the door of the Tent of Meeting. Moses washes and clothes Aaron and his sons, anoints the tabernacle, the altar, its vessels, and Aaron, and then offers the bull of the sin offering, the ram of the burnt offering, and the ram of consecration. Blood from the ram of consecration is put on the right ear, right thumb, and right great toe of Aaron and his sons, and portions are waved before the LORD and burned on the altar. Moses sprinkles anointing oil and altar blood on Aaron, his sons, and their garments, and instructs them to eat the meat and bread at the door, burn the remainder, and stay there seven days so they do not die. Aaron and his sons do everything the LORD commanded through Moses.

Chapter 9

On the eighth day Moses summons Aaron, his sons, and the elders of Israel and instructs them on the offerings to be made before the LORD, promising that the LORD will appear that day. Aaron offers a sin offering and burnt offering for himself, then the people's goat sin offering, burnt offering, meal offering, and peace offerings of a bull and a ram, with his sons delivering the blood and the fat being burned on the altar. Aaron waves the breasts and right thigh as a wave offering and blesses the people. Moses and Aaron enter the Tent of Meeting and come out to bless the people, and the LORD's glory appears to all. Fire comes out from before the LORD and consumes the burnt offering and fat on the altar, and the people shout and fall on their faces.

Chapter 10

Nadab and Abihu, sons of Aaron, offer strange fire before the LORD, which he had not commanded, and fire from before the LORD devours them. Moses tells Aaron that this fulfills the LORD's word about showing himself holy, and Aaron holds his peace. Moses has Mishael and Elzaphan carry the bodies out of the camp, and instructs Aaron, Eleazar, and Ithamar not to mourn openly or leave the Tent of Meeting. The LORD forbids Aaron and his sons wine and strong drink when entering the Tent of Meeting and commands them to distinguish holy from common and to teach the statutes. Moses directs the remaining sons to eat the meal offering and portions, then becomes angry that the sin offering goat was burned rather than eaten, until Aaron explains and Moses is satisfied.

Chapter 11

The LORD speaks to Moses and Aaron, instructing them to tell the children of Israel which living things may and may not be eaten. Land animals must part the hoof, be cloven-footed, and chew the cud; the camel, hyrax, hare, and pig are named as unclean. Water creatures must have fins and scales, and a list of birds and most winged insects are forbidden, though locusts, katydids, crickets, and grasshoppers may be eaten. Touching or carrying carcasses of unclean animals, including creeping things such as the weasel, rat, and lizards, brings uncleanness until evening and sometimes washing of clothes, and vessels contaminated by them must be washed or broken. The chapter closes with the call to be holy because the LORD is holy, and a statement that these laws distinguish the unclean from the clean.

Chapter 12

The LORD speaks to Moses, instructing him to tell the children of Israel the law for a woman who gives birth. After bearing a male child she is unclean seven days, the boy is circumcised on the eighth day, and she remains in the blood of purification thirty-three more days. After bearing a female child she is unclean two weeks and remains in the blood of purification sixty-six days. During this time she may not touch any holy thing or enter the sanctuary. When the days are completed she brings a year old lamb for a burnt offering and a young pigeon or turtledove for a sin offering to the priest at the door of the Tent of Meeting, who makes atonement so she is cleansed; if she cannot afford a lamb, she brings two turtledoves or two young pigeons instead.

Chapter 13

Chapter 13 records the LORD's instructions to Moses and Aaron on how skin diseases called leprosy are to be diagnosed. The priest examines swellings, scabs, bright spots, boils, burns, and plagues on the head or beard, looking at depth, white hair, and spread, and isolates the person for seven-day periods before pronouncing them clean or unclean. A person with leprosy must wear torn clothes, let their hair hang loose, cover the upper lip, cry 'Unclean!', and live alone outside the camp. The chapter then turns to mildew in garments of wool, linen, or leather, which the priest examines, isolates, and has washed, burning the item if the plague spreads or stays unchanged. It closes by calling this the law of the plague of mildew for pronouncing a garment clean or unclean.

Chapter 14

Leviticus 14 gives the LORD's instructions to Moses, and later to Moses and Aaron, on how a priest cleanses a person healed of leprosy. The ritual begins outside the camp with two living birds, cedar wood, scarlet, and hyssop, followed by washing, shaving, a seven-day wait, and on the eighth day trespass, sin, burnt, and meal offerings, with blood and oil applied to the ear, thumb, and big toe. A poorer person may offer one lamb plus turtledoves or young pigeons in place of the full set of animals. The chapter then covers mildew in a house in Canaan, which the priest inspects, shuts up for seven days, and then scrapes, repairs, or tears down if it spreads, and cleanses with a bird ritual if it is healed. It closes by summarizing this as the law for leprosy, itch, mildew in garments and houses, swelling, scab, and bright spot, to teach what is clean and unclean.

Chapter 15

Leviticus 15 gives the LORD's instructions, spoken to Moses and Aaron for the children of Israel, on ritual uncleanness from bodily discharges. A man with a discharge makes unclean the beds, seats, saddles, and vessels he uses, and people who touch him or these things must wash their clothes, bathe, and remain unclean until evening. After he is cleansed he counts seven days, bathes in running water, and on the eighth day brings two turtledoves or young pigeons to the priest for a sin offering and a burnt offering. Emission of semen requires bathing and uncleanness until evening, and a woman's menstrual period or prolonged blood discharge brings seven days of impurity that spreads to what she lies on or sits on and to those who touch her. The chapter ends by stating that these laws separate Israel from their uncleanness so they do not die by defiling the tabernacle.

Chapter 16

After the death of Aaron's two sons, the LORD tells Moses to warn Aaron not to enter the Most Holy Place at just any time, lest he die. Aaron is to enter only after bathing and putting on the holy linen garments, bringing a young bull for a sin offering and a ram for a burnt offering for himself, and taking two goats and a ram from the congregation of Israel. He kills the bull, brings incense and blood within the veil, and sprinkles blood on the mercy seat, then does the same with the blood of the people's sin offering goat, atoning for the Holy Place, the Tent of Meeting, and the altar. Aaron lays his hands on the live goat, confesses Israel's sins over it, and sends it into the wilderness by a man who is ready, with rules for washing and for burning the bull and goat outside the camp. The LORD establishes a permanent statute for the tenth day of the seventh month, a Sabbath of solemn rest and afflicting souls, when atonement is made once a year for all Israel's sins.

Chapter 17

The LORD instructs Moses to tell Aaron, his sons, and all the children of Israel that any animal slaughtered for sacrifice, whether in the camp or outside it, must be brought to the door of the Tent of Meeting, where the priest sprinkles the blood on the altar and burns the fat. This is meant to end sacrifices to the goat idols, and anyone who fails to comply is to be cut off from his people. The same requirement applies to strangers living among Israel. The LORD forbids eating blood, explaining that the life of the flesh is in the blood, which he gave on the altar to make atonement. Hunters must pour out the blood of game and cover it with dust, and anyone who eats what died of itself or was torn by animals must wash his clothes and bathe and be unclean until evening, or else bear his iniquity.

Chapter 18

The LORD speaks to Moses and instructs him to tell the children of Israel not to follow the practices of Egypt, where they lived, or Canaan, where they are being brought, but to keep the LORD's statutes and ordinances. The chapter lists forbidden sexual relations, including those with close relatives, a neighbor's wife, a woman during impurity, a man with a man, and animals, and it also forbids giving children to Molech. The LORD explains that the nations being cast out defiled themselves and the land with these acts, so the land vomited out its inhabitants. The Israelites, both native-born and strangers, are warned to avoid these abominations lest the land vomit them out too, and whoever does them will be cut off from their people.

Chapter 19

In Leviticus 19, the LORD tells Moses to speak to the whole congregation of the children of Israel and command them to be holy because the LORD their God is holy. The chapter gives a wide range of commandments: respecting parents, keeping Sabbaths, avoiding idols, rules for eating peace offerings, leaving field and vineyard gleanings for the poor and the foreigner, and prohibitions on stealing, lying, false oaths, oppression, slander, hatred, and vengeance. It commands loving one's neighbor as oneself and loving the stranger as oneself, since Israel lived as foreigners in Egypt. It also covers rules on mixed kinds, a trespass offering for a case involving a slave girl, fruit trees, blood, sorcery, mourning and bodily practices, mediums, honoring the elderly, and just weights and measures. It closes with a charge to observe all the LORD's statutes and ordinances.

Chapter 20

In Leviticus 20 the LORD instructs Moses to tell the children of Israel the penalties for serious offenses. He prescribes death by stoning for giving offspring to Molech, for cursing parents, and for being a medium or wizard, and he declares he will set his face against those who turn to mediums and wizards. He lists death or being cut off as penalties for adultery, for various forbidden sexual relations such as incest, same-sex intercourse, and bestiality, and for lying with a woman during her period. He calls the people to sanctify themselves and be holy, to keep his statutes so the land does not vomit them out, and not to follow the customs of the nation he is casting out. He reminds them he has separated them from the peoples and promised them a land flowing with milk and honey.

Chapter 21

In Leviticus 21 the LORD instructs Moses to speak to Aaron and the priests, the sons of Aaron, about the holiness required of them. Ordinary priests may not defile themselves for the dead except for near relatives, may not shave their heads or beards or cut their flesh, and may not marry a prostitute, a profane woman, or a divorced woman. A priest's daughter who plays the prostitute is to be burned with fire. The high priest, anointed with oil, is held to stricter rules: he may not go near any dead body, even for his father or mother, may not leave the sanctuary, and may marry only a virgin of his own people. Any descendant of Aaron with a defect, such as blindness, lameness, deformity, or damaged testicles, may not approach the altar or veil to offer the bread of God, though he may eat of the holy and most holy food; Moses then relays all this to Aaron, his sons, and all Israel.

Chapter 22

In Leviticus 22 the LORD instructs Moses to tell Aaron and his sons how to handle the holy things of the children of Israel without profaning his name. Priests who are unclean through leprosy, a discharge, or contact with the dead or other uncleanness may not eat the holy things until they are clean and have bathed. The chapter also specifies who in a priest's household may eat the holy things, and requires a fifth part added as restitution for unwitting eating. The LORD then sets rules for offerings: animals must be male and without defect, young animals stay seven days with their mothers, a mother and its young are not killed on the same day, and a thanksgiving sacrifice must be eaten the same day. The chapter closes with a call to keep the commandments, since the LORD sanctifies Israel and brought them out of Egypt.

Chapter 23

In Leviticus 23 the LORD instructs Moses to tell the children of Israel about his set feasts, which are to be proclaimed as holy convocations. The chapter begins with the weekly Sabbath, then lists the Passover and feast of unleavened bread in the first month, the first fruits sheaf offering, and the fifty-day count to a new meal offering with two loaves. It continues with the instruction to leave the field corners and gleanings for the poor and the foreigner, then the day of trumpets, the day of atonement, and the feast of booths in the seventh month. The chapter ends with a statement that Moses declared the appointed feasts to the children of Israel.

Chapter 24

The LORD instructs Moses to have the children of Israel bring pure olive oil so a lamp burns continually, with Aaron tending the lamps from evening to morning. He also commands that twelve cakes of fine flour be set in two rows of six on the pure gold table with frankincense, renewed every Sabbath, and eaten by Aaron and his sons in a holy place. The son of an Israelite woman, Shelomith of the tribe of Dan, and an Egyptian father blasphemes the Name and curses during a quarrel in the camp, and he is held in custody until the LORD's will is declared. The LORD orders that he be stoned by the congregation and sets out laws: death for blasphemy, death for killing a man, restitution for killing an animal, and equal retribution for injuries, with one law for foreigner and native-born. Moses relays this, and the children of Israel stone the man outside the camp as commanded.

Chapter 25

On Mount Sinai the LORD instructs Moses to tell the children of Israel the rules for the land they will enter. Every seventh year is a Sabbath of rest for the land, and after seven cycles of seven years the fiftieth year is a Jubilee, announced by trumpet on the Day of Atonement, in which liberty is proclaimed and people return to their property and family. The chapter regulates fair sales based on years of crops, forbids permanent sale of the land because it belongs to the LORD, and sets out redemption rights through kinsmen, with special rules for houses in walled cities and Levite cities. It also commands support of a poor brother without interest, forbids making him a slave, and provides for his redemption or release at Jubilee, while allowing slaves to be bought from surrounding nations.

Chapter 26

Chapter 26 closes the Sinai laws with a covenant of blessings and curses addressed by the LORD to the Israelites through Moses. After forbidding idols and requiring Sabbath-keeping, the LORD promises rain, abundant harvests, peace, victory over enemies, fruitfulness, and his own presence if the people obey his statutes and commandments. If they refuse, he threatens escalating punishments in stages of sevenfold intensity: terror, disease, drought, wild animals, sword, pestilence, famine, cannibalism, ruined cities, and scattering among the nations. The land will then enjoy its Sabbaths while desolate, and the survivors will waste away in fear in enemy lands. Yet if they confess their iniquity and humble their hearts, the LORD promises to remember his covenant with Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham and not utterly reject them.

Chapter 27

In Leviticus 27 the LORD instructs Moses to tell the children of Israel how to value and redeem things dedicated to Him. Persons consecrated by vow are valued in shekels of silver by age and sex, with the priest adjusting the value for the poor. Dedicated animals, houses, and fields are valued by the priest, and redeeming them requires adding a fifth, with fields adjusted according to the years remaining to the Year of Jubilee. Permanently devoted things cannot be sold or redeemed, firstborn animals already belong to the LORD, and the tithe of the land and of herds and flocks is holy to the LORD. The chapter closes by stating that these are the commandments given to Moses on Mount Sinai.
