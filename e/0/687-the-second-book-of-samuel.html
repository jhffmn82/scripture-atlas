The Second Book of Samuel

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

This is the Second Book of Samuel, a narrative of David's reign, following him from grief over Saul's death to the end of his rule. David is central; Joab, Abner, Absalom, Nathan, Michal, Mephibosheth, and Bathsheba matter most. After an Amalekite claims to have killed Saul, David mourns Saul and Jonathan, has the man executed, and composes a lament. David is anointed king of Judah at Hebron while Abner makes Ishbosheth king over the rest of Israel. War between the houses follows, with Asahel killed by Abner. Abner defects to David, Joab murders him, and David disowns the act. Ishbosheth is then killed by Baanah and Rechab, whom David punishes. All Israel anoints David, who takes Zion, defeats the Philistines, and brings the ark to Jerusalem after Uzzah's death; Michal despises his dancing. The LORD promises through Nathan an enduring house for David, whose victories widen and who shows kindness to Jonathan's son Mephibosheth. The narrative then turns: David sees Bathsheba, lies with her, and arranges the death of her husband Uriah. Nathan's parable confronts him, he confesses, their child dies, and Solomon is born. Family disaster follows: Amnon rapes Tamar, Absalom kills Amnon and flees, is brought back through Joab and the woman of Tekoa, then wins Israel's hearts and rebels. David flees Jerusalem; Ahithophel's counsel is defeated by Hushai, and Ahithophel hangs himself. Joab kills Absalom against David's command, David's grief nearly costs him the people, and he returns to power. Sheba's revolt is ended, with Joab killing Amasa. The closing chapters include a famine tied to Saul's killing of the Gibeonites and the hanging of seven of Saul's sons, wars with Philistine giants, David's song of deliverance, his last words, and a list of his mighty men. Finally David orders a census, confesses his sin, chooses pestilence that kills seventy thousand, and buys Araunah's threshing floor, builds an altar, and the plague stops.

The Second Book of Samuel / Chapter 1

After returning from slaughtering the Amalekites and staying two days in Ziklag, David receives a man from Saul's camp with torn clothes and earth on his head. The man, an Amalekite, reports that the people fled the battle and that Saul and Jonathan are dead, claiming he killed Saul at Saul's own request and brought David Saul's crown and bracelet. David and his men tear their clothes, mourn, weep, and fast for Saul, Jonathan, the people of the LORD, and the house of Israel. David then has the Amalekite struck down, saying his own mouth testified against him for slaying the LORD's anointed. David also laments Saul and Jonathan in a song, which he commands be taught to the children of Judah and which is written in the book of Jashar.

Chapter 2

After inquiring of the LORD, David moves with his two wives and his men to Hebron, where the men of Judah anoint him king over the house of Judah, and he sends a message of blessing to the men of Jabesh Gilead for burying Saul. Meanwhile Abner, captain of Saul's army, takes Ishbosheth to Mahanaim and makes him king over Gilead, Ephraim, Benjamin, and all Israel. Abner's and Joab's forces meet at the pool of Gibeon, where twelve men from each side kill one another, and a severe battle follows in which Abner's men are beaten. Asahel pursues Abner despite warnings and is killed by Abner's spear, after which Abner appeals to Joab to stop the fighting and Joab blows the trumpet. David's side loses nineteen men and Asahel, while 360 of Benjamin and Abner's men die, and Joab's men bury Asahel in Bethlehem and reach Hebron by daybreak.

Chapter 3

The war between Saul's house and David's house continues, with David growing stronger and six sons being born to him in Hebron. After Ishbosheth rebukes Abner over Saul's concubine Rizpah, Abner angrily vows to transfer the kingdom to David and sends messengers offering an alliance. David demands the return of his wife Michal, whom Ishbosheth takes from her husband Paltiel, and Abner rallies Israel's elders and Benjamin before feasting with David in Hebron and leaving in peace. Joab, returning from a raid, accuses Abner of deceit and, without David's knowledge, has him brought back and kills him in the gate to avenge his brother Asahel. David publicly disowns the killing, curses Joab's house, mourns and fasts for Abner, and laments that the sons of Zeruiah are too hard for him.

Chapter 4

After Abner dies in Hebron, Saul's son Ishbosheth and all Israel are discouraged. The text introduces Baanah and Rechab, Benjamite captains of raiding bands and sons of Rimmon the Beerothite, and notes Jonathan's lame son Mephibosheth. Rechab and Baanah enter Ishbosheth's house at midday, strike him on his bed, kill and behead him, and carry his head to David in Hebron, claiming the LORD has avenged the king on Saul. David rebukes them, recalling that he killed the man who brought news of Saul's death, and declares that wicked men who killed a righteous person in his bed must answer for his blood. David's young men kill the brothers, cut off their hands and feet, and hang them by the pool in Hebron, while Ishbosheth's head is buried in Abner's grave in Hebron.

Chapter 5

All the tribes of Israel come to David at Hebron, acknowledge him as their own flesh and bone, and anoint him king over Israel after making a covenant with him before the LORD. David, who began reigning at thirty and reigned forty years, takes the Jebusite stronghold of Zion and makes it David's city, growing greater because the LORD is with him. Hiram king of Tyre sends cedar, carpenters, and masons to build David a house, and David's sons born in Jerusalem are listed. When the Philistines go up to seek David, he inquires of the LORD twice: the first time he defeats them at Baal Perazim, and the second time he follows the LORD's instruction to circle behind them near the mulberry trees and strikes them from Geba to Gezer.

Chapter 6

David gathers thirty thousand chosen men of Israel and sets out to bring God's ark from Baale Judah on a new cart driven by Uzzah and Ahio, sons of Abinadab. When Uzzah reaches out to steady the ark at the threshing floor of Nacon, the LORD strikes him dead, and David, displeased and afraid, leaves the ark in the house of Obed-Edom the Gittite. After the LORD blesses Obed-Edom's household for three months, David brings the ark into David's city with sacrifices, shouting, trumpets, and his own dancing in a linen ephod. Michal, Saul's daughter, despises him in her heart and rebukes him for uncovering himself, but David answers that he acted before the LORD who chose him over her father's house. The chapter ends noting that Michal had no child to the day of her death.

Chapter 7

King David, settled in his house and at rest from his enemies, tells the prophet Nathan he is troubled that God's ark dwells in curtains while he lives in cedar. Nathan first approves, but that night the LORD tells Nathan to say that he has never asked for a house of cedar, and recalls taking David from the sheep pen to be prince over Israel. The LORD promises to give Israel a settled place and rest from enemies, to make David a house, and to set up David's offspring, who will build a house for his name and whose throne will be established forever. Even if this son sins he will be chastened, but the LORD's loving kindness will not leave him as it left Saul. David sits before the LORD and prays, praising God's greatness, recalling Israel's redemption from Egypt, and asking God to confirm and bless his house forever.

Chapter 8

Chapter 8 recounts King David's military victories over the Philistines, Moab, Hadadezer king of Zobah, the Syrians of Damascus, and Edom, with the LORD giving him victory wherever he went. David took horsemen, footmen, gold shields, and bronze from Hadadezer, and Toi king of Hamath sent his son Joram with silver, gold, and bronze vessels to bless him. David dedicated the silver and gold from all the subdued nations to the LORD. The chapter closes by noting that David reigned over all Israel with justice and righteousness, and lists his officials: Joab, Jehoshaphat, Zadok, Ahimelech, Seraiah, Benaiah, and David's sons.

Chapter 9

David asks whether anyone remains of Saul's house to whom he can show kindness for Jonathan's sake. Saul's servant Ziba tells him that Jonathan has a lame son, living in the house of Machir son of Ammiel in Lo Debar. David has the son, Mephibosheth, brought to him, assures him he need not fear, promises to restore all of Saul's land, and grants him a permanent place at the king's table. David then instructs Ziba, who has fifteen sons and twenty servants, to farm the land for Mephibosheth, and Ziba agrees. Mephibosheth, who has a young son named Mica, lives in Jerusalem and eats at the king's table like one of the king's sons, still lame in both feet.

Chapter 10

After the king of the Ammonites dies and his son Hanun takes the throne, David sends servants to comfort Hanun, but Ammonite princes persuade Hanun that they are spies. Hanun shames the servants by shaving half their beards and cutting their garments, and David tells them to wait at Jericho. The Ammonites hire Syrian and other mercenaries, and David sends Joab, who divides the army with his brother Abishai, defeats the Syrians, and sees the Ammonites flee into their city. Hadadezer then brings more Syrians to Helam, where David himself crosses the Jordan, kills many charioteers and horsemen, and strikes down Shobach. The kings serving Hadadezer make peace with Israel, and the Syrians no longer dare help the Ammonites.

Chapter 11

While Joab and Israel's army fight the Ammonites and besiege Rabbah, David stays in Jerusalem, sees Bathsheba bathing, summons her, and lies with her. When she tells him she is pregnant, David calls her husband Uriah the Hittite home from the war, hoping he will go to his house, but Uriah refuses to enjoy comfort while the ark and the army are in tents. David then sends a letter to Joab, carried by Uriah himself, ordering that Uriah be placed in the hottest fighting and abandoned so he will die. Joab does so, Uriah dies, and David responds calmly to the news, then marries Bathsheba after her mourning, and she bears him a son. The chapter ends by stating that what David did displeased the LORD.

Chapter 12

The LORD sends Nathan to David, and Nathan tells a parable of a rich man who took a poor man's only ewe lamb to feed a traveler. David burns with anger and declares the man deserves to die, and Nathan answers, "You are the man," pronouncing judgment for David's killing of Uriah the Hittite and taking of his wife. David confesses his sin, is told he will not die, but the child born to him will, and after fasting for seven days he sees the child die, then rises, worships, and eats. David comforts Bathsheba, and she bears Solomon, whom the LORD loves and Nathan names Jedidiah. Meanwhile Joab takes Rabbah's royal city and summons David, who captures the city, takes the Ammonite king's crown, and puts the people to forced labor.

Chapter 13

Amnon, son of David, loves his half-sister Tamar, sister of Absalom, and on the advice of his friend Jonadab feigns illness so that David sends Tamar to prepare food for him. Amnon rapes Tamar despite her protests, then hates her and has her thrown out; she mourns publicly and stays desolate in Absalom's house. David is very angry but the text records no action, while Absalom says nothing to Amnon for two years. Absalom then invites the king's sons to a sheep shearing and has his servants kill Amnon when he is merry with wine. Absalom flees to Talmai, king of Geshur, where he stays three years, while Jonadab calms David's fear that all his sons are dead and David mourns Amnon and eventually longs to go out to Absalom.

Chapter 14

Joab, seeing that the king's heart is toward Absalom, sends for a wise woman of Tekoa and instructs her to pose as a long-grieving widow and tell the king a made-up story. She says one of her two sons killed the other in a field and that her family demands the killer's death, which would leave her husband without name or heir. The king promises that not a hair of her son will fall, and she then turns this against him, saying he is guilty for not bringing home his own banished one. The king discerns Joab's hand in it, and she admits Joab put the words in her mouth. The king tells Joab to bring Absalom back from Geshur, but Absalom must live in his own house and not see the king's face. After two years Absalom burns Joab's barley field to force a meeting with Joab, and Joab then takes Absalom's plea to the king, who receives Absalom and kisses him.

Chapter 15

Absalom prepares a chariot, horses, and fifty runners, then sits at the city gate intercepting Israelites with lawsuits, promising justice if he were judge and kissing those who bow to him, thereby stealing the hearts of the men of Israel. After forty years he asks King David's permission to go to Hebron to pay a vow, and David sends him in peace. Absalom sends spies through the tribes to proclaim him king at the trumpet sound, takes two hundred unwitting invitees, and summons David's counselor Ahithophel, so the conspiracy grows strong. Warned that Israel's hearts follow Absalom, David flees Jerusalem with his household and servants, leaving ten concubines behind, and Ittai the Gittite pledges loyalty to him. David sends Zadok and the ark back to the city, weeps on the Mount of Olives, prays that Ahithophel's counsel become foolishness, and sends Hushai the Archite back to Absalom to defeat that counsel and report through the priests' sons, as Absalom enters Jerusalem.

Chapter 16

As David flees past the top of the hill, Ziba, servant of Mephibosheth, brings him provisions and claims that Mephibosheth is staying in Jerusalem hoping to regain his father's kingdom; David then gives Ziba all that belonged to Mephibosheth. At Bahurim, Shimei son of Gera, of Saul's house, curses David and throws stones, and David stops Abishai from killing him, saying the LORD may have bidden him to curse. In Jerusalem, Hushai the Archite, David's friend, declares loyalty to Absalom, who questions him. Ahithophel advises Absalom to go in to his father's concubines in sight of all Israel, and Absalom does so on a tent atop the house. The text notes that Ahithophel's counsel was regarded as if inquired of God.

Chapter 17

Ahithophel advises Absalom to let him pursue David that night with twelve thousand men and strike only the king, but Absalom and the elders of Israel prefer the counsel of Hushai the Archite, who urges gathering all Israel and Absalom leading the battle in person. The LORD had ordained that Ahithophel's good counsel be defeated. Hushai tells Zadok and Abiathar to warn David, and Jonathan and Ahimaaz carry the message, hiding in a well at Bahurim with the help of a woman who misleads Absalom's servants. David and his people cross the Jordan before morning, and Ahithophel, seeing his counsel rejected, goes home and hangs himself. David reaches Mahanaim, Absalom crosses the Jordan and appoints Amasa over the army instead of Joab, and Shobi, Machir and Barzillai bring food and supplies to David's hungry people.

Chapter 18

David organizes his followers into captains and into three divisions under Joab, Abishai, and Ittai, but the people persuade him to stay behind at the city gate, and he commands the captains to deal gently with Absalom. In the battle in the forest of Ephraim, Israel loses twenty thousand men, and the forest devours more than the sword. Absalom's head is caught in an oak and his mule runs on, and Joab, ignoring the king's command and the refusal of a man who saw it, thrusts three darts through Absalom's heart, and Joab's armor-bearers finish him. Joab calls off the pursuit, Absalom is cast into a pit under a heap of stones, and Ahimaaz and the Cushite race to bring news to David. When David learns Absalom is dead, he goes up to the room over the gate and weeps, wishing he had died instead.

Chapter 19

Joab learns that King David is weeping for Absalom, and the people's victory turns to mourning; Joab rebukes David for shaming those who saved him and warns that everyone will desert him unless he comforts them. David sits in the gate, then sends Zadok and Abiathar to urge the elders of Judah to bring him back, and offers Amasa the captaincy of the army in place of Joab. David crosses the Jordan, where he pardons Shimei over Abishai's objection, hears Mephibosheth's explanation for not following him and tells him to divide the land with Ziba, and blesses Barzillai, who declines to come to Jerusalem and sends Chimham instead. The chapter ends with the men of Israel and the men of Judah quarreling over who has the greater claim to the king, with Judah's words proving fiercer.

Chapter 20

Sheba the son of Bichri, a Benjamite, blows a trumpet and rallies the men of Israel away from David, while Judah stays loyal. David returns to Jerusalem, shuts up his ten concubines, and orders Amasa to gather Judah's men; when Amasa is late, David sends Abishai and Joab's men after Sheba. Joab kills Amasa with a sword at Gibeon, and the people follow Joab in pursuit of Sheba. At Abel of Beth Maacah, Joab besieges the city until a wise woman negotiates with him, and the city's people cut off Sheba's head and throw it over the wall. Joab blows the trumpet, the army disperses, and Joab returns to Jerusalem; the chapter ends with a list of David's officials.

Chapter 21

A three-year famine leads David to seek the LORD, who says it is because Saul put the Gibeonites to death. David asks the Gibeonites what atonement they want, and they ask for seven of Saul's sons to be hanged; David spares Mephibosheth son of Jonathan because of his oath, and hands over Rizpah's two sons and Merab's five sons. Rizpah guards the bodies from birds and animals until rain falls, and David, hearing of it, retrieves the bones of Saul and Jonathan and buries them with the bones of those hanged in Kish's tomb, after which God answers prayer for the land. The chapter then records renewed wars with the Philistines, in which David grows faint and is rescued by Abishai, and four descendants of the giant are killed by David and his servants.

Chapter 22

Chapter 22 presents a song David spoke to the LORD on the day the LORD delivered him from all his enemies and from Saul. David praises the LORD as his rock, fortress, shield and savior, and recounts calling on him in distress amid the cords of Sheol and snares of death. He describes the LORD's stormy descent, with quaking earth, fire, thunder, darkness and lightning, to draw him out of many waters. David claims the LORD rewarded his righteousness and clean hands, and credits the LORD with arming him for battle so that he destroyed his enemies, who cried out without being answered. The song ends with David vowing to give thanks among the nations and praising the LORD for deliverance to his king, David, and his offspring forever.

Chapter 23

Chapter 23 opens with David's last words, in which he says the LORD's Spirit spoke through him and describes a righteous ruler as morning light, while the ungodly are like thorns burned with fire. It then lists the mighty men of David, beginning with Josheb Basshebeth, Eleazar, and Shammah the son of Agee, who won victories against the Philistines with the LORD's help. Three of the thirty broke through the Philistine camp to bring David water from the well of Bethlehem, and David poured it out to the LORD instead of drinking it. Abishai and Benaiah the son of Jehoiada are praised for their deeds but are not counted among the three. The chapter ends with a list of the thirty, closing with Uriah the Hittite, thirty-seven in all.

Chapter 24

The LORD, angry with Israel, moves David to order a census, and David commands Joab and the army captains to count the people from Dan to Beersheba despite Joab's objection. After nine months and twenty days Joab reports 800,000 swordsmen in Israel and 500,000 in Judah, and David's heart strikes him, so he confesses his sin. Through the prophet Gad the LORD offers David a choice of seven years of famine, three months of flight from foes, or three days of pestilence, and David chooses to fall into the LORD's hand. The pestilence kills seventy thousand before the LORD's angel, at the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite, is told to stop. At Gad's direction David buys the threshing floor from Araunah for fifty shekels of silver, builds an altar, and offers sacrifices, and the plague is removed from Israel.
