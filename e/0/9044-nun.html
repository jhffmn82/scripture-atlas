nun

Also called: A nun, a nun

Abstract written by a model

A nun is a female mendicant and ascetic whose life is governed by disciplined begging, travel, food, conduct, contemplation, speech, and restraint from sensory attachment. She undertakes and uses begging-tours, enters a householder’s abode for alms only under proper conditions, and accepts food that is pure, acceptable, and respectfully given. She avoids food affected by living beings or impurities, refuses âdhâkarmika food, does not select food by smell, consumes what she receives, and consults fellow-ascetics before rejecting excess. She must avoid seeking food or drink in houses of nearer or remoter relations and observe restrictions concerning houses, householders, and food prepared for others. She does not travel with a householder and moves carefully between villages or across water, especially during appropriate seasons and conditions, so as not to harm living beings or accept unsuitable help. When threatened or disturbed, she collects herself for contemplation and stays apart where people do not pass or see her. Her speech must be deliberate, precise, moderate, restrained, and free from wrath, pride, deception, gain, roughness, divisiveness, or harm; non-truth-or-untruth speech is permitted only when not sinful or blamable. Finally, she must not go to walls or ditches to hear sounds, seek entertainments improperly, or delight in sounds of either world.

Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra), Book II, translated by Hermann Jacobi / Second Book / First Part. / First Lecture, Call

A nun should follow the rules for rejecting food affected by living beings or other impurities and may accept food recognised as pure and acceptable. She should also observe the prescribed restrictions when entering houses, travelling, and dealing with food prepared for others.

Fourth Lesson.

A nun should not attend certain entertainments or enter a householder’s abode when the specified conditions make doing so improper. She may attend or enter when the conditions are suitable, and may accept acceptable alms at the proper time.

Sixth Lesson.

A nun on a begging-tour is subject to the same rules as a monk. She should avoid improper conduct around the householder’s abode and should accept only food and salt considered pure and acceptable under the stated conditions.

Ninth Lesson.

A nun should not enter or leave the houses of her relations for food or drink, and should not address householders quickly when meat, fish, oil cakes, or food for a sick person is being prepared. She should consume all received food and drink without selecting parts and consult fellow-ascetics before rejecting excess.

Third Lecture, Called Walking. / First Lesson. .. Third Lesson.

She should remain during the rainy season in a suitable village or scot-free town and should travel only when roads and conditions are appropriate. While wandering from village to village or crossing water, she should proceed circumspectly, avoid harming living beings, refuse unsuitable assistance or tasks, and collect herself for contemplation when threatened or disturbed.

Fourth Lecture, Called Modes of Speech. / First Lesson. .. Second Lesson.

A nun should avoid speech spoken in wrath, pride, deception, or gain, as well as sinful, blamable, rough, stinging, coarse, divisive, or harmful speech. She should speak precisely, with moderation and restraint, using accurate or non-truth-or-untruth speech when it is not sinful.

Eleventh Lecture.

A nun should not resolve to go to places to hear drums, stringed instruments, kettledrums, wind instruments, or other sounds. She should not seek or love sounds, or become enraptured with sounds of this or the other world, whether heard or unheard, seen or unseen.
