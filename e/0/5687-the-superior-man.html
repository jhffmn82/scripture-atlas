the superior man

person · 1 work · 70 quoted facts · 10 connections
Also called: Such a one, The superior man, he, he is, he is sure, himself, his, superior man, superior man's

Abstract written by a model

The superior man is a person defined by steadfast self-cultivation and faithful adherence to the Mean. He is cautious, apprehensive, and watchful over himself even when alone; he maintains friendly harmony without weakness, stands firmly in the middle, and remains unchanged whether good or bad principles prevail. Unknown or unregarded, he has no regret. He acts appropriately in every station, wealth, poverty, foreign tribes, sorrow, or difficulty, without despising inferiors, courting superiors, murmuring against Heaven, or grumbling against men. Instead, he rectifies himself, seeks the cause of failure in himself, and waits calmly for the appointments of Heaven. His growth proceeds through unceasing study, inquiry, reflection, discrimination, and practice, enabling intelligence and strength despite dullness or weakness. He values sincerity most highly, honors virtuous nature, preserves old knowledge while acquiring new knowledge, and practices propriety earnestly. In public he is humble when high and not insubordinate when low; when conditions are bad, his restraint appears through silence. Finally, he conceals virtue even as it becomes more illustrious, examines his heart, remains reverent and truthful, and influences others without rewards or anger through sincerity and reverence.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

The Doctrine of the Mean / Chapter 1

The superior man does not wait until he sees things to be cautious or until he hears things to be apprehensive. He is watchful over himself when he is alone.

Chapter 2

The superior man embodies the course of the Mean and always maintains the Mean because he is a superior man.

Chapter 10

The superior man cultivates friendly harmony without weakness and stands firmly in the middle without inclining to either side. He does not change from what he was in retirement when good principles prevail, and he maintains his course to death when bad principles prevail.

Chapter 11

The superior man accords with the course of the Mean. Even when all unknown and unregarded by the world, he feels no regret.

Chapter 12

He pursues a way that extends broadly and deeply. If he described it in its greatness, nothing could embrace it; if he described it in its minuteness, nothing could split it.

Chapter 13

The superior man governs people according to their nature and stops correcting them when they change what is wrong. He practices reciprocity, strives to serve others as he would have them serve him, and ensures that his words correspond to his actions.

Chapter 14

The superior man does what is proper to whatever station he occupies, remaining himself in wealth, poverty, among barbarous tribes, and in sorrow or difficulty. He does not despise inferiors or court superiors, but rectifies himself and seeks nothing from others. He waits quietly for the appointments of Heaven, and when he misses the center of the target, he seeks the cause of his failure in himself.

Chapter 15

The superior man is associated with a way that begins with what is near or lower before reaching what is distant or high.

Chapter 20

The superior man does not stop laboring while anything remains unstudied, unknown, unapprehended, unclear, or unpracticed. Through persistent effort, he becomes intelligent despite dullness and strong despite weakness.

Chapter 25

The superior man regards the attainment of sincerity as the most excellent thing.

Chapter 27

The superior man honors his virtuous nature, maintains constant inquiry and study, preserves old knowledge, acquires new knowledge, and practices propriety with honest and generous earnestness. He pursues the course of the Mean, remains humble in high position, and is not insubordinate in low position.

Chapter 33

The superior man conceals his virtue while it becomes more illustrious, examines his heart, and ensures that he has no cause for dissatisfaction with himself. He remains reverent when still and truthful when silent, does not rely on rewards or anger, and influences the people toward virtue and tranquility.
