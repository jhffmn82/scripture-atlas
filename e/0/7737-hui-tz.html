Hui Tzŭ

person · 1 work · 31 quoted facts · 8 connections
Also called: Hui Tzŭ daily, Hui Tzŭ makes, Hui Tzŭ may, Hui Tzŭ was a man

Abstract written by a model

Hui Tzŭ is a schoolman, arguer, debater, dialectician, philosopher, sophist, and prime minister in the Liang State, contemporary with and antagonistic to Chuang Tzŭ. He is presented as subtle, ingenious, and prolific: he has many ideas, works filling five carts, doctrines of his own, and a reputation as a great philosopher and favourite of dialecticians. Yet his views differ from the world’s, particularly in treating hardness and whiteness as separate existences, and he is said to end in the obscurity of the hard and white rather than in TAO. He criticizes a huge gourd and a worthless-looking tree as useless, prompting Chuang Tzŭ to argue that unusual things may be valuable when properly used; Hui Tzŭ later calls Chuang Tzŭ’s theme useless. As prime minister, he fears Chuang Tzŭ seeks his office, searches for him, and debates him about the pleasure of fishes. He debates Confucianists, Mihists, and followers of Yang and Ping about standards of right, while Chuang Tzŭ challenges his restricted search for TAO. Skilled at winning debates but not hearts, he advances preposterous theses, claims no peer in the universe, and investigates creation without reaching TAO. The account judges him morally weak, physically violent, without a sound system, and ultimately a specialist and sophist; after his death, Chuang Tzŭ says conversation has lost its material.

Hui Tzŭ is in one work on this site, Chuang Tzŭ. This is the abstract a model wrote of it from that work.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER I.

Hui Tzŭ criticizes the large-sized gourd and a large tree of a worthless kind because they seem unusable. Chuang Tzŭ answers him with examples showing that unusual things may have value when properly applied.

CHAPTER II.

Hui Tzŭ is associated with the alternation theory of subjective and objective and with arguing about distinctions. His particular views differed from those of the world, and he ended in the obscurity of the hard and white.

The walrus envies the centipede;

Hui Tzŭ is prime minister in the Liang State and fears that Chuang Tzŭ wants his position, so he searches the state for three days and three nights. Chuang Tzŭ later tells him that his suspicion resembles an owl screeching at a passing phœnix, and Hui Tzŭ debates him over whether he can know the pleasure of fishes.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Hui Tzŭ debates the Confucianists, Mihists, and followers of Yang and Ping over whether any general standard of right exists. Chuang Tzŭ challenges his restricted search for TAO and later says he has lost the material needed for conversation because Hui Tzŭ has died.

CHAPTER XXVI.

Hui Tzŭ challenges Chuang Tzŭ’s theme as useless. He agrees that the earth has no further use to a person once only the space beneath the soles of the feet is needed.

CHAPTER XXVII.

Hui Tzŭ said that Confucius was a persevering worker whose wisdom increased day by day. He is described as skilled in winning debates but unskilled in winning hearts.

Hui Tzŭ was a man of many ideas. His works would fill five carts. But

Hui Tzŭ was a man of many ideas whose works would fill five carts. He advanced preposterous theses, argued endlessly, claimed that the universe held no peer, and investigated all creation without concluding in TAO. The text portrays him as morally weak, physically violent, extravagantly energetic, and ultimately a sophist lacking a sound system.
