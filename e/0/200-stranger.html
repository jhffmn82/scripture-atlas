Abstract written by a model

In Leviticus, strangers living among the children of Israel follow the same rules as Israelites regarding offerings, blood, carcasses, cleansing, and uncleanness. In Leviticus, the non-priestly outsider living as a foreigner must not be wronged, but treated as native-born and loved as oneself, with field and vineyard gleanings left for him. In Numbers, the stranger living as a foreigner among Israel shares the same standing before the Lord, offers fire offerings like natives, is forgiven with the congregation for unwitting sin, but is cut off for high-handed sin. In Deuteronomy, the foreigner is protected from being deprived of justice, paid promptly as a poor hired servant, and given forgotten sheaves, olive boughs, and vineyard gleanings. In The Second Book of Chronicles, foreigners residing in Israel are counted by Solomon at 153,600 and assigned to labor as burden bearers, stone cutters, and overseers. In The Book of Ezekiel, aliens living among Israel and raising children there are regarded as native-born, sharing in the inheritance of the tribe where they live.

Abstract written by a model

Foreigners living among the children of Israel are bound by the same rules as Israelites: bringing offerings to the Tent of Meeting and avoiding blood, with cleansing and uncleanness until evening required if they eat carcasses.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

Chapter 17

The strangers who live as foreigners among Israel are bound by the same rules: bringing burnt offerings and sacrifices to the Tent of Meeting and not eating blood. They too are to be cut off for eating blood, and a foreigner who eats what died of itself or was torn must wash, bathe, and be unclean until evening.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

The stranger who lives as a foreigner among Israel is to offer offerings by fire just as the native-born do, under one statute, one law, and one ordinance forever.
