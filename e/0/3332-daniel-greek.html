Daniel (Greek)

Abstract written by a model

This is the Book of Daniel in its longer form with the Greek portions, in fourteen chapters. It follows Daniel, a Jewish youth taken into Babylonian service, and his companions Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah, renamed Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. The central theme is that the God of Israel rules over kings and kingdoms, and that faithful servants are vindicated. Chapters 1 to 6 are court tales. After Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem, the four youths are trained in Babylon. Daniel refuses the king's food, and God gives them wisdom. Daniel then recovers and interprets Nebuchadnezzar's forgotten dream of a great image, and is promoted. Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refuse to worship a golden image and are thrown into a furnace. An angel protects them, Azarias prays, the three sing a hymn, and the king honors their God. Nebuchadnezzar's dream of a tree leads to his seven-times madness and restoration after he acknowledges the Most High. At Belshazzar's feast, using the temple vessels, a hand writes on the wall. Daniel reads it as the end of Belshazzar's kingdom, and Belshazzar is slain, with Darius the Mede receiving the kingdom. Under Darius, jealous officials trap Daniel with a decree against petition, and he is cast into the lions' den. An angel shuts the lions' mouths, and his accusers are destroyed instead. Chapters 7 to 12 are visions. Daniel sees four beasts, the Ancient of Days, and one like a son of man given everlasting dominion, with the saints of the Most High receiving the kingdom. A ram and goat vision, explained by Gabriel, concerns Media, Persia, Greece, and a fierce-faced king who desecrates the sanctuary. Daniel prays a confession for Jerusalem, and Gabriel announces seventy weeks. A radiant man in linen strengthens Daniel, speaks of conflict with the princes of Persia and Greece, and names Michael as Daniel's prince. Chapter 11 details wars between the kings of the south and north and a self-exalting king. Chapter 12 promises Michael's arising, deliverance for those written in the book, resurrection to life or shame, sealed words, and day counts of 1,290 and 1,335, with Daniel told he will stand in his inheritance at the end of the days. Chapters 13 and 14 add two stories. In Susanna, two lustful elders falsely accuse her, and Daniel exposes them by questioning them separately about the tree. In Bel and the Dragon, under Cyrus, Daniel shows through ashes that the priests, not the idol Bel, eat the offerings, destroys Bel and the dragon, and survives a second lions' den, fed by Habakkuk, before his accusers are devoured.

The Book of Daniel with Greek Portions / Chapter 1

In the third year of Jehoiakim's reign, Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon besieged Jerusalem, and the Lord gave Jehoiakim into his hand along with part of the vessels of God's house, which were carried to Shinar. The king told Ashpenaz, master of his eunuchs, to bring in flawless, skillful Israelite youths of royal and noble descent to learn the Chaldeans' learning and language and to be fed from the king's delicacies and wine for three years. Among them were Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah, who were renamed Belteshazzar, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. Daniel refused to defile himself with the king's food, and after God gave him favor with the prince of the eunuchs, the steward agreed to a ten-day test of vegetables and water. The four looked fairer and fatter than the others, so the steward kept them on vegetables, God gave them knowledge and wisdom, and Nebuchadnezzar found them ten times better than all his magicians and enchanters.

Chapter 2

In the second year of his reign, King Nebuchadnezzar is troubled by a dream he has forgotten and demands that the Chaldeans, magicians, and enchanters tell him both the dream and its interpretation. When they say no man can do this, he angrily orders all the wise men of Babylon destroyed, and Arioch is sent to carry out the decree, which includes Daniel and his companions. Daniel asks the king for time, has Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah pray for mercy, and receives the secret in a night vision, after which he blesses the God of heaven. Daniel describes the great image of gold, silver, bronze, iron, and iron mixed with clay that is struck by a stone, and interprets it as a succession of kingdoms ending with a kingdom God will set up forever. Nebuchadnezzar falls on his face, acknowledges Daniel's God, and promotes Daniel to rule over the province of Babylon, while Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego are put over the province's affairs.

Chapter 3

King Nebuchadnezzar sets up a golden image on the plain of Dura, gathers the officials of the provinces to its dedication, and has a herald command all peoples to worship it at the sound of music on pain of the fiery furnace. Certain Chaldeans accuse the Jews Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego of refusing, and the enraged king confronts them; they declare that their God is able to deliver them, but that even if not, they will not worship the image. The furnace is heated seven times hotter, the three are bound and thrown in, and the flames kill the men who carry them. Inside the fire, Azarias prays a confession of Israel's sins and asks for deliverance, the angel of the Lord comes down and makes the furnace like a moist whistling wind, and the three sing a long hymn of blessing. Nebuchadnezzar sees four men walking unharmed, calls the three out, blesses their God, decrees punishment for anyone who speaks evil against him, and promotes the three in Babylon.

Chapter 4

King Nebuchadnezzar addresses all peoples, declaring the signs the Most High God has worked for him. He recounts a troubling dream of a great tree that is cut down to a stump bound with iron and bronze, which his wise men cannot interpret but Daniel, called Belteshazzar, can. Daniel explains that the tree is Nebuchadnezzar himself, who will be driven from men to live with the animals for seven times until he knows the Most High rules, and he urges the king to break off his sins by righteousness and mercy to the poor. Twelve months later, as Nebuchadnezzar boasts of the Babylon he built, a voice from the sky pronounces the sentence, and it is fulfilled that hour. At the end of the days his understanding returns, he blesses the Most High, is restored to his kingdom, and praises the King of heaven.

Chapter 5

King Belshazzar holds a great feast for a thousand lords and has the golden and silver vessels taken from the Jerusalem temple brought out so he, his lords, wives, and concubines can drink from them while praising gods of gold, silver, bronze, iron, wood, and stone. A hand appears and writes on the palace wall, terrifying the king, and the wise men of Babylon cannot read or interpret it. The queen recalls Daniel, whom Nebuchadnezzar had made master of the magicians, and Daniel is brought in and refuses the offered rewards. Daniel rebukes Belshazzar for not humbling himself as Nebuchadnezzar eventually did before the Most High God, then reads the writing as meaning that God has numbered and ended Belshazzar's kingdom, found him wanting, and given the kingdom to the Medes and Persians. Belshazzar has Daniel honored as promised, but that night Belshazzar is slain and Darius the Mede receives the kingdom.

Chapter 6

King Darius appoints one hundred twenty local governors and three presidents, one of whom is Daniel, and Daniel is distinguished above them all. Jealous, the presidents and local governors can find no fault in him, so they persuade Darius to sign a decree forbidding petitions to any god or man except the king for thirty days, on pain of the den of lions. Daniel keeps praying to his God three times a day, is reported by the men, and Darius, though he labors to rescue him, is bound by the unalterable law and has Daniel cast into the den. After a sleepless night Darius finds Daniel unharmed because God sent an angel to shut the lions' mouths; the accusers and their families are thrown to the lions and killed, and Darius decrees that all in his kingdom must fear the God of Daniel, while Daniel prospers under Darius and Cyrus.

Chapter 7

In the first year of Belshazzar king of Babylon, Daniel records a dream in which four great animals rise from the sea: a lion with eagle's wings, a bear, a leopard with four wings and four heads, and a terrible fourth animal with iron teeth and ten horns. A little horn with eyes and a boastful mouth uproots three of the horns, until the Ancient of Days takes his throne for judgment, the fourth animal is slain, and one like a son of man is given everlasting dominion. An attendant explains to Daniel that the animals are four kings and that the saints of the Most High will possess the kingdom forever. The fourth kingdom and its final king will speak against the Most High and wear out the saints for a time, times and half a time, before his dominion is taken away. Daniel is greatly troubled, his face changes, and he keeps the matter in his heart.

Chapter 8

In the third year of King Belshazzar, Daniel sees a vision at the citadel of Susa by the river Ulai: a two-horned ram is attacked and trampled by a male goat with a notable horn, whose great horn breaks and is replaced by four horns, one of which produces a little horn that grows great, casts down the army and stars, takes away the continual burnt offering, and throws down the sanctuary. A holy one says the desolation will last 2,300 evenings and mornings before the sanctuary is cleansed. Gabriel is commanded by a man's voice to make Daniel understand, and he explains that the ram is the kings of Media and Persia, the goat is the king of Greece, and the great horn is its first king. Gabriel describes a fierce-faced king who will arise later, destroy the mighty and the holy people, rise against the prince of princes, and be broken without human power. Gabriel tells Daniel to seal up the vision, and Daniel faints, is sick for some days, returns to the king's business, and remains puzzled because no one understands it.

Chapter 9

In the first year of Darius the son of Ahasuerus, king over the Chaldeans, Daniel understands from the books that Jeremiah's prophecy set seventy years for the desolations of Jerusalem. He prays with fasting, sackcloth and ashes, confessing the sin of Israel, acknowledging God's righteousness and the curse written in the law of Moses, and pleading for mercy on Jerusalem and the desolate sanctuary. While he is still praying, Gabriel comes at the time of the evening offering to give him wisdom and understanding. Gabriel announces seventy weeks decreed on Daniel's people and holy city, describing the rebuilding of Jerusalem, the Anointed One being cut off, the destruction of the city and sanctuary, and a covenant broken in the middle of a week by one who makes desolate.

Chapter 10

In the third year of Cyrus king of Persia, Daniel, also called Belteshazzar, receives a true revelation about a great warfare and mourns for three weeks, abstaining from pleasant bread, meat, wine, and anointing. On the twenty-fourth day of the first month, by the river Hiddekel, he sees a man clothed in linen with a radiant, awe-inspiring appearance; his companions flee in terror while Daniel alone sees the vision and loses his strength. The man touches and strengthens Daniel, tells him he is greatly beloved, and says his words were heard from the first day he set his heart to understand. The man explains that the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood him twenty-one days until Michael came to help, and that he has come to explain what will happen to Daniel's people in the latter days. He says he will return to fight the prince of Persia, that the prince of Greece will come, and that only Michael, Daniel's prince, supports him against them.

Chapter 11

Chapter 11 is a long prophetic account of conflict between the king of the south and the king of the north. It opens with kings of Persia and a mighty king whose kingdom is broken toward the four winds and not passed to his posterity. The two kings then trade alliances, invasions, and defeats, including a failed marriage agreement and campaigns into the glorious land. A contemptible person gains the kingdom by flatteries, deceives, and moves against the king of the south, then turns against the holy covenant, with forces profaning the sanctuary and setting up the abomination that makes desolate. The wise among the people instruct many and fall, and a self-exalting king magnifies himself above every god until the end, when the king of the north comes against him and then meets his end between the sea and the glorious holy mountain.

Chapter 12

Chapter 12 describes the end-time events revealed to Daniel. Michael, the great prince who stands for Daniel's people, will arise during an unprecedented time of trouble, when everyone found written in the book will be delivered. Many who sleep in the dust will awake, some to everlasting life and some to shame and contempt, and the wise and those who turn many to righteousness will shine like the expanse and the stars. Daniel is told to shut up and seal the words until the time of the end, and he sees two others on the river banks ask the man clothed in linen how long until the end; the man swears by him who lives forever that it will be a time, times, and a half. Daniel does not understand, is told the words remain sealed, and is given the figures of 1,290 and 1,335 days before being told to go his way and that he will rest and stand in his inheritance at the end of the days.

Chapter 13

Joakim, a rich man of Babylon, has a beautiful and God-fearing wife, Susanna. Two elders who serve as judges lust after her, hide in Joakim's garden, and threaten to accuse her of adultery with a young man unless she lies with them. Susanna refuses and cries out, and the elders publicly accuse her, and the assembly condemns her to death. As she is led away, Daniel cries out that he is innocent of her blood, has the elders separated, and questions each about the tree under which they saw her, and their conflicting answers (a mastick tree and an evergreen oak) expose their lie. The people bless God, put the elders to death according to the law of Moses, and Susanna's family praises God while Daniel gains a great reputation.

Chapter 14

After King Astyages dies and Cyrus the Persian takes his kingdom, Daniel, honored by the king, refuses to worship the idol Bel and proves it eats nothing by scattering ashes on the temple floor, where the footprints of Bel's seventy priests and their families reveal their secret entrance. The king kills the priests and gives Bel and its temple to Daniel, who destroys them. Daniel then kills the worshiped dragon of Babylon by feeding it lumps of pitch, fat, and hair, which enrages the Babylonians, who threaten the king until he hands Daniel over. Daniel is thrown into the lions' den for six days, where the prophet Habakkuk is carried by the angel of the Lord from Jewry to bring him a meal. On the seventh day the king finds Daniel alive, praises Daniel's God, and casts Daniel's accusers into the den, where the lions devour them.
