Yao

person · 7 works · 31 quoted facts · 13 connections
Also called: Di Yao, Emperor Yao, The Emperor Yao, the high meritorious sovereign

Abstract written by a model

In Chuang Tzŭ, Yao is an emperor and ruler who governs through repose, protects the vulnerable, consults sages, seeks to abdicate, and ultimately gives the throne to Shun. In The Analects of Confucius, Book VIII (T'ai-po), Yao is praised as a majestic sovereign whose virtue matches Heaven and whose works and regulations are glorious. In The Analects of Confucius, Book XX (Yao Yueh), Yao tells Shun that Heaven’s succession rests with him and charges him to uphold the Mean. In Mencius, Book III (Teng Wen Gong), Yao, a sage, confronts disorder caused by floods, vegetation, and beasts before raising Shun to office. In Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou), Yao is presented as an ancient model king whose conduct should be imitated. In Mencius, Book V (Wan Zhang), Yao associates Shun with government and presents him to Heaven and the people, who accept Shun after his successful sacrifices and administration. In Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), Yao is described as naturally benevolent and righteous, devoted to the virtuous, and focused on what matters.

Yao in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where Yao first appears

Abstract written by a model

Yao is an emperor and ruler of the empire, associated with China’s Golden Age and counted, with Shun, among the foremost of creation. He governs so that happiness prevails, rules successfully through repose, tranquillity, stillness, and inaction, and protects the defenceless, poor, dying, orphaned, and widowed. Yet he is described as devoted to charity rather than truly great, and Hsü Yu warns that good men may injure the empire. Yao seeks to abdicate or resign the empire first to Hsü Yu and later to Tzŭ Chou Chih Fu, consults Hsü Yu on rulers, and is tutored by him. After visiting the sages of Miao-ku-shê and Hua, where he rejects long life, wealth, and many sons, the empire ceases to be a worldly concern for him. He ultimately abdicates in favour of Shun. While on the throne he desires to smite Tsungs, Kueis, and Hsü-aos, attacks Ts'ung-chih and Hsü-ao, kills his eldest son, and gives Ta-chang.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

_Note on the Philosophy of Chaps._ i-vii.

Emperor Yao is said to have been willing to abdicate in favor of a hermit. He is also compared with Heracleitus, who allegedly abdicated in his brother’s favor.

CHAPTER I.

Emperor Yao offers to abdicate in favour of Hsü Yu, believing that the empire will remain peaceful. After visiting the sages of the Miao-ku-shê mountain, he returns with the empire no longer existing for him as a worldly concern.

CHAPTER XII.

Yao is portrayed as an emperor who consults Hsü Yu about suitable rulers and later visits Hua, where he rejects wishes for long life, wealth, and many sons. He is also associated with handing the empire to Shun.

CHAPTER XIII.

Yao succeeds as a ruler through understanding repose, tranquillity, stillness, and inaction. He tells Shun that he protects the defenceless, poor, dying, orphaned, and widowed, but Shun says this is good rather than great.

Kuan Chung being at the point of death, Duke Huan went to see him.

Yao is portrayed as devoted to charity and as believing that good men benefit the empire. Hsü Yu says Yao fails to see that good men can also injure it.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

Yao offered to resign the empire to Hsü Yu and then to Tzŭ Chou Chih Fu. His offers illustrate that one who will not let the empire injure life may be fit to receive it.
