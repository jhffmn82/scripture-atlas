The First Book of Samuel

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

This is the book of 1 Samuel, a narrative of Israel's transition from judges to monarchy, centered on Samuel, Saul, and David, with the LORD's guidance and judgment as the driving force. It opens with Hannah, barren and provoked by Peninnah, vowing a son to the LORD; Samuel is born and given to Eli at Shiloh. Eli's wicked sons Hophni and Phinehas are condemned, and the young Samuel is called as prophet. Israel loses the ark to the Philistines in battle, Hophni, Phinehas and Eli die, and the ark afflicts Dagon and Philistine cities until it is returned and rests at Kiriath Jearim. Samuel leads Israel back to the LORD and judges them, but when his sons take bribes the elders demand a king. Despite warnings that this rejects the LORD as king, Samuel anoints Saul, a tall Benjamite looking for lost donkeys. Saul is chosen at Mizpah, rescues Jabesh Gilead from Nahash, and is confirmed at Gilgal; Samuel gives a farewell address. Saul's reign then falters: he offers the sacrifice himself rather than waiting for Samuel, and spares Agag and the best livestock of Amalek, so Samuel declares that obedience is better than sacrifice and that the kingdom is torn from him. Meanwhile Jonathan's bold attacks defeat Philistines. The LORD sends Samuel to anoint David, Jesse's youngest son, whose harp soothes Saul, and who kills Goliath with a sling. Saul's jealousy grows after the women's song, and he repeatedly tries to kill David. Jonathan covenants with David and protects him, and Michal helps him escape. David flees, receives bread and Goliath's sword at Nob, and gathers a band of about four hundred, later six hundred. Saul has Doeg kill eighty-five priests of Nob; Abiathar escapes to David. David saves Keilah, is pursued through wilderness, and twice spares Saul, the LORD's anointed, in a cave at En Gedi and in Saul's camp, while Saul admits his guilt. Samuel dies, and Abigail restrains David from vengeance on Nabal, then becomes his wife after Nabal dies. David takes refuge with Achish of Gath at Ziklag but is sent back by the Philistine princes before the final battle. He recovers his family and goods from the Amalekites and shares the plunder fairly. Saul, abandoned by the LORD, consults the woman of Endor, and Samuel's spirit foretells his death. The book ends on Mount Gilboa, where Saul and his sons, including Jonathan, die. The Philistines display Saul's body, and the men of Jabesh Gilead retrieve and bury it.

1 Samuel / Chapter 1

Elkanah of Ramathaim Zophim, an Ephraimite, had two wives: Peninnah, who had children, and Hannah, who had none because the LORD had shut up her womb. At Shiloh, Peninnah provoked Hannah year after year, and Hannah wept in bitterness and prayed, vowing that if given a boy she would give him to the LORD all his life. Eli the priest mistook her silent prayer for drunkenness, then, learning of her sorrow, blessed her and prayed that God would grant her petition. Hannah conceived and bore Samuel, and after weaning him she brought him to the LORD's house in Shiloh with offerings and presented him to Eli, giving him to the LORD as she had vowed.

Chapter 2

Hannah prays a song of praise exalting the LORD as holy, a reverser of fortunes who raises the poor and brings down the mighty, and who will strengthen his anointed king. Elkanah returns to Ramah while the child Samuel serves the LORD before Eli the priest. Eli's sons are wicked men who despise the LORD's offering by seizing meat by force and sleeping with the women who serve at the Tent of Meeting, and Eli's rebuke goes unheeded because the LORD intends to kill them. A man of God tells Eli that the LORD will cut off his house, that Hophni and Phinehas will die in one day, and that a faithful priest will be raised up in their place. Meanwhile Samuel grows in favor with the LORD and with men, and Hannah, visited by the LORD, bears three more sons and two daughters.

Chapter 3

The child Samuel serves the LORD under Eli, whose eyesight is failing, at a time when the LORD's word is rare. The LORD calls Samuel three times, and each time Samuel runs to Eli, who finally perceives that the LORD is calling and tells Samuel to answer, 'Speak, LORD; for your servant hears.' The LORD then tells Samuel he will judge Eli's house forever because Eli did not restrain his sons, who brought a curse on themselves. Samuel, afraid, finally tells Eli everything when pressed, and Eli responds, 'It is the LORD. Let him do what seems good to him.' Samuel grows, the LORD is with him, and all Israel from Dan to Beersheba knows he is established as a prophet.

Chapter 4

Israel fights the Philistines at Ebenezer and Aphek and loses about four thousand men. The elders bring the ark of the LORD's covenant from Shiloh, along with Eli's sons Hophni and Phinehas, and Israel shouts in celebration. The frightened Philistines nonetheless fight, defeat Israel again with thirty thousand footmen dead, capture the ark, and kill Hophni and Phinehas. A man of Benjamin runs to Shiloh with the news, and Eli, ninety-eight years old, falls from his seat and dies after hearing of the ark's capture. Phinehas' wife, hearing of the ark, her father-in-law and her husband, gives birth and names her son Ichabod, saying the glory has departed from Israel.

Chapter 5

The Philistines bring God's ark from Ebenezer to Ashdod and place it in the house of Dagon beside Dagon. Two mornings in a row Dagon is found fallen on his face before the ark, and the second time his head and palms are cut off on the threshold, leaving only the torso. The LORD's hand is heavy on Ashdod, striking its people with tumors, so the men of Ashdod gather the Philistine lords and send the ark to Gath. The same affliction strikes Gath, and the ark is then sent to Ekron, where the Ekronites cry out in fear and demand that it be sent back to its own place. Deadly panic and tumors afflict Ekron, and the cry of the city goes up to heaven.

Chapter 6

After the LORD's ark has been in Philistine territory seven months, the Philistines ask their priests and diviners how to send it back, and are told to return it with a trespass offering of five golden tumors and five golden mice, citing the Egyptians and Pharaoh as a warning against hardening their hearts. They put the ark and the offering on a new cart pulled by two milk cows never yoked, as a test of whether the LORD's hand struck them. The cows go straight to Beth Shemesh, where the people reaping wheat rejoice, split the cart's wood, and offer the cows as a burnt offering while the Levites set the ark on a great stone. The LORD strikes down fifty thousand seventy men of Beth Shemesh for looking into the ark, and the survivors, mourning, ask who can stand before this holy God and send messengers to Kiriath Jearim to come take the ark.

Chapter 7

After the men of Kiriath Jearim bring the LORD's ark into Abinadab's house and consecrate Eleazar to keep it, Israel laments for twenty years. Samuel calls Israel to return to the LORD with all their hearts and put away foreign gods, and the people remove the Baals and Ashtaroth and serve the LORD only. At Mizpah, Israel fasts, confesses sin, and Samuel judges them and prays for them; when the Philistines advance, Samuel offers a lamb as a burnt offering and the LORD thunders and confuses the Philistines, who are struck down and pursued by Israel. Samuel sets up a stone called Ebenezer, the Philistines are subdued, Israel's cities and border are restored, and Samuel judges Israel all his life, circuiting Bethel, Gilgal, and Mizpah and returning to Ramah, where he builds an altar.

Chapter 8

In old age, Samuel appoints his sons Joel and Abijah as judges in Beersheba, but they take bribes and pervert justice. The elders of Israel come to Samuel at Ramah and demand a king to judge them like all the nations. Displeased, Samuel prays, and the LORD tells him to heed the people because they have rejected the LORD as their king, while also warning them solemnly. Samuel describes how a king will take their sons, daughters, fields, produce, servants, and flocks, and warn that the LORD will not answer their cry; the people refuse to listen and insist on a king, so the LORD tells Samuel to give them one and Samuel sends the men home.

Chapter 9

Saul, the tall and handsome son of Kish, a Benjamite, is sent by his father with a servant to look for lost donkeys. After searching the hill country of Ephraim and the lands of Shalishah, Shaalim, and Benjamin without success, they reach the land of Zuph, where the servant suggests consulting the man of God in the city. The LORD had told Samuel the day before that he would send a man of Benjamin to be anointed prince over Israel to save it from the Philistines, and he identifies Saul when he appears. Samuel tells Saul the donkeys are found, hints that Israel desires him, and seats him in the place of honor at a sacrifice feast with about thirty guests, giving him the reserved portion. The next morning Samuel sends the servant ahead so that he can tell Saul God's message alone.

Chapter 10

Samuel anoints Saul with oil, declares him prince over the LORD's inheritance, and gives him a series of signs: men at Rachel's tomb, three men at the oak of Tabor, and a band of prophets near the hill of God. All the signs come true that day, Saul is given another heart and prophesies among the prophets, which gives rise to the proverb "Is Saul also among the prophets?" Saul tells his uncle only about the donkeys and not about the kingdom. At Mizpah Samuel rebukes Israel for rejecting God by asking for a king, and lots are cast until Saul, hiding among the baggage, is chosen. The people shout "Long live the king!", Samuel records the regulations of the kingdom, and Saul goes home to Gibeah, where some worthless fellows despise him but he holds his peace.

Chapter 11

Nahash the Ammonite encamps against Jabesh Gilead and offers the men of Jabesh a covenant only if all their right eyes are gouged out. The elders win seven days to send messengers through Israel, and when the messengers reach Gibeah of Saul the people weep. Saul, coming from the field behind his oxen, is filled with God's Spirit and anger, cuts a yoke of oxen into pieces, and sends them through Israel to summon the people after Saul and Samuel. Israel musters at Bezek, Saul divides the people into three companies and routs the Ammonites the next morning, and Saul forbids putting to death those who had doubted him. At Samuel's urging the people go to Gilgal and make Saul king before the LORD, offering peace offerings and rejoicing.

Chapter 12

Samuel addresses all Israel after a king has been set over them, noting that he is old and gray-headed and inviting them to testify against him; the people affirm he has not defrauded, oppressed, or taken anything from anyone. He recounts the LORD's righteous acts, from sending Moses and Aaron out of Egypt, through the people's forgetting and being sold to enemies, to the deliverers Jerubbaal, Bedan, Jephthah, and Samuel. He charges that when Nahash king of the Ammonites came against them, the people demanded a king though the LORD was their king. Samuel calls on the LORD to send thunder and rain during wheat harvest, and the people, terrified, confess their sin and ask him to pray for them. Samuel tells them not to fear, to keep serving the LORD with all their heart, promising to keep praying and instructing them, but warning that persistent evil will consume both them and their king.

Chapter 13

Saul, who reigned over Israel forty-two years, chose three thousand men, and Jonathan struck the Philistine garrison in Geba, provoking the Philistines to gather a huge force at Michmash. Israel's frightened people hid or scattered while Saul waited at Gilgal for Samuel for seven days. When Samuel did not arrive, Saul offered the burnt offering himself, and Samuel then arrived and told him he had acted foolishly, that his kingdom would not continue, and that the LORD had sought a man after his own heart. Saul was left with about six hundred men at Geba while Philistine raiders went out in three companies. The chapter closes by explaining that the Philistines had left Israel without blacksmiths, so only Saul and Jonathan had a sword or spear on the day of battle.

Chapter 14

Jonathan son of Saul secretly goes with his armor bearer to attack a Philistine garrison, trusting that the LORD can save by many or by few, and they kill about twenty men. Panic spreads through the Philistine camp, and Saul and his people join the battle as the Philistines strike each other with swords while Hebrews who had been with the Philistines and hidden Israelites turn against them. Saul's oath forbidding food until evening leaves the people faint; Jonathan, unaware of it, eats honey, and the weary people afterward eat meat with the blood, so Saul builds his first altar to the LORD. When God does not answer Saul's inquiry about pursuing the Philistines, lots identify Jonathan as the offender, and Saul declares he must die, but the people rescue him. The chapter ends with a summary of Saul's wars against surrounding enemies, his family, and his army captain Abner.

Chapter 15

Samuel tells Saul that the LORD commands him to utterly destroy Amalek, including all people and livestock. Saul gathers an army, warns the Kenites to leave, and defeats the Amalekites, but he and the people spare King Agag and the best of the animals. The LORD grieves that he made Saul king, and Samuel confronts Saul, who blames the people and says the animals were for sacrifice. Samuel declares that obedience is better than sacrifice and that the LORD has rejected Saul as king; Saul confesses, tears Samuel's robe, and Samuel says the kingdom is torn from him and given to a better neighbor. Samuel then kills Agag at Gilgal, and he and Saul part for good, with Samuel mourning and the LORD grieving over Saul's kingship.

Chapter 16

The LORD tells Samuel to stop mourning for the rejected Saul and to go to Jesse of Bethlehem, where he has provided a king among Jesse's sons. Samuel, fearing Saul, goes under the pretext of a sacrifice, and the LORD rejects Eliab and the other six sons presented, saying he looks at the heart rather than outward appearance. Samuel has the youngest son, David, brought from the sheep, and anoints him in front of his brothers, after which the LORD's Spirit comes mightily on David. Meanwhile the LORD's Spirit departs from Saul and an evil spirit troubles him, so his servants recommend a harp player and one of them names David. David comes to Saul, becomes his armor bearer, and his playing refreshes Saul and drives the evil spirit away.

Chapter 17

The Philistines and Saul's Israelite army face each other across the valley of Elah, where the Philistine champion Goliath of Gath, a heavily armed giant, challenges Israel to send a man to fight him, and Saul and all Israel are dismayed. David, the youngest son of Jesse of Bethlehem, brings provisions to his brothers at the camp, hears Goliath's challenge, and is rebuked by his eldest brother Eliab. David volunteers before Saul, citing how he killed a lion and a bear while keeping sheep, refuses Saul's armor, and goes out with his staff, five smooth stones, and a sling. He kills Goliath with a stone to the forehead, cuts off his head with Goliath's own sword, and the Philistines flee and are pursued by Israel and Judah. Saul then asks Abner whose son David is, and David answers that he is the son of Jesse the Bethlehemite.

Chapter 18

Jonathan bonds with David in covenant, giving him his robe, sword, bow, and sash, while Saul keeps David at his court and sets him over the men of war. When women sing that Saul has slain thousands and David ten thousands, Saul grows angry and jealous, and twice tries to pin David to the wall with his spear. Afraid because the LORD is with David, Saul makes him captain of a thousand, offers him Merab and then Michal in marriage, and sets a dowry of one hundred Philistine foreskins hoping the Philistines will kill him. David instead kills two hundred Philistines, delivers the foreskins, and marries Michal. Saul becomes David's continual enemy as David's name grows highly esteemed.

Chapter 19

Saul orders his son Jonathan and his servants to kill David, but Jonathan warns David to hide and then persuades Saul to swear that David will not be put to death. After David wins another battle against the Philistines, an evil spirit comes on Saul and he tries to pin David to the wall with a spear; David escapes. Saul sends messengers to David's house, and Michal lowers David through a window and deceives the messengers with a teraphim in the bed, then tells Saul that David threatened her. David flees to Samuel at Ramah and lives with him in Naioth, where Saul's three groups of messengers are overcome by God's Spirit and prophesy. Saul himself goes to Naioth, prophesies, strips off his clothes, and lies naked all day and night, which gives rise to the saying, "Is Saul also among the prophets?"

Chapter 20

David flees from Naioth in Ramah and asks Jonathan what he has done to make Saul seek his life; Jonathan doubts it, but David swears there is only a step between him and death. They devise a test: David will hide in the field during the new moon feast, and Jonathan will report Saul's reaction using a signal of three arrows shot beside the stone Ezel. Jonathan and David renew their covenant, and at the feast Saul notices David's empty place, then erupts in anger at Jonathan, insults him, declares David must die, and hurls a spear at him. Jonathan, grieved and angry, shoots the arrows beyond his boy as the agreed warning, sends the boy to the city, and he and David weep, kiss, and part in peace, reaffirming their oath in the LORD's name.

Chapter 21

David comes alone to Nob and tells the priest Ahimelech that the king has sent him on secret business, asking for bread. Ahimelech gives him the holy show bread after David assures him that the young men have kept themselves from women, and Doeg the Edomite, a servant of Saul, is present and detained before the LORD. David also asks for a weapon and receives the sword of Goliath, which is wrapped in a cloth behind the ephod. David then flees from Saul to Achish, king of Gath, where the servants of Achish recall the song about Saul's thousands and David's ten thousands. Afraid, David pretends to be insane by scribbling on the gate doors and letting spittle run down his beard, and Achish, disgusted, dismisses him.

Chapter 22

David flees to Adullam's cave, where his brothers, his father's house, and about four hundred distressed, indebted, and discontented men gather to him as captain. He takes his parents to the king of Moab for safekeeping, and the prophet Gad then tells him to leave the stronghold for the land of Judah, so he goes to the forest of Hereth. Saul, learning David has been found, accuses his Benjamite servants of conspiracy, and Doeg the Edomite reports that David came to Ahimelech at Nob, who inquired of the LORD for him and gave him food and Goliath's sword. Saul summons Ahimelech and the priests, rejects Ahimelech's defense, and condemns them to death; when his guards refuse, Doeg kills eighty-five priests and destroys Nob with its people and animals. Abiathar, one of Ahimelech's sons, escapes to David, who admits responsibility and promises him safety.

Chapter 23

David learns that the Philistines are attacking Keilah, inquires of the LORD, and with his men saves the town, after which Abiathar the son of Ahimelech comes to him with an ephod. Saul hears David is in Keilah and summons the people to besiege him, but the LORD tells David that Saul will come and that the men of Keilah would deliver him up, so David and his roughly six hundred men leave. David stays in the wilderness of Ziph, where Jonathan comes to strengthen him and they make a covenant before the LORD. The Ziphites inform Saul of David's hiding place, and Saul pursues him into the wilderness of Maon, nearly surrounding him, until a messenger reports a Philistine raid and Saul turns back, after which David goes to the strongholds of En Gedi.

Chapter 24

Saul takes three thousand chosen men to hunt David in the wilderness of En Gedi, and goes into a cave to relieve himself, where David and his men are hiding. David's men urge him to seize the chance, but David only cuts off the skirt of Saul's robe and then stops his men from attacking, because Saul is the LORD's anointed. David calls after Saul, shows him the skirt as proof he spared him, and asks the LORD to judge between them. Saul weeps, admits David is more righteous, acknowledges that David will be king, and makes David swear not to cut off his offspring or destroy his name. David swears, Saul goes home, and David and his men go up to the stronghold.

Chapter 25

After Samuel dies and is mourned, David goes down to the wilderness of Paran and sends ten young men to ask the wealthy, surly Nabal of Maon for provisions during his sheep-shearing. Nabal insults David and refuses, so David arms about four hundred men and swears to kill every male of Nabal's house. Nabal's wife Abigail, warned by one of Nabal's young men, secretly brings a large gift of food to David, takes the blame on herself, and persuades him not to shed blood or avenge himself. She tells Nabal nothing until morning because he is drunk, and when he hears he becomes as a stone and dies about ten days later when the LORD strikes him. David praises the LORD, then sends for Abigail and she becomes his wife, along with Ahinoam of Jezreel, while Saul has given David's wife Michal to Palti.

Chapter 26

The Ziphites tell Saul that David is hiding in the hill of Hachilah, so Saul goes down to the wilderness of Ziph with three thousand chosen men and camps there. David learns of this through spies and goes by night with Abishai to Saul's camp, where Saul, Abner, and the people lie asleep under a deep sleep from the LORD. Abishai urges David to let him kill Saul with the spear, but David refuses to harm the LORD's anointed and instead takes the spear and the jar of water from Saul's head. From a distant mountaintop David calls out to Abner, rebukes him for failing to guard the king, and shows the spear as proof, and Saul recognizes David's voice. Saul admits he has sinned and played the fool, blesses David, and the two part ways.

Chapter 27

David, fearing he will one day die by Saul's hand, decides to escape to the land of the Philistines and goes with his six hundred men and their households, including his two wives Ahinoam and Abigail, to Achish, king of Gath. When Saul hears David has fled to Gath, he stops searching for him. David asks Achish for a place in the country, and Achish gives him Ziklag, where David lives a full year and four months. David and his men raid the Geshurites, Girzites, and Amalekites, killing every man and woman and taking livestock and clothing. David tells Achish he raided the South of Judah, Jerahmeelites, and Kenites, and Achish believes him, concluding that David has made himself abhorrent to Israel and will be his servant forever.

Chapter 28

The Philistines gather to fight Israel, and Achish tells David he and his men must march with him, promising to make David his bodyguard. Saul, terrified at the Philistine camp at Shunem and unanswered by the LORD through dreams, Urim, or prophets, disguises himself and goes by night with two men to a woman at Endor who has a familiar spirit. After Saul swears she will not be punished, she brings up Samuel, who tells Saul that the LORD has become his adversary, has given the kingdom to David because of Saul's disobedience regarding Amalek, and will deliver Israel and Saul and his sons to the Philistines the next day. Saul collapses from fear and from having eaten nothing, and the woman and his servants persuade him to eat a meal of a fattened calf and unleavened bread before they leave that night.

Chapter 29

The Philistines gather all their armies at Aphek while the Israelites camp by the spring in Jezreel. David and his men march in the rear with Achish, prompting the Philistine princes to ask about these Hebrews. Achish vouches for David's faithfulness, but the princes angrily demand he be sent back, fearing David would turn against them in battle and win his lord's favor with their heads, and they recall the song about Saul's thousands and David's ten thousands. Achish reluctantly tells David to go in peace, and David protests that he has done nothing wrong. David and his men rise early and depart for the land of the Philistines, while the Philistines go up to Jezreel.

Chapter 30

David and his men return to Ziklag on the third day to find it burned by the Amalekites, who had taken their wives, sons, and daughters captive. The grieving men talk of stoning David, but he strengthens himself in the LORD, has Abiathar the priest bring the ephod, and inquires of the LORD, who tells him to pursue and he will recover all. Of the six hundred men, two hundred are too faint to cross the brook Besor, so David presses on with four hundred, and an Egyptian servant abandoned by the Amalekites leads them to the raiders' camp. David strikes the Amalekites from twilight until the evening of the next day, with only four hundred young men on camels escaping, and recovers everything and everyone taken. When wicked men among his company refuse to share the plunder with the two hundred who stayed behind, David rules that all shall share alike, makes it a statute for Israel, and sends gifts from the plunder to the elders of Judah and to many towns.

Chapter 31

The Philistines fight Israel on Mount Gilboa, and the men of Israel flee and fall slain. The Philistines kill Saul's sons Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchishua, and archers overtake Saul and badly wound him. Saul asks his armor bearer to kill him so the uncircumcised cannot abuse him; when the armor bearer refuses out of terror, Saul falls on his own sword, and the armor bearer then does likewise. Israelites across the valley and beyond the Jordan abandon their cities, and the Philistines occupy them. The Philistines cut off Saul's head, strip his armor, display it in the house of the Ashtaroth, and fasten his body to the wall of Beth Shan, but the valiant men of Jabesh Gilead retrieve the bodies at night, burn them, bury the bones under the tamarisk tree in Jabesh, and fast seven days.
