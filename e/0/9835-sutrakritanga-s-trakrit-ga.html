Abstract written by a model

This document summarizes the first book of the Sutrakritanga, a sequence of sixteen lectures on bondage through Karman and liberation through disciplined religious life. Sudharman, Gambûsvâmin, Mahâvîra, Rishabha, Kêvalin, Kâsyapa, Arhat Gñâtriputra, and the Venerable One frame or convey its teachings. Its central claim is that possessions, violence, passions, false doctrine, sensual attachment, and carelessness bind the Soul to Karman, repeated birth, death, suffering, and hells; restraint, non-killing, purity, detachment, austerity, meditation, truthful conduct, and control of mind, speech, and body lead toward Nirvâna, Môksha, Peace, and final Liberation. The work first rejects views denying rebirth, moral causation, the Soul, or the true Law, then teaches that every being bears the consequences of its own Karman and that worldly ties are transient. It addresses the trials of monks, hostility, hunger, weather, injury, insult, illness, relatives, rulers, heretics, and desire, and urges endurance, mutual care, and adherence to the Law. Several lectures warn especially against sexual temptation, domestic attachment, property, and harmful conduct; the description of hells illustrates the torments awaiting sinners. Mahâvîra, also called Gñâtriputra and Vardhamâna, is praised as the omniscient teacher who conquered passions, annihilated Karman, and proclaimed the highest Law. The later lectures define careful monastic conduct: avoid injury, theft, sexual intercourse, false speech, improper food, attachment, and alms linked to slaughter; obey wise teachers; accept correction; and treat all beings tenderly. They criticize false creeds and merely external practices, affirming that right knowledge and right conduct, not ritual alone, free one from the Circle of Births. The book closes by describing the qualities of the true Brâhmana, Sramana, Bhikshu, and Nirgrantha: one who abandons sin and passion, subdues the senses, endures hardship, follows prescribed conduct, and seeks Liberation.

Sutrakritanga (Sûtrakritâṅga), Book I / First Book. / First Lecture, Called the Doctrine. / First Chapter. .. Fourth Chapter.

This unit of Sutrakritanga concerns the bondage and liberation of Soul, as explained through Sudharman’s answers to Gambûsvâmin and through Mahâvîra’s teaching. It teaches that possessions, violence, passions, wrong doctrines, and attachment cause Karman and misery, while restraint, purity, and non-killing lead toward Nirvâna and perfection. It rejects theories that deny rebirth, moral causation, the Soul, or the true Law, including doctrines concerning the five gross elements, fate, creation, and the nature of the world. Sramanas and Brâhmanas who follow such errors remain in Samsâra and repeatedly undergo birth, death, and suffering.

Second Lecture, Called the Destruction of Karman. / First Chapter. .. Third Chapter.

Rishabha teaches his sons that all living beings suffer the consequences of their own Karman and that life, pleasures, possessions, and domestic ties are transient. The unit instructs Sramanas and monks to cease from sin, control mind, speech, and body, abstain from harming living beings, practice austerities and meditation, and follow the Law. It presents right conduct and detachment as the way to destroy Karman, cross Samsâra, and attain liberation. Arhat Gñâtriputra concludes by affirming that the Ginas of the past, present, and future commend these virtues.

Third Lecture, Called the Knowledge of Troubles / First Chapter. .. Fourth Chapter.

The Third Lecture, Called the Knowledge of Troubles, concerns the hardships faced by monks and Sramanas and the weakness that causes some to abandon the mendicant's life. It describes opposition from relatives, rulers, heretics, villagers, hunger, weather, injuries, insults, and attachment to worldly desires and women. It teaches that monks should practise control, follow the Law, care for sick brothers, avoid sin, and endure suffering and hardships until final liberation and Nirvâna.

Fourth Lecture, Called Knowledge of Women. / First Chapter. .. Second Chapter.

The Fourth Lecture, Called Knowledge of Women, concerns monks and Sramanas who seek to avoid sexual pleasures and intercourse with women. The First Chapter describes women’s temptations, the dangers of intimacy, the sins and punishments associated with adulterous conduct, and the need for monks to guard themselves. The Second Chapter describes monks who abandon discipline for women and pleasures, become servants in domestic tasks, and are urged to abstain from women and commit no unnatural crime. A monk whose soul is pure is instructed to endure troubles and wander until final liberation.

Fifth Lecture, Called Description of the Hells. / First Chapter. .. Second Chapter.

The Fifth Lecture, Called Description of the Hells, concerns the punishments suffered by sinners for bad deeds. Kêvalin asks Kâsyapa about the punishment in the hells, and Kâsyapa describes many places of torture, including Vaitaranî and Asûrya. Sinners are repeatedly beaten, cut, burned, boiled, pierced, devoured, and subjected to other torments according to their deeds. The lecture teaches that a wise man should avoid harming living beings, renounce property, understand the Circle of Births, and practise self-control.

Sixth Lecture, Called Praise of Mahâvîra.

The Sixth Lecture praises Mahâvîra, also called Gñâtriputra and Vardhamâna, as an omniscient sage who proclaimed the highest Law. It describes his infinite knowledge, faith, virtue, austerities, mastery of philosophical systems, and conquest of the passions. Mahâvîra annihilated all Karman and reached the highest perfection, while serving as the foremost teacher of Nirvâna. The lecture compares his greatness with mountains, gods, rivers, animals, and other supreme examples.

Seventh Lecture, Called Description of the Wicked.

The unit concerns living beings, men, monks, and the conduct that leads toward or away from liberation. It teaches that all beings desire happiness, that harming them creates Karman, and that sinners suffer and are repeatedly born in the Samsâra. It rejects bathing, fire-tending, food abstinence, and similar practices as paths to perfection, while urging restraint, wisdom, nonviolence, and freedom from attachment. A monk should endure pain, subdue passions through control, annihilate Karman, and avoid returning to the world.

Eighth Lecture, Called on Exertion.

The Eighth Lecture, Called on Exertion, explains the difference between exertion that leads to works and exertion that does not. It describes how carelessness, passions, and harmful acts cause Karman and misery for sinners, while the virtuous pursue control, meditation, and abstention from sins. It teaches that a wise man should conquer greed, follow the noble path and right doctrine, and prepare for a religious death. The unit concludes that a pious monk should practise restraint and religious discipline until liberation.

Ninth Lecture, Called the Law.

The unit explains the Law preached by Mahâvîra and the noble Law of the Ginas. It teaches that a monk should abandon property, worldly interests, sinful acts, pleasures, and harmful conduct, while treating all living beings tenderly. The monk should control speech, endure abuse and pain without anger, obey a wise teacher, and reject worldly vanities and false teachings. By following these teachings, a wise man brings about final Liberation.

Tenth Lecture, Called Carefulness.

The Tenth Lecture, Called Carefulness, teaches how a monk should live carefully according to the Law proclaimed by the Arhat. It instructs him to avoid injuring living beings, taking what is not freely given, sexual intercourse, property, unallowed food, false speech, and worldly attachment. The unit explains that sin and Karman arise from harmful acts and that carefulness, control, truth, austerity, and impartiality lead toward Liberation and Nirvâna.

Eleventh Lecture, Called the Path.

The unit explains the Path taught by Kâsyapa and Mahâvîra as the disciplined way across Samsâra toward liberation, Beatitude, and Peace. It teaches that the six classes of living beings must be studied and not killed, and that a monk must avoid harm in thought, word, and deed. It also gives rules for accepting alms and food and drink, especially rejecting offerings connected with slaughter of living beings. Heretical Sramanas are condemned for ignorance and wrong meditation, while the restrained monk who follows the Law crosses Samsâra and benefits his soul.

Twelfth Lecture, Called the Creed.

The Twelfth Lecture, Called the Creed, contrasts the four creeds: Kriyâvâda, Akriyâvâda, Vinayavâda, and Agñânavâda. It criticizes agnostics, Akriyâvâdins, and Vinayavâda for false or incoherent views, especially denial of action and Karman. It presents the teachings of Sramanas, Brâhmanas, and Tîrthakaras that works produce misery, right knowledge and conduct lead to liberation, and the world and its individual beings continue through repeated births. It praises pious men who practise control, avoid injury, understand the Law, and become free from the Circle of Births.

Thirteenth Lecture, Called the Real Truth.

The Thirteenth Lecture, Called the Real Truth, explains the qualities, virtues, and faults of men, especially monks. It teaches that right knowledge, conduct, self-control, humility, and truthful instruction lead away from deceit, pride, and harmful Karman. It warns that attachment to birth, fame, knowledge, austerities, sensual objects, or dishonest teaching prevents progress and may prolong punishment or the Circle of Births. It concludes by urging a monk to avoid injury, honour, fame, passion, and desire for life or death, and to wander released from the Circle of Births.

Fourteenth Lecture, Called the Nirgrantha.

The Nirgrantha concerns the conduct and instruction of monks who have given up worldly ties and follow the creed. It teaches that a monk should live with a teacher, master the Law, avoid carelessness, accept correction without anger, and protect living beings. It explains how a novice learns the Law through the words of the Ginas and how a qualified monk should teach the creed truthfully and clearly. Such practice leads toward peace, the annihilation of sin, and final Liberation.

Fifteenth Lecture, Called the Yamakas.

The Fifteenth Lecture, Called the Yamakas, concerns the Leader and Saviour, the Law, control, Karman, and the path beyond misery. It teaches that those who renounce hostility, subdue their senses, avoid new acts, and practise control can destroy Karman and reach Môksha. It states that men may become perfected saints or gods, while gods do not put an end to misery in the same way. It describes wise men, Great Heroes, and Tathâgatas as reaching or embodying freedom from rebirth and death.

Sixteenth Lecture, Called the Song.

The Sixteenth Lecture, Called the Song, explains why a person who subdues the senses, is well qualified, and abandons the body is called a Brâhmana, Sramana, Bhikshu, or Nirgrantha. The Venerable One describes the conduct and qualities associated with each designation. These include abandoning sinful actions and passions, practising restraint, enduring troubles, following prescribed conduct, and seeking Liberation. The Venerable One declares that this teaching is true because he is the Saviour.
