Abstract written by a model

In Chuang Tzŭ, Yen Yüan is a disciple of Confucius who seeks to help suffering people, develops inward straightness and outward crookedness, progresses toward unity with the Infinite, and remains with Confucius during hardship. In The Analects of Confucius, Book V (Kung-Ye Ch'Ang), Yen Yuan says he wishes neither to boast of his excellence nor display his meritorious deeds. In The Analects of Confucius, Book V (Kung-Ye Ch'Ang), Hui understands an entire subject after hearing one point and surpasses Tsze-kung in the Master's comparison. In The Analects of Confucius, Book VI (Yung Yey), Yen Hui loves learning, does not transfer anger or repeat faults, and dies after a short appointed time. In The Analects of Confucius, Book VI (Yung Yey), Hui maintains a mind free from anything contrary to perfect virtue and lives joyfully despite poverty and distress. In The Analects of Confucius, Book IX (Tsze Han), Hui is described as never flagging when the Master sets forth anything to him. In The Analects of Confucius, Book IX (Tsze Han), Yen Yuan admires and studies the Master's doctrines and advances constantly despite difficulty grasping them. In The Analects of Confucius, Book XI (Hsien Tsin), Yen Yuan is a devoted disciple who nearly attains perfect virtue, dies young, and is deeply mourned by Confucius.

Yen Yüan in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where Yen Yüan first appears

Abstract written by a model

Yen Yüan, also called Tzŭ Yüan and Yen Hui, is a disciple of Confucius. He seeks to help suffering people in Wei and influence its prince, initially proposing gravity, dispassionateness, energy, and singleness of purpose. Confucius warns against direct moral intervention and teaches him the fasting of the heart. Yen Yüan develops inward straightness and outward crookedness: he serves God inwardly, serves people through conformity, and voices the warnings of the sages. In a progression toward perfection, he discards charity and duty, then ceremonial and music, and finally body and reasoning powers, becoming one with the Infinite. He later travels east to Ch'i, where Confucius fears his preaching may endanger him. He questions Confucius about medicine, boatmanship, God, man, adaptation, and influence without striving; he can imitate Confucius's outward teaching but not follow him beyond mortality. Content with land, lute, and study, he declines official life and remains with Confucius during hardship.

He plans to visit the State of Wei to help its suffering people and reform the Prince of Wei. Confucius warns him against direct moral intervention and teaches him the fasting of the heart.

Nan Po Tzŭ K'uei

Yen Hui successively says that he has discarded charity and duty, ceremonial and music, and finally everything. He describes freeing himself from body and reasoning powers and becoming one with the Infinite.

CHAPTER XVIII.

Yen Yüan goes east to the Ch'i State. Confucius worries that he may preach the Tao of Yao and Shun to the prince and thereby place himself in danger.

CHAPTER XX.

Yen Hui listens while Confucius explains that injury from God is easy to escape but the benefits of man are difficult to avoid. He asks about adaptation, beginnings and ends, and the unity of man and God.

CHAPTER XXI.

Yen Yüan says that he can imitate Confucius's walking, speaking, arguing, and preaching but cannot follow him beyond mortality. He is told that the death of the mind is the deepest sorrow, and later hears Confucius acknowledge the vastness of the universe after speaking with Lao Tzŭ.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

Yen Hui was content with modest land, his lute, and studying Confucius's doctrines, so he did not wish to enter official life. During Confucius's hardship, he picked over herbs and reported Tzŭ Lu's and Tzŭ Kung's doubts to Confucius.
