Abstract written by a model

In Genesis, Joseph rises from favored son and enslaved prisoner to ruler of Egypt, preserves life during famine, forgives his brothers, and is buried in a coffin in Egypt. In Deuteronomy, Joseph receives Moses’ blessing of fruitful land and strength to overcome peoples through Ephraim and Manasseh. In Psalms, Joseph is sold as a slave, imprisoned, and then made lord of the king’s house after his word proves true. In The Book of Ezekiel, Joseph is represented by a stick that the LORD promises to join with Judah’s stick as one. In The Acts of the Apostles, Joseph is sold into Egypt by his jealous brothers, becomes governor through God’s favor, and summons Jacob and seventy-five relatives.

Joseph in Genesis

author unknown · about 449 BC · the work’s page · read it where Joseph first appears

Abstract written by a model

Joseph is a Hebrew man, son of Jacob (Israel) in his old age, whose life moves from favored youth to slave, prisoner, and finally ruler of Egypt. At seventeen he is loved most by his father, given a tunic of many colors, and tells dreams of his family bowing to him. His brothers strip him, throw him into a pit, and sell him for twenty pieces of silver into Egypt, where Potiphar buys him. Because the LORD is with him, he prospers and becomes overseer of Potiphar's house. He refuses Potiphar's wife, saying it would be sin against God, but is falsely accused and imprisoned. There he again gains favor with the keeper and is put in charge of the prisoners, including Pharaoh's chief cup bearer and chief baker. He says interpretations belong to God and interprets their dreams; the cup bearer forgets him, though Joseph claims he was stolen from the land of the Hebrews and is innocent. Brought before Pharaoh at thirty, he interprets the dreams of cattle as seven years of plenty followed by seven of famine and advises storing grain. Pharaoh sets him over Egypt, gives him a signet ring, the name Zaphenath-Paneah, and Asenath as wife; she bears Manasseh and Ephraim. He sells grain during the famine. When his brothers come, he recognizes them, acts as a stranger, accuses them of spying, keeps Simeon bound, and weeps privately. He tests them with Benjamin, favors Benjamin at the meal, and plants his silver cup in Benjamin's sack, claiming to practice divination. He then reveals himself, saying God sent him ahead to preserve life and made him father to Pharaoh and ruler of Egypt. He gives gifts, including silver and clothing to Benjamin, brings Jacob's family to Goshen, and provides them bread. He acquires the money, livestock, and land of the Egyptians for Pharaoh, and sets a fifth of the harvest as Pharaoh's due. He swears to bury Jacob in his fathers' burying place, and objects when Jacob lays his right hand on Ephraim. Jacob calls him a fruitful vine, strengthened by the Mighty One of Jacob, separated from his brothers. After burying Jacob in Canaan, he reassures his fearful brothers that God meant their evil for good and promises to provide for them, showing a forgiving, kind disposition. He lives 110 years, sees Ephraim's children to the third generation, makes Israel's children swear to carry up his bones, and is embalmed and placed in a coffin in Egypt.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

13 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 37

Joseph, seventeen years old, brings an evil report of his brothers to their father, is loved most by Israel, and tells two dreams of his family bowing to him. Sent by his father to find his brothers, he is found at Dothan, stripped of his tunic, thrown into a pit, and sold for twenty pieces of silver into Egypt, where he is sold to Potiphar, captain of Pharaoh's guard.

Chapter 39

Joseph is brought to Egypt, sold to Potiphar, and prospers as overseer of his household because the LORD is with him. He refuses the advances of his master's wife, saying it would be great wickedness and sin against God, and flees, leaving his garment behind. Falsely accused, he is imprisoned, but again gains favor and is put in charge of all the prisoners.

Chapter 40

Joseph, bound in the prison, is assigned to care for Pharaoh's two officers and notices they are sad. He says interpretations belong to God, interprets both dreams, and asks the cup bearer to remember him and mention him to Pharaoh, saying he was stolen from the land of the Hebrews and has done nothing to deserve the dungeon. His interpretations prove true, but the cup bearer forgets him.

Chapter 41

Joseph is brought hastily from the dungeon, shaves, changes his clothing, and appears before Pharaoh. He says the interpretation is not in him but that God will give Pharaoh an answer, explains the dreams as seven years of plenty and seven of famine, and advises storing grain. Pharaoh sets him over Egypt at age thirty; he gathers grain, fathers Manasseh and Ephraim, and sells grain to Egyptians and to all countries during the famine.

Chapter 42

Joseph, governor over the land and seller of grain, recognizes his brothers and acts like a stranger, speaking roughly and accusing them of being spies as he remembers his dreams about them. He imprisons them for three days, keeps Simeon bound, and weeps privately after overhearing their remorse. He orders their bags filled with grain and their money restored to their sacks.

Chapter 43

Joseph sees Benjamin with the brothers and orders his steward to bring them to his house for a noon meal. He asks after their father, is moved to tears at the sight of Benjamin and weeps in his room, then washes his face, controls himself, and has the meal served. He seats the brothers by age and sends Benjamin a portion five times larger than the others'.

Chapter 44

Joseph ordered his steward to fill the men's sacks, return their money, and put his silver cup in the youngest's sack, then sent the steward after them to accuse them of theft. When the cup was found, he said the one holding it would be his slave and the others could go in peace to their father, and he claimed that a man like him can do divination.

Chapter 45

Joseph clears the room, weeps aloud, and reveals himself to his brothers, telling them that God sent him ahead to preserve life and made him father to Pharaoh, lord of Pharaoh's house, and ruler of Egypt. He directs them to bring Jacob and their households to Goshen, embraces and kisses them, and sends them off with wagons, provisions, clothing, and gifts, including three hundred pieces of silver and five changes of clothing for Benjamin.

Chapter 46

Joseph is said to have had Manasseh and Ephraim born to him in Egypt by Asenath, daughter of Potiphera, priest of On. He prepares his chariot and goes to Goshen to meet his father, falling on his neck and weeping, then instructs his brothers on what to tell Pharaoh so they can dwell in Goshen.

Chapter 47

Joseph reports his family's arrival to Pharaoh, presents five of his brothers, and settles his father and brothers in the best of the land, providing them with bread. He collects all the money, then the livestock, then the land of the Egyptians for Pharaoh, moves the people to the cities, and sets a statute that Pharaoh receives a fifth of the harvest. At the end he promises and swears to Jacob that he will bury him in his fathers' burying place rather than in Egypt.

Chapter 48

Joseph is told his father is sick and brings his sons Manasseh and Ephraim to him, then bows with his face to the earth. He positions the boys so Manasseh is at Jacob's right hand, and is displeased when Jacob lays his right hand on Ephraim, trying to move it. Jacob refuses his objection, blesses him, and gives him one portion above his brothers.

Chapter 49

Jacob calls Joseph a fruitful vine by a spring whose branches run over the wall. Though archers grieved and persecuted him, his bow remained strong by the Mighty One of Jacob, and the blessings of his father will rest on the head of him who is separated from his brothers.

Chapter 50

Joseph weeps over his father, has him embalmed, secures Pharaoh's permission, and leads the funeral party to Canaan to bury him. When his brothers fear revenge, he reassures them, saying he is not in God's place and that God meant their evil for good, and he promises to provide for them. He lives to one hundred ten, sees Ephraim's children to the third generation, makes Israel's children swear to carry up his bones, and is embalmed and placed in a coffin in Egypt.
