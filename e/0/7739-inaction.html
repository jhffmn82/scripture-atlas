Inaction

topic · 1 work · 8 quoted facts · 3 connections
Also called: Tranquil inaction, _Inaction_, inaction, tranquil inaction

Abstract written by a model

Inaction is a wondrous doctrine, identified with passivity and the TAO, through which doing nothing enables all things to be done. It teaches that the soft and weak overcome the hard and strong, brings fame, and makes a perfect man the centre of thought, responsibility, and wisdom without outward display. Made the head of existence by four friends, it is linked to wandering beyond mortality and conventionality. For rulers, it adapts people and the world to natural conditions, lets things mature without interference, and supports the State through respect and love. In the empire of old, it allowed natural conditions to prevail: the sovereign avoided cruel mandates, people and officials fulfilled their roles, and all things followed their course. It also settles disputes, disarms armed men, and achieves great political effects without striving.

Inaction is in one work on this site, Chuang Tzŭ. This is the abstract a model wrote of it from that work.

Inaction in Chuang Tzŭ

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

_Introduction._

Inaction is the wondrous doctrine on which Lao Tzŭ's claim to immortality is said to rest. It teaches that doing nothing allows all things to be done and that the soft and weak can overcome the hard and strong.

Nan Po Tzŭ K'uei

Inaction is made the head of existence in the resolution accepted by four friends. It is also associated with the state in which men wander beyond mortality and conventionality.

CHAPTER VII.

It brings fame, makes one a centre of thought and responsibility, and allows compliance with divine principles without outward display. It is identified with passivity.

CHAPTER XI.

Inaction allows people and the world to adapt to natural conditions and brings things to maturity without premeditated interference. A ruler who practices it can support and govern the State while avoiding harm to internal economy and natural goodness.

CHAPTER XII.

Inaction governed the empire of old, allowing natural conditions to prevail over artificial arrangements. Through inaction, the sovereign avoided cruel mandates, people fulfilled their roles, officials governed appropriately, and all things proceeded on their course.

Kuan Chung being at the point of death, Duke Huan went to see him.

Inaction is credited with arranging disputes and causing armed men to lay down their weapons. It is described as part of the TAO through which great effects are achieved without striving.
