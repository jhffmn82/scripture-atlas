Mencius, Book III (Teng Wen Gong)

Abstract written by a model

This record summarizes Mencius, Book III (Teng Wen Gong), in which Mencius teaches rulers, scholars, and critics that humane government and moral integrity depend on following the way of the sages. It begins with the prince who becomes duke Wen of Teng: Mencius tells him that human nature is good and that even small Teng can become a good State. After Duke Ding dies, Mencius urges the prince to observe the three years’ mourning despite opposition; the prince’s sincere grief wins public admiration. As duke, he asks how to govern, and Mencius prescribes secure livelihoods, fair land arrangements, education, and benevolent rule, explaining the nine-squares system and mutual cultivation of a public field. Mencius then disputes Xu Xing’s demand that rulers farm alongside everyone else, defending a division of labor and rulers’ duty to provide order and moral instruction. He challenges the Mohist Yi Zhi for advocating spare funerals while burying his own parents richly, arguing that filial feeling naturally requires proper burial; Yi Zhi accepts the instruction. Part II develops Mencius’s defense of principle. He rejects waiting on princes merely for influence, says a great man cannot be moved by riches, poverty, or power, and explains that officers may seek office only through proper means. He defends support for scholars whose moral service benefits society. Addressing Song’s danger from Qi and Chu, he says genuine royal government would draw the people’s allegiance, as Tang and king Wu did when overthrowing oppressors. He stresses that a ruler’s attendants shape his character and that one virtuous adviser cannot reform a king surrounded by bad influences. Mencius also condemns delaying the end of known injustice, using the stolen fowls comparison. He explains that he disputes Yang Zhu, Mo Di, and other teachings because disorder follows when sage principles decline; he aims to defend the sovereign, filial affection, and the work of Yu, Zhou Gong, and Confucius. The book closes with his criticism of Chen Zhong’s supposed purity as impractical and inconsistent.

Mencius, Book III (Teng Wen Gong) / Part I / Chapter 1

The prince, afterwards duke Wen of Teng, visits Mencius while traveling to Chu by way of Song and hears that human nature is good, with Yao and Shun praised. On his return from Chu, he visits Mencius again and is urged not to doubt that the path to excellence is one and available to everyone. Mencius cites Cheng Gan, Yan Yuan, Gong Ming Yi, King Wen, and the duke of Zhou to support this teaching. Mencius says Teng is small but sufficient to become a good State and compares his instruction to medicine that must provoke a commotion to cure disease.

Chapter 2

After Duke Ding of Teng dies, the prince sends Ran You to consult Mencius about funeral duties. Mencius teaches that the three years’ mourning and related practices were prescribed by the three dynasties for everyone from the sovereign to the common people. Although the prince’s aged relatives and officers oppose the practice, Mencius says the prince must set the example himself. The prince observes mourning, and the interment draws people from across the State, who are impressed by his grief.

Chapter 3

Duke Wen of Teng asks Mencius about governing, and Mencius explains that the people need a secure livelihood, fair land arrangements, and education. Mencius contrasts the tax system with the system of mutual aid, arguing that taxation can burden the people in bad years. He urges the establishment of educational institutions and says Teng can renew its kingdom through benevolent government. Mencius later explains the nine-squares system of dividing the land to Bi Zhan, including the cultivation of a public field by eight families.

Chapter 4

Chapter 4 concerns Xu Xing's teachings and Mencius's responses to them. Xu Xing teaches that rulers should cultivate the ground and work alongside their people, while Mencius argues for the division of labor and the distinct duties of rulers, husbandmen, and handicraftsmen. Mencius also recounts how Yao, Shun, Yu, and other sages secured food, order, and moral instruction for the people. The chapter concludes with Mencius rejecting Xu Xing's claim that equal prices would eliminate deceit in the market.

Chapter 5

Yi Zhi, a Mohist, sought to meet Mencius through Xu Bi, and Mencius initially declined because he was unwell. When Mencius agreed to receive him, he criticized Yi Zhi for promoting spare simplicity in funerals while burying his own parents sumptuously. Yi Zhi defended loving all people while beginning with one's parents, but Mencius argued that Heaven gives creatures one root and that filial feeling naturally leads to proper burial. After hearing Mencius's explanation, Yi Zhi acknowledged that Mencius had instructed him.

Part II / Chapter 1

Chen Dai questions Mencius's refusal to wait upon the princes, arguing that doing so might enable one of them to become sovereign or chief. Mencius cites Confucius's approval of a forester who refused an inappropriate summons and tells the story of Wang Liang, who declined to drive deceitfully for a mean archer. He argues that bending one's principles for gain is wrong, concluding that no one who bends himself can make others straight.

Chapter 2

Jing Chun asks Mencius whether Gong Sun Yan and Zhang Yi are great men because their anger frightens the princes and their quietness extinguishes trouble. Mencius rejects this view and contrasts such compliance with the rule for women. He says a great man lives in the wide house, stands in the correct seat, walks in the great path, serves the people when in office, and maintains his principles when disappointed. A great man is not swayed by riches, poverty, or power.

Chapter 3

Zhou Xiao asks Mencius whether superior men of old time took office and why they were so concerned about employment by a ruler. Mencius explains that officers were condoled with after three months without office because losing office is like a prince losing his State, and an officer needs office to fulfill his proper work. He compares an officer carrying his gift of introduction between States to a husbandman carrying his plough. Mencius says that although the ancients desired office, they hated obtaining it through improper means.

Chapter 4

Peng Geng questions Mencius about whether it is extravagant for a scholar to move between princes and receive substantial support without performing ordinary labor. Mencius argues that support is proper when there is a proper ground for receiving it, comparing the scholar’s service to the interdependence of farmers, textile workers, carpenters, and carriage-wrights. He maintains that a scholar who is filial, respectful, and preserves benevolence and righteousness serves society and deserves support. Mencius concludes that people remunerate work done rather than intention, as shown by refusing to pay someone who damages tiles and walls.

Chapter 5

Wan Zhang asks Mencius what Song should do because Qi and Chu attack its ruler, who is said to be practising true royal government. Mencius recounts how Tang punished the chief of Ge after his people robbed and killed provision-carriers, and how Tang’s campaigns won popular support. He also describes how king Wu rescued the people of Shang by destroying their oppressors. Mencius concludes that if Song truly practised royal government, people throughout the four seas would want its ruler as their sovereign, leaving little for Qi and Chu to threaten.

Chapter 6

Mencius tells Dai Bu Sheng how the king of Song may be made virtuous. He explains that a person cannot learn a language if surrounded by people speaking another language, despite repeated punishment. Mencius argues that if all the king’s attendants were Xue Ju Zhous, the king would have no one with whom to do evil, but if none were like Xue Ju Zhou, he would have no one with whom to do good. He concludes that one Xue Ju Zhou cannot accomplish much alone for the king of Song.

Chapter 7

Gong Sun Chou asks Mencius about the righteousness involved in not going to see the princes. Mencius explains examples of avoiding or meeting princes, including Duan Gan Mu, Xie Liu, Yang Huo, and Confucius. He concludes that remarks by Zengzi and Zi Lu reveal the spirit that the superior man nourishes.

Chapter 8

Dai Ying Zhi tells Mencius that he cannot immediately levy only a tithe and abolish duties at passes and markets. He proposes lightening the tax and duties until next year, then ending them. Mencius compares this delay to a man who takes his neighbor’s strayed fowls and argues that known unrighteousness should be ended without waiting.

Chapter 9

Mencius tells Gong Du that he is compelled to dispute because the world has repeatedly fallen into disorder when sage principles decline. He recounts how Yu controlled the great inundation, how Zhou Gong restored order after the tyrant Zhou, and how Confucius used the Spring and Autumn to condemn rebellious ministers and villainous sons. Mencius opposes Yang Zhu and Mo Di because their doctrines deny the claims of the sovereign or the special affection due to a father. He seeks to rectify men's hearts, defend the doctrines of the former sages, and carry on the work of the three sages.

Chapter 10

Kuang Zhang praises Chen Zhong as a man of true self-denying purity because he endured hunger in Wu Ling and ate worm-eaten plums. Mencius argues that Chen Zhong's standards could only be followed by an earthworm and questions the origins of his house and food. Mencius also recounts that Chen Zhong rejects his brother Dai's wealth and house but accepts food from his wife, showing inconsistency in his principles.
