Abstract written by a model

The records summarize the first eighteen lectures of the Uttaradhyayana Sutra, a teaching on liberation from Samsâra through disciplined ascetic conduct. Its central figures include Mahâvîra, Sudharman, Gautama, Kapila, King Nami, Harikêsa-Bala, Kitra, Brahmadatta, Bhrigu, Sañgaya, and other renouncers. The work begins by defining the obedient, truthful, restrained houseless monk and the hardships a wandering mendicant must patiently conquer. It presents rare human birth, instruction, belief, and self-control as requisites for ending Karman, while warning that carelessness, passion, greed, false asceticism, and attachment produce suffering, hell, and rebirth. It contrasts ignorant pursuit of pleasure with the wise acceptance of death, compassion, renunciation, penance, and careful almsgiving. Parables and teachings show that self-conquest exceeds worldly victory and that true religion rejects violence, possessions, sensuality, and merely external rites. Narrative examples then dramatize these lessons: Nami refuses restored kingship; Gautama abandons attachments; Harikêsa-Bala teaches that restraint and purity are the true sacrifice; Kitra reaches perfection while Brahmadatta falls into hell; and Bhrigu’s household and King Ishukâra renounce domestic life and attain perfection. The later lectures prescribe communal monastic discipline, perfect celibacy, and avoidance of careless or exploitative conduct, condemning the bad Sramana while praising the true monk. The sequence closes with King Sañgaya’s conversion after killing a deer, affirming that royal renunciation and righteous conduct lead beyond transient worldly ties toward heaven and perfection.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

18 sections; each heading opens the text

Uttaradhyayana Sutra / First Lecture.

The First Lecture of the Uttaradhyayana Sutra (Uttarâdhyayana Sûtra) concerns discipline for a houseless monk who has abandoned worldly ties. It explains how a pupil should obey and respect the teacher, accept instruction, speak truthfully, control anger, and avoid evil conduct. It also describes proper behavior in meditation, travel, eating, and collecting alms. A disciplined and learned monk gains knowledge, removes doubts, keeps the five vows, and may become an eternal Siddha or a powerful god.

Second Lecture.

Sudharman recounts a Discourse from the Venerable Ascetic Mahâvîra on twenty-two troubles that a monk must learn, know, bear, and conquer while living as a wandering mendicant. The troubles include bodily hardships, social mistreatment, deprivation, illness, ignorance, and doubts about righteousness and future existence. The unit explains how a monk should endure each trouble without anger, attachment, forbidden acts, or abandoning the Law.

Third Lecture.

The unit concerns the Four Requisites: human birth, instruction in the Law, belief in it, and energy in self-control. It describes living beings wandering through Samsâra in various births according to their actions, suffering until cessation of Karman may lead them to human birth. Those who obtain the four requisites can practice penances and self-control, shake off sinfulness, and reach the highest Nirvâna as an eternal Siddha. It also describes Yakshas dwelling in the upper Kalpa heavens before descending and being born as men.

Fourth Lecture.

The Fourth Lecture, titled Impurity, concerns the dangers of carelessness and the consequences of passions and actions. It teaches that human life is limited, wealth cannot protect the careless, and people cannot escape the effects of their own actions. It instructs a monk to remain watchful, conquer the will, avoid delusion and greed, and seek liberation through disciplined conduct. It also contrasts the impurity of heretics with the virtues a person should desire throughout life.

Fifth Lecture.

The unit contrasts death against one's will, suffered by ignorant men repeatedly, with death with one's will, attained by wise men at most once. It describes how attachment to pleasures leads ignorant men to cruelty, sin, fear, remorse, hell, and rebirth according to their deeds. It teaches that virtuous monks and householders who practise self-control, penance, and the Law can meet death calmly and attain heavenly regions or liberation. A wise man chooses the better death through the Law of Compassion and prepares for the dissolution of the body without fear or joy.

Sixth Lecture.

The False Ascetic concerns the suffering caused by ignorance of the Truth and the conduct required to escape bondage and birth in Samsâra. It teaches that a wise man should seek the truth, show kindness to all creatures, renounce possessions and attachments, avoid accepting presents, and sustain his body only to annihilate Karman. Merely attending a teacher or speaking about liberation without abstaining from sins and acting accordingly does not bring salvation. Arhat Gñâtriputra teaches that an ascetic should wander carefully, without a fixed residence or stored provisions, receiving permitted food in his alms-bowl while awaiting death.

Seventh Lecture.

The Seventh Lecture presents the Parable of the Ram and compares a ram raised for a guest with an ignorant great sinner who pursues pleasures and commits sins. It teaches that human life is capital, while gain represents heaven and loss of capital leads to hell or rebirth as a brute animal. The unit contrasts human pleasures with divine pleasures and the paths of the sinner and the virtuous man under the Law. It concludes that renouncing pleasure and following the true Law leads toward a godly state.

Eighth Lecture.

This unit presents Kapila's teaching on escaping sorrow and misery in unstable, eternal Samsâra. It instructs a monk to abandon attachment, hatred, pleasures, and killing of living beings, while practicing careful conduct, disciplined almsgiving, meditation, and ascetic practices. It warns that ignorance, lust, worldly desires, and false religious claims lead to sin, rebirth, hell, or birth as Asuras. Those who follow the Law taught by Kapila will be saved and gain both worlds.

Ninth Lecture.

King Nami remembers his former birth, becomes a Svayamsambuddha in the true Law, and retires from the world after placing his son on the throne. When Mithilâ is filled with uproar, Sakra tests Nami by urging him to resume royal duties, pleasures, wealth, punishment, conquest, and sacrifices. Nami rejects these pursuits, praises self-control and austerity, and teaches that conquering oneself is greater than conquering others. Sakra reveals himself, praises Nami's virtues, and Nami takes upon him Sramanahood.

Tenth Lecture.

The unit concerns Gautama and the soul's perilous passage through embodied existence and Samsâra. It teaches that human life and human birth are brief and rare opportunities, and that souls may remain for vast periods in earth, water, fire, wind, vegetable, animal, divine, or hellish bodies. Gautama is urged to abandon attachments, wealth, wife, friends, relations, and carelessness, and to follow the best Law and the great path. The Buddha's well-delivered sermon leads Gautama to cut off love and hatred and reach perfection, the world of safety and perfect happiness.

Eleventh Lecture.

The Eleventh Lecture explains the right discipline of a houseless monk who has abandoned worldly ties. It identifies causes that make wholesome discipline impossible and qualities that make discipline a virtue. It distinguishes ill-behaved monks from well-behaved monks and praises the superiority, learning, and virtues of a very learned monk. It urges the seeker after the highest truth to study sacred lore so that self and others may attain perfection.

Twelfth Lecture

Harikêsa-Bala, a monk and sage born in a family of Svapâkas, is assaulted while begging at a Brahmanical sacrifice by young men acting on the Brahmanas’ orders. Bhadrâ identifies him as the ascetic who had refused her after king Kausalika gave her to him, and Yakshas protect him from further harm. The Brahmanas and their followers repent, offer Harikêsa food and drink, and learn from him that true sacrifice consists in self-restraint, non-injury, renunciation, and purity rather than external rites. Gods honor the event and proclaim that penance surpasses birth and caste.

Thirteenth Lecture.

This unit concerns Sambhûta, later born as Brahmadatta, and Kitra, who meet again in Kâmpilya and recall their former births and the rewards of their actions. Kitra urges Brahmadatta, king of the Pañkâlas, to abandon sensual pleasures and enter the order, but Brahmadatta refuses. Brahmadatta later sinks into hell, while Kitra practices self-control and reaches the highest place of perfection.

Fourteenth Lecture.

The unit concerns Ishukâra, its king and queen, the Brahmanical Purôhita Bhrigu, his wife, and their two sons. The two sons reject Vedic study, household duties, wealth, and pleasures, and choose the path of the Ginas. Bhrigu, his wife, King Ishukâra, and Kamalâvatî successively abandon domestic life and enter the monastic order. They understand that birth, old age, death, and worldly pleasures bind beings to Samsâra, and they all eventually reach perfection and the end of misery.

Fifteenth Lecture.

This unit describes the conduct and discipline of a true monk who adopts the Law and lives with other monks. He abandons former connections, desire, worldly gain, divination, medical practices, praise, and dependence on householders. He protects his soul from sins, practises austerities and self-discipline, endures hardships, and eats whatever lowly food and drink are given. The unit concludes that a true monk leaves the house, lives single, subdues his senses, and remains sinless.

Sixteenth Lecture.

The Sixteenth Lecture concerns the ten conditions for the realisation of perfect celibacy among monks. Sudharman recounts that Mahâvîra taught how these conditions lead monks to self-discipline, Samvara, contemplation, guarded senses, and faithful observance of religious duties. The rules prohibit unsuitable lodgings, conversation and proximity with women, sensual attention, excessive or refined food, ornaments, and attachment to pleasant sense objects. The concluding verses praise the chaste monk and affirm the eternal Law proclaimed by the Ginas.

Seventeenth Lecture.

The Seventeenth Lecture concerns The Bad Sramana and the conduct that makes a religious practitioner bad. It describes a Nirgrantha who, after entering the order, learning the Law, receiving discipline, and obtaining Bôdhi, becomes careless, indulgent, disrespectful, or harmful. It lists many behaviors by which a Sramana is called bad, including neglect of inspection, careless walking and sitting, eating improperly, quarrelling, following heretical teachers, and living by fortune-telling. It concludes that a bad monk is despised like poison, while one who avoids these sins is welcomed like nectar and conquers this world and the next.

Eighteenth Lecture.

Sañgaya, king of Kâmpilya, encounters a houseless ascetic after killing a deer and is taught the Law. He abandons kingly power and adopts the faith of the Ginas under Gardabhâli. The unit teaches that worldly ties are transient, sinful actions bring punishment, and righteous conduct leads to heaven and perfection. It also recounts many kings who achieved perfection through royal renunciation and entry into the ascetic order.

Abstract written by a model

These records summarize the later lectures of the Uttarâdhyayana, a religious teaching centered on renunciation, self-control, destruction of Karman, and liberation from Samsâra. It begins with exemplary lives: Balasrî renounces kingship, Samudrapâla becomes a monk after witnessing punishment, Arishtanêmi abandons marriage after seeing animals destined for slaughter, and Râgîmatî and Rathanêmi return to the Law through austerity. King Srênika is converted by a saint’s teaching, while Kêsi and Gautama reconcile the laws of Pârsva and Vardhamâna, with Kêsi accepting the five vows. Gayaghôsha rejects ritual status as the measure of true Brâhmanahood and leads Vigayaghôsha into monastic discipline. The lectures then prescribe monastic practice: Samitis, Guptis, correct daily conduct, care for living beings, confession, meditation, alms-seeking, austerities, and obedience to the Guru. Garga’s comparison of bad bullocks and bad pupils warns against laziness and disobedience. Doctrinal sections define the path as right faith, knowledge, conduct, and austerity; explain righteous exertion, penance, causes of bondage, the chain of delusion, and the eight kinds of Karman. They also describe the six Lêsyâs, whose bad forms lead to misery and whose good forms arise from discipline and lead to happier states. The conclusion describes the houseless monk’s renunciation of possessions and sensuality, divides the world into living and nonliving things, classifies beings from perfected souls to immovable and movable creatures, and presents perfected souls as dwelling at the top of the world. Across the whole work, disciplined renunciation and knowledge culminate in release, beatitude, and freedom from transmigration.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

19 sections; each heading opens the text

Nineteenth Lecture.

Balasrî, also called Mrigâputra, is the crown-prince of Sugrîva who remembers his former birth as a Sramana and renounces royal pleasures. Despite his parents' warnings about the severe duties and suffering of the Sramana order, he receives their permission, gives up his wealth and possessions, and enters the order. He practises the Law through vows, self-control, penance, and meditation, purifies himself, and reaches liberation after many years.

Twentieth Lecture.

King Srênika meets a restrained and concentrated saint at the Mandikukshi Kaitya and learns that worldly wealth and relatives cannot protect a person from suffering. The saint recounts his severe illness in Kausâmbî, his recovery, and his ordination as a houseless monk. He teaches that self-control, careful observance of the great vows, and freedom from sensuality are required to follow the Law of the Nirgranthas. Srênika accepts the teaching, asks forgiveness, and becomes a staunch believer in the Law.

Twenty-First Lecture.

The Twenty-First Lecture concerns Samudrapâla, the son of the merchant and Srâvaka Pâlita, who was born at sea and raised in Kampâ. After seeing a condemned man, Samudrapâla understood the consequences of wicked actions, renounced household life, and became a monk. He followed the Law of monks, endured hardships and calamities, and cultivated restraint and detachment. Having annihilated his Karman, he crossed worldly existence and obtained exemption from transmigration.

Twenty-Second Lecture.

This unit concerns Arishtanêmi, his intended marriage to Râgîmatî, and his renunciation after seeing animals prepared for slaughter. Râgîmatî, hearing of his ordination, also renounces and later resists Rathanêmi when he is tempted to pursue her. Both Rathanêmi and Râgîmatî return to the Law, practise severe austerities, become Kêvalins, and reach the highest perfection. The unit teaches that the enlightened turn away from pleasures and maintain self-control.

Twenty-Third Lecture.

This unit concerns the meeting of Kêsi, disciple of Pârsva, and Gautama, disciple of Vardhamâna, at Srâvastî. They resolve questions about the differences between the Law of Pârsva and the Law of Vardhamâna, including four versus five vows and clothing rules. Gautama explains metaphors for conquering the passions and senses, destroying fetters and love of existence, extinguishing the fire of the passions, controlling the mind, following the right path, and reaching Nirvâna. Kêsi accepts the Law of the five vows, and the assembly praises both teachers.

Twenty-Fourth Lecture.

The Twenty-Fourth Lecture explains the Samitis and Guptis, the eight articles of the creed. It describes five Samitis governing walking, speech, begging, handling necessary articles, and bodily waste, along with three Guptis governing mind, speech, and body. It sets out how a well-disciplined monk should practice these disciplines to avoid harm and sinful conduct. The unit concludes that applying them leads beyond the Circle of Births.

Twenty-Fifth Lecture.

Gayaghôsha, a Brâhmana and houseless sage, comes to Benares and challenges Vigayaghôsha and the assembled priests about the essentials of the Vêdas, sacrifice, heavenly bodies, dharma, and true Brâhmanahood. He teaches that true spiritual status depends on conduct, chastity, knowledge, penance, non-violence, truthfulness, non-attachment, and freedom from Karman rather than outward marks or ritual learning. Vigayaghôsha accepts the teaching, enters the monastic order, and together with Gayaghôsha annihilates Karman through self-control and penance, reaching the highest perfection.

Twenty-Sixth Lecture.

The lecture explains the correct behaviour of monks, called sâmâkârî, as the discipline that frees them from misery and helps them cross Samsâra. It defines the tenfold correct behaviour and prescribes the monks’ duties during the four divisions of the day and night, including study, meditation, begging-tour, inspection, confession, and Kâyôtsarga. It also gives rules for inspecting possessions, protecting living beings, observing the Guru, and performing Kâla-pratikramana and the Pratikramana Sûtra. The unit concludes that many souls have crossed Samsâra by practising this correct behaviour.

Twenty-Seventh Lecture.

The Twenty-Seventh Lecture, titled The Bad Bullocks, concerns the reflections of Garga, a Sthavira and Ganadhara. Garga compares crossing a wilderness by car and crossing the Samsâra through religious exercise, then compares bad bullocks with bad pupils yoked to the car of the Law. The unit describes the unruly behavior of bad bullocks and the lack of zeal, objections, disobedience, and dispersal of bad pupils. Garga ultimately leaves the lazy donkeys and practises severe austerities.

Twenty-Eighth Lecture.

The unit explains the road to final deliverance taught by the Ginas, which consists of right knowledge, faith, conduct, and austerities. It describes five kinds of knowledge, six kinds of substances, the nine truths, and ten causes by which faith is produced. It teaches that right faith, knowledge, conduct, and austerities are mutually connected and lead beings toward beatitude and perfection. Great sages destroy their Karman through control and austerities and proceed to final deliverance.

Twenty-Ninth Lecture.

The Twenty-Ninth Lecture, called The Exertion in Righteousness, records Sudharman’s discourse from the venerable Ascetic Mahâvîra. It explains seventy-three practices and virtues through which the soul develops faith, discipline, knowledge, conduct, renunciation, and control. These practices destroy or prevent Karman, lead the soul beyond Samsâra, and culminate in pure meditation, perfection, enlightenment, deliverance, and final beatitude.

Thirtieth Lecture.

The Thirtieth Lecture, called The Road of Penance, explains how a monk destroys Karman acquired through love and hatred. It describes how abstaining from prescribed actions and possessing specified virtues makes the soul free from Âsravas. It classifies austerities as external and internal, detailing their six kinds and their subdivisions. It teaches that faithfully performing both kinds of austerities leads to release from the Circle of Births.

Thirty-First Lecture.

The unit concerns the mode of life that benefits the soul and enables souls to cross the ocean of Samsâra. It teaches that one should desist from neglect of self-control and practise self-control. A monk avoids love, hatred, harmful acts, calamities, sinful meditations, and many listed causes of bondage, while exerting himself in vows, duties, virtues, and other prescribed points. A clever monk who follows these teachings will soon be thoroughly released from the Circle of Births.

Thirty-Second Lecture.

The unit explains how a Sramana may attain final deliverance from beginningless misery through true knowledge, avoidance of ignorance and delusion, and destruction of love and hatred. It teaches that desire, greed, Karman, and birth and death arise in a chain rooted in delusion, while discipline, seclusion, proper food, study, and meditation support righteousness. The text warns that attachment or aversion to pleasures and sensory objects produces passions, suffering, wrongdoing, and further Karman. A dispassionate person becomes free from these obstructions, knows and sees all things, and ultimately obtains beatitude.

Thirty-Third Lecture.

The lecture explains the Nature of Karman and the eight kinds of Karman by which the soul turns round and round in the Circle of Births. It describes their subdivisions, including the forms of obstruction to right knowledge and right faith, pleasure and pain, delusion, life duration, individuality, Gôtra, and prevention of progress toward eternal bliss. It also states their numbers of atoms, spatial extent, durations, and development. A wise man should learn these subdivisions and strive to prevent and destroy them.

Thirty-Fourth Lecture.

The unit concerns the six Lêsyâs produced by Karman and describes their names, colours, tastes, smells, touches, degrees, character, varieties, duration, results, and life. The black, blue, and grey Lêsyâs arise from wicked and uncontrolled habits, while the red, yellow, and white Lêsyâs arise from increasing discipline, calm, and observance of the Law. Their durations vary across hell, animals, men, and gods, and their degrees have three, nine, twenty-seven, eighty-one, or two hundred and forty-three divisions. The bad Lêsyâs bring souls into miserable courses of life, while the good Lêsyâs bring them into happy courses of life.

Thirty-Fifth Lecture.

This unit concerns the Houseless Monk and the conduct required of a monk seeking liberation. It teaches that taking Pravragyâ requires renouncing attachments, possessions, building houses, cooking, fire, buying and selling, sensual desires, and praise. The monk should live simply, beg for alms without attachment, restrain his senses, and meditate on true things. At death, one who has rejected food, property, egoism, passions, and Âsravas becomes liberated from misery and reaches eternal beatitude.

Thirty-Sixth Lecture.

The unit explains the division of Living Beings and Things without life for a monk who seeks self-control. Living Beings and Things without life make up the world (Lôka), while the Non-world (Alôka) contains only Things without life. Things without life are classified as formless things and things possessing form, and are described by substance, place, time, and development. Their development is considered through colour, smell, taste, touch, and figure.

B. Living Beings.

This unit explains the division of Living Beings into perfected souls (siddhas) and beings still belonging to the Samsâra. It describes perfected souls, their abode at the top of the world, and their qualities, then classifies immovable and movable beings, including Earth Lives, Water Lives, plants, Fire Lives, Wind Lives, animals, men, denizens of hell, and gods. It also gives durations, bodily continuance, varieties, and habitats for many classes. The unit concludes with religious exercises, Bhâvanâs, conditions affecting Bôdhi and rebirth, and the teaching of Gñâtri(putra) in the Thirty-six Lectures of the Uttarâdhyayana.
