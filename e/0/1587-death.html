Abstract written by a model

In The Book of Job, death is portrayed as a quiet, leveling refuge from suffering and as a final, irreversible departure from life. In Ecclesiastes or, The Preacher, death is depicted as the universal event that returns all people to dust and ends earthly labor, memory, and activity. In The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach, or Ecclesiasticus, death is presented as a universal and inevitable decree imposed on all flesh. In Paul’s Letter to the Romans, death is described as the consequence of sin, from which believers are freed through Christ’s death and resurrection. In The Letter to the Hebrews, death is what Jesus tasted for everyone and used to destroy the devil’s power and free humanity from lifelong fear. In Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang), death is the punishment a ruler should impose only after determining that a person deserves it.

death in The Book of Job

author unknown · about 574 BC · the work’s page · read it where death first appears

Abstract written by a model

Death, as spoken of by Job, is a place of quiet and rest that he longs for amid suffering. In Chapter 3 it is pictured as a leveling refuge: the wicked cease troubling, the weary rest, prisoners are at ease, servants are freed from masters, and kings, counselors, and infants who never saw light lie together. The miserable dig for it like hidden treasure. In Chapter 7 the emphasis shifts to finality: like a vanishing cloud, those going down to Sheol come up no more, never return home, and are no longer known, so Job would choose death over his suffering.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 3

Job pictures death as a place of quiet and rest, where he would lie with kings, counselors, and princes, like a hidden untimely birth. There the wicked cease from troubling, the weary rest, prisoners are at ease, the small and great are together, and the servant is free from his master. Job says the miserable long for it and dig for it more than for hidden treasures, rejoicing when they find the grave.

Chapter 7

Death is described as final: like a cloud that is consumed and vanishes, he who goes down to Sheol will come up no more. The dead return no more to their houses and their place knows them no more. Job says the eye that sees him will see him no more, and that he will soon lie down in the dust. He says he would choose death over his bones and suffering.

death in Ecclesiastes or, The Preacher

author unknown · about 299 to 199 BC · the work’s page · read it where death first appears

Abstract written by a model

Death is the one event that befalls everyone alike: wise and fool, righteous and wicked, people and animals. It is an appointed time, returning all to dust. It erases memory, so wisdom confers no lasting advantage, feeding the narrator's hatred of life and despair over labor he must leave behind. After it, all go to Sheol, where there is no work, plan, knowledge or wisdom.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 2

Death comes to the wise man just as to the fool, and the narrator sees that one event happens to them all. Because of it neither is remembered forever, and the narrator asks why he was more wise. It contributes to his hatred of life and his despair over his labor, which he must leave behind.

Chapter 3

Death appears among the appointed times, 'a time to be born, and a time to die.' The narrator says as one dies, so the other dies, for people and animals alike. All go to one place, from dust to dust.

Chapter 9

Death is the one event that comes to all alike, righteous and wicked, clean and unclean. After it people go to the dead, to Sheol, where there is no work, plan, knowledge or wisdom.

death in The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach, or Ecclesiasticus

author unknown · about 179 BC · the work’s page · read it where death first appears

Abstract written by a model

Death is presented as a universal, inevitable decree that will not wait. It is the covenant from the beginning over all flesh, 'You must die!', and the sentence of the Most High, whose good pleasure it is. Generations pass like leaves and works rot with their builders. Death falls on every creature, sevenfold on sinners, and the fear of it troubles everyone from king to poor. Reactions differ: it is bitter in memory to a prosperous man at peace, but acceptable to a needy, failing, very old man. The text concludes it should be accepted rather than feared, since no one asks about life in Hades however long it lasted.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 14

Death will not wait, and the covenant of Hades has not been shown to the listener. All flesh grows old like a garment under the covenant from the beginning, 'You must die!' Generations pass like leaves, and every work rots with its builder; in Hades there is no seeking of luxury.

Chapter 40

Death is among the things that come to all creatures, human and animal, and sevenfold more upon sinners, together with bloodshed, strife, sword, calamities, famine, suffering, and plague. The fear of death is listed among the troubles shared by everyone from king to the poor, and the text says it is better to die than to beg.

Chapter 41

Death is described as bitter in memory to a prosperous man at peace, but acceptable to a needy, failing, extremely old man. It is the sentence from the Lord over all flesh, to be accepted rather than feared, and no one inquires about life in Hades whether life lasted ten, a hundred or a thousand years.

death in Paul’s Letter to the Romans

author unknown · about AD 57 · the work’s page · read it where death first appears

Abstract written by a model

Death is a consequence of sin: it entered through sin, passed to all humanity because all sinned, and reigned from Adam until Moses. Believers who died with Christ are freed from sin; Christ died once and death no longer rules him, while grace brings life through him.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

2 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 5

Death entered through sin and passed to all men because all sinned. It reigned from Adam until Moses, even over those whose sins weren't like Adam's disobedience, and reigned through the one trespass. Those who receive the abundance of grace will reign in life through Jesus Christ.

Chapter 6

Death is the end of the things believers are now ashamed of and the result of serving sin. Christ died once to sin, and death no longer has dominion over him. Believers died with him and are thereby freed from sin, while the wages of sin is death.
