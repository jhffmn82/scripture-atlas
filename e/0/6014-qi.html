Abstract written by a model

In Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang), Qi is described as a small, narrow but strong State and kingdom whose governance and conduct toward Yan are discussed. In Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang), Qi is associated with the people going to fortify Xue and is said not to be the duke's concern compared with doing good. In Mencius, Book II (Gong Sun Chou), Qi is described as a State capable of attaining royal dignity through benevolent government, where Mencius served and traveled. In Mencius, Book III (Teng Wen Gong), Qi is described as attacking Song with hostility. In Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou), Qi is identified as the destination to which Yue Zheng traveled. In Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), the kingdom of Qi is imagined as being offered to Chen Zhong contrary to righteousness. In Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), Qi is identified as the destination of Mencius's journey from Fan, where the king of Qi's son was present.

Qi in Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang)

Mencius · about 299 BC · the work’s page · read it where Qi first appears

Abstract written by a model

Qi is a small, narrow State and kingdom, one of the nine divisions, yet is regarded as strong. It has defined borders and is one of the kingdoms near Teng. Mencius inquires into its prohibitory regulations on arriving there and presents its government under king Wen as an example of true royal government. Qi could approach good government if its king shared his great love of music with the people. It defeats Jin in the east, after which King Hui of Liang’s eldest son perishes. Qi then attacks, conquers, smites, and takes possession of Yan, doubling its territory; but its treatment of Yan and lack of benevolent government lead other princes to plan an attack.

Qi in Mencius, Book II (Gong Sun Chou)

Mencius · about 299 BC · the work’s page · read it where Qi first appears

Abstract written by a model

Qi is a place with territory comparable to the royal domains of flourishing Xia, Yin, and Zhou, and people throughout the State. It needs neither more territory nor population to attain royal dignity; benevolent government could achieve it easily. Mencius lived there as a high dignitary, received no money despite a present, and was associated with advice concerning Qi Wa. Qi’s king sent Wang Huan to Teng. Mencius traveled from Qi to Lu after burying his mother, returned toward Qi, and later took leave of Qi, having remained longer than intended. Qi smote Yan, though Mencius denied advising this and said only the minister of Heaven could smite Yan. If its king used Mencius, happiness could extend from Qi to the whole kingdom.
