Psalms

Abstract written by a model

This document is the Book of Psalms, chapters 1 to 150, a collection of Hebrew prayers, laments, hymns and wisdom poems addressed to the LORD. Many carry headings naming David, the sons of Korah, Asaph, Solomon, Moses, Heman and Ethan, and instructions for the Chief Musician. The Songs of Ascents (120 to 134) form a distinct group. The central figures are the LORD, who is refuge, shepherd, judge and King, and David, who speaks most often, frequently pursued by enemies such as Saul, Absalom and Doeg. Israel, Zion and Jerusalem, and the contrast between the righteous and the wicked, recur throughout. It opens with Psalm 1, which contrasts the blessed man, a tree by streams, with the wicked, who are like chaff. Psalm 2 then presents the LORD's Anointed King set on Zion. The first long stretch (3 to 41) is mostly David's prayer. He laments enemies, confesses sin (32, 51), asks for vindication, and affirms trust. Notable pieces include the creation and law hymn (19), the suffering and praise of 22, the shepherd psalm (23), and the entry of the King of glory (24). Psalms 42 to 89 add Korah and Asaph material. These include longing for God, national laments over defeat and the destroyed sanctuary, a wedding song for the king (45), the refuge psalm (46), Zion celebrations, a prayer for a just king (72), and Asaph's struggle over the wicked's prosperity (73). Psalms 78 and 105 to 106 retell Israel's history, with God's wonders and the people's repeated rebellion, while 88 ends in unrelieved lament and 89 turns from praising David's covenant to complaint. Psalm 90, attributed to Moses, reflects on human brevity. Psalms 91 to 100 stress the LORD's reign, security under him, and calls for all the earth to worship. Psalms 101 to 119 include David's vows, praise of God's mercy and creation (103, 104), the priest-king oracle (110), the long acrostic on God's law (119), and thanksgiving. The Songs of Ascents express trust, joy and longing for Zion, and Psalm 137 mourns exile in Babylon. Psalms 138 to 145 return to David's prayers for deliverance and praise. The book closes with five hymns (146 to 150) summoning all creation, with instruments and every breath, to praise the LORD.

Psalms / Chapter 1

Psalm 1 contrasts the blessed man with the wicked. The blessed man avoids the counsel of the wicked, the path of sinners, and the seat of scoffers, and instead delights in the LORD's law and meditates on it day and night. He is compared to a tree planted by streams of water that bears fruit in season, whose leaf does not wither, and whatever he does prospers. The wicked are compared to chaff driven away by the wind, and they will not stand in the judgment or in the congregation of the righteous. The LORD knows the way of the righteous, while the way of the wicked will perish.

Chapter 2

Chapter 2 opens by asking why the nations and peoples rage and plot in vain. The kings and rulers of the earth take counsel together against the LORD and his Anointed, resolving to break their bonds and cast off their cords. The LORD, sitting in the heavens, laughs at them in derision, then speaks in anger and wrath, declaring that he has set his King on his holy hill of Zion. A speaker relates the LORD's decree that he is his son, who is promised the nations as an inheritance and the ends of the earth as a possession, and who will break them with a rod of iron. The chapter closes by urging the kings and judges to be wise, serve the LORD with fear, and give homage to the Son lest they perish, while blessing all who take refuge in him.

Chapter 3

Chapter 3 is a psalm by David, written when he fled from his son Absalom. David laments that his adversaries have multiplied and that many say there is no help for him in God. He affirms that the LORD is his shield, his glory, and the one who lifts up his head, and that the LORD answers his cry from his holy hill. David says he lay down, slept, and woke because the LORD sustains him, so he will not fear tens of thousands set against him. He asks the LORD to arise and save him, recalls that the LORD has struck his enemies and broken the teeth of the wicked, and closes by declaring that salvation belongs to the LORD and asking for blessing on his people.

Chapter 4

Chapter 4 is a psalm by David, addressed to the Chief Musician and set for stringed instruments. David calls on God, the God of his righteousness, to answer him, relieve his distress, and show mercy. He then turns to the sons of men, asking how long his glory will be turned into dishonor and why they love vanity and seek falsehood. He affirms that the LORD has set apart the godly and hears when he calls, and he urges his hearers to stand in awe, not sin, search their hearts, offer sacrifices of righteousness, and trust the LORD. The psalm ends with David saying the LORD has put gladness in his heart and that he will lie down and sleep in peace, since the LORD alone makes him live in safety.

Chapter 5

Chapter 5 is a psalm by David, addressed to the Chief Musician with the flutes, in which the psalmist prays to the LORD, his King and God, asking to be heard in the morning. He affirms that the LORD does not delight in wickedness and will destroy liars, the arrogant, the bloodthirsty and the deceitful. The psalmist says he will enter God's house through God's loving kindness and bow toward the holy temple. He asks the LORD to lead him in righteousness and make his way straight because of his enemies, whom he describes as unfaithful, destructive and flattering. He asks God to hold them guilty and thrust them out, and he closes by saying those who take refuge in God will rejoice and the righteous will be surrounded with favor like a shield.

Chapter 6

Chapter 6 is a psalm by David, addressed to the LORD and written for the Chief Musician on stringed instruments, upon the eight-stringed lyre. David pleads not to be rebuked or disciplined in the LORD's anger, asking for mercy and healing because he is faint, his bones are troubled, and his soul is in great anguish. He appeals to the LORD to return, deliver him, and save him for the sake of his loving kindness, noting that in death and Sheol there is no memory of or thanks to the LORD. David describes his weariness, nightly weeping, and eyes wasted by grief because of his adversaries. He then turns to address the workers of iniquity, declaring that the LORD has heard his weeping and accepted his prayer, and he asks that his enemies be ashamed, dismayed, and suddenly disgraced.

Chapter 7

Chapter 7 is a meditation sung by David to the LORD concerning the words of Cush, the Benjamite. David takes refuge in the LORD, asks to be saved from pursuers who would tear his soul apart like a lion, and swears that if he has done wrong or repaid evil to one at peace with him, the enemy may overtake him and lay his glory in the dust. He calls on the LORD to arise in anger and judge him according to his righteousness and integrity, and asks that the wicked come to an end while the righteous are established. He describes God as a righteous judge who has indignation every day and who prepares weapons against the man who does not repent. That man's own mischief and violence fall back on his own head, and David closes by promising thanks and praise to the LORD Most High.

Chapter 8

Chapter 8 is a psalm by David, addressed to the Chief Musician and set for an instrument of Gath. It opens and closes by praising the LORD, whose name is majestic in all the earth and whose glory is set above the heavens. The psalmist says the LORD has established strength from the lips of babes and infants to silence the enemy and the avenger. Considering the heavens, the moon and the stars, the psalmist wonders why the LORD thinks of and cares for man. The psalm answers that man has been made a little lower than the angels, crowned with glory and honor, and made ruler over sheep, cattle, animals of the field, birds, and fish.

Chapter 9

Chapter 9 is a psalm by David, set to "The Death of the Son" and addressed to the Chief Musician, in which he thanks the LORD with his whole heart and tells of his marvelous works. David praises the LORD for upholding his just cause, rebuking the nations, destroying the wicked, and reigning forever on a throne of judgment. The LORD is described as a high tower for the oppressed who does not forsake those who seek him or forget the cry of the afflicted. David asks for mercy and to be lifted from the gates of death so he can declare praise in the gates of the daughter of Zion. The psalm ends with the observation that the nations fall into their own traps, and a plea that the LORD arise, judge the nations, and make them know they are only men.

Chapter 10

In Chapter 10, the speaker asks the LORD why he stands far off and hides during times of trouble, while describing how the wicked arrogantly hunt the weak. The wicked man boasts of his cravings, ignores God, speaks curses and deceit, and ambushes and crushes the helpless like a lion catching prey, believing God has forgotten and will never see. The speaker then calls on the LORD to arise, lift up his hand, and not forget the helpless. The speaker affirms that the LORD sees trouble and grief, helps the victim and the fatherless, and is King forever. The chapter closes with the hope that the LORD will hear the desire of the humble, strengthen their heart, break the arm of the wicked, and judge the fatherless and oppressed so that man of the earth terrifies no more.

Chapter 11

Chapter 11, attributed to David and addressed to the Chief Musician, is a declaration of trust in the LORD. David states that he takes refuge in the LORD and questions those who urge his soul to flee like a bird to a mountain. He describes the wicked bending their bows to shoot in darkness at the upright in heart, and asks what the righteous can do if the foundations are destroyed. The psalm answers that the LORD is in his holy temple and on his throne in heaven, observing and examining people. The LORD examines the righteous, hates the wicked and the lover of violence, will rain blazing coals, fire, sulfur, and scorching wind on the wicked, and, being righteous, will let the upright see his face.

Chapter 12

Chapter 12 is a psalm of David, addressed to the Chief Musician and set for an eight-stringed lyre. The speaker cries out to the LORD for help because the godly and faithful are vanishing, while everyone lies to neighbors with flattering lips and a double heart. The speaker asks the LORD to cut off flattering lips and boasting tongues, which claim that their own lips make them lord over themselves. The LORD answers that, because of the oppression of the weak and the groaning of the needy, he will arise and set them in safety from those who malign them. The psalm closes by praising the LORD's flawless words, trusting him to preserve them, and observing that the wicked walk on every side while what is vile is exalted.

Chapter 13

Chapter 13 is a psalm attributed to David and addressed to the Chief Musician. David laments that the LORD seems to have forgotten him and hidden his face, and he asks how long he must carry sorrow in his heart each day. He asks how long his enemy will triumph over him. He pleads with the LORD, his God, to look, answer, and give light to his eyes so he does not sleep in death, and so his enemy and adversaries do not say they have prevailed or rejoice at his fall. The psalm ends with David declaring trust in the LORD's loving kindness, rejoicing in his salvation, and vowing to sing to the LORD because he has been good to him.

Chapter 14

Chapter 14, a piece for the Chief Musician by David, opens by describing the fool who says in his heart that there is no God, and declares that people are corrupt and no one does good. The LORD looks down from heaven on the children of men to see whether any understand and seek God, and finds that all have gone aside and become corrupt. The text asks whether the workers of iniquity have no knowledge, since they devour God's people like bread and do not call on the LORD. It says they are in great fear because God is in the generation of the righteous, and that they frustrate the plan of the poor, whose refuge is the LORD. The chapter closes with a longing for the salvation of Israel to come out of Zion, so that when the LORD restores his people's fortunes, Jacob and Israel will rejoice.

Chapter 15

Chapter 15 is a psalm of David that asks the LORD who may dwell in his sanctuary and live on his holy hill. The answer describes a person who walks blamelessly, does what is right, and speaks truth in his heart. This person does not slander, wrong his friend, or cast slurs against his fellow man. He despises a vile man, honors those who fear the LORD, and keeps an oath even when it hurts. He does not lend money for usury or take a bribe against the innocent, and the psalm promises that one who does these things will never be shaken.

Chapter 16

Chapter 16 is a poem attributed to David in which he asks God to preserve him because he takes refuge in the LORD. David declares that the LORD is his Lord and his only good thing, and he delights in the saints on the earth. He rejects those who give gifts to another god, refusing to offer their blood drink offerings or speak their names. He praises the LORD for assigning his portion, cup, and lot, which have fallen in pleasant places, and for giving him counsel. He expresses confidence that the LORD at his right hand will keep him from being moved, that his soul will not be left in Sheol, and that the LORD will show him the path of life and fullness of joy.

Chapter 17

In Chapter 17, labeled a prayer by David, David appeals to the LORD to hear his righteous plea and to vindicate him, claiming his heart has been tested and found faithful and that he has kept from the ways of the violent. He asks the LORD to show loving kindness, keep him as the apple of his eye, and hide him under the shadow of his wings. He describes wicked enemies who surround him, are proud and callous, and are likened to a lion lurking in secret places. He calls on the LORD to arise, confront and cast down the enemy, and deliver him by sword and hand from men of the world whose portion is in this life. He ends confident that he will see the LORD's face in righteousness and be satisfied when he awakes.

Chapter 18

Chapter 18 is a song by David, the servant of the LORD, spoken on the day the LORD delivered him from all his enemies and from Saul. David praises the LORD as his rock, fortress, and shield, and recounts how the LORD heard his cry from the cords of death and answered with a storm-like descent of shaking earth, fire, darkness, hail, and lightning. The LORD drew David out of many waters and brought him to a large place, which David links to his righteousness and clean hands. David then describes being armed by God for battle, defeating and crushing his enemies, and being made head of the nations. He closes by blessing the living LORD, vowing to give thanks among the nations, and declaring that the LORD gives great deliverance to his king, to David and his offspring forever.

Chapter 19

Chapter 19 is a psalm of David for the Chief Musician. It first describes how the heavens and expanse declare God's glory and knowledge day and night, with their voice reaching all the earth, and how God set a tent in them for the sun, which runs its course like a bridegroom or strong man with nothing hidden from its heat. It then praises the LORD's law, covenant, precepts, commandment, fear, and ordinances as perfect, sure, right, pure, clean, and true, more desirable than gold and sweeter than honey. Finally the psalmist says he is warned by them, asks to be forgiven hidden errors and kept from presumptuous sins, and prays that his words and meditation be acceptable to the LORD, his rock and redeemer.

Chapter 20

Chapter 20 is a psalm for the Chief Musician by David in which the speakers pray for a king in a day of trouble. They ask the LORD to answer him, send help from the sanctuary and Zion, remember his offerings, and grant his heart's desire and requests. The speakers declare they will triumph and set up banners in the name of their God. They affirm that the LORD saves his anointed and answers him from heaven with the strength of his right hand. They contrast those who trust in chariots and horses, who fall, with themselves, who trust in the LORD's name and stand upright, and they close by calling on the LORD to save and the King to answer.

Chapter 21

Chapter 21 is a psalm of David for the Chief Musician that celebrates the king's joy in the LORD's strength and salvation. It recounts how the LORD granted the king's heart's desire, crowned him with fine gold, gave him long life, and laid honor and majesty on him. The king trusts in the LORD and, through the loving kindness of the Most High, will not be moved. The psalm then turns to the LORD's enemies, whom his hand will find out and consume like a fiery furnace, destroying their descendants because they plotted evil that cannot succeed. It closes with a call for the LORD to be exalted in his strength so that the people will sing and praise his power.

Chapter 22

In Psalm 22, a psalmist identified as David cries out to God, asking why he has been forsaken and noting that God does not answer his groaning. He recalls that Israel's fathers trusted God and were delivered, while he himself is a despised worm mocked by onlookers who taunt that the LORD should rescue him. He describes his suffering through images of bulls, lions and dogs, bones out of joint, pierced hands and feet, and enemies dividing his garments, and he pleads for deliverance. The psalm then turns to praise: he vows to declare God's name in the assembly, and calls those who fear the LORD to glorify him because he has heard the afflicted. It closes by declaring that the ends of the earth and the nations will turn to and worship the LORD, whose kingdom rules over the nations, and that future generations will be told of his righteousness.

Chapter 23

Chapter 23 is a psalm attributed to David in which the speaker describes the LORD as his shepherd. The speaker says he lacks nothing, and describes being made to lie down in green pastures, led beside still waters, and guided in paths of righteousness. He states he will fear no evil even while walking through the valley of the shadow of death, because the LORD is with him and his rod and staff comfort him. The LORD prepares a table for him in the presence of his enemies, anoints his head with oil, and fills his cup to overflowing. The speaker concludes that goodness and loving kindness will follow him all his life and that he will dwell in the LORD's house forever.

Chapter 24

Chapter 24 is a psalm by David declaring that the earth and everything in it belong to the LORD, who founded it on the seas and floods. It asks who may ascend the LORD's hill and stand in his holy place, and answers that it is the one with clean hands and a pure heart who has not lifted his soul to falsehood or sworn deceitfully. That person will receive a blessing and righteousness from the God of his salvation, and is identified with the generation of those who seek God's face, even Jacob. The psalm then calls on the gates and everlasting doors to lift up their heads so the King of glory may come in. The King of glory is identified as the LORD strong and mighty, mighty in battle, the LORD of Armies.

Chapter 25

Chapter 25 is a prayer attributed to David, who lifts his soul to the LORD and asks for guidance, mercy, and deliverance. He asks not to be shamed or triumphed over by his enemies, and begs the LORD to forget the sins of his youth and to pardon his great iniquity. He describes the LORD as good and upright, one who instructs sinners and the humble and shows loving kindness and truth to those who keep his covenant. He says the LORD's friendship is with those who fear him, and that such people will dwell at ease with offspring who inherit the land. David describes himself as desolate, afflicted, and surrounded by many enemies who hate him cruelly, and the chapter ends with a plea for the LORD to redeem Israel from all his troubles.

Chapter 26

In Chapter 26, attributed to David, he asks the LORD to judge and examine him, claiming he has walked in integrity and trusted the LORD without wavering. He says the LORD's loving kindness and truth guide him, and that he has avoided deceitful men, hypocrites, and the assembly of evildoers. He vows to wash his hands in innocence, go about the LORD's altar, and proclaim thanksgiving and the LORD's wondrous deeds. He expresses love for the LORD's house, where the LORD's glory dwells, and asks not to be gathered with sinners and bloodthirsty men who are full of wickedness and bribes. He resolves to keep walking in integrity, asks for redemption and mercy, and says he stands on even ground and will bless the LORD in the congregations.

Chapter 27

In Chapter 27, attributed to David, the speaker declares the LORD to be his light, salvation, and the strength of his life, so that he fears no one. He recalls that evildoers who attacked him stumbled and fell, and says he would stay confident even if an army or war came against him. He asks to dwell in the LORD's house all his days, expects to be hidden and lifted up on a rock in trouble, and promises sacrifices and songs of joy. He then pleads for mercy, guidance, and protection from adversaries and false witnesses, and states that even if his father and mother forsake him the LORD will take him up. The chapter ends with his confidence that he will see the LORD's goodness in the land of the living and an exhortation to wait for the LORD and be strong.

Chapter 28

In this chapter, attributed to David, he calls on the LORD, his rock, asking not to be met with silence lest he become like those who go down into the pit. He lifts his hands toward the LORD's Most Holy Place and pleads not to be drawn away with the wicked, who speak peace to neighbors while holding mischief in their hearts. He asks that they be repaid according to their works, because they do not respect the works of the LORD. The tone then turns to praise as David blesses the LORD for hearing his petitions, declaring the LORD his strength and shield. The chapter closes with a prayer that the LORD save his people, bless his inheritance, shepherd them, and bear them up forever.

Chapter 29

Chapter 29 is a psalm by David that calls the sons of the mighty to ascribe glory and strength to the LORD and to worship him in holy array. It then describes the LORD's voice on the waters, powerful and full of majesty, breaking the cedars of Lebanon, making Lebanon and Sirion skip like a calf and a young wild ox, striking with flashes of lightning, and shaking the wilderness of Kadesh. The voice also makes the deer calve and strips the forests bare, while in his temple everything says, "Glory!" The psalm closes by stating that the LORD sat enthroned at the Flood and sits as King forever, and that he will give strength and bless his people with peace.

Chapter 30

Chapter 30 is a psalm attributed to David, headed as a song for the dedication of the temple. The speaker praises the LORD for raising him up, healing him, and bringing his soul up from Sheol so that he did not go down to the pit. He calls the LORD's saints to sing and give thanks, noting that the LORD's anger lasts a moment while his favor lasts a lifetime. The speaker recalls saying in his prosperity that he would never be moved, then being troubled when the LORD hid his face, and crying out for mercy and help. The LORD turned his mourning into dancing and clothed him with gladness, so that he vows to give thanks forever.

Chapter 31

Psalm 31, a psalm by David for the Chief Musician, is a prayer in which David takes refuge in the LORD and asks to be delivered from enemies who plot against him. He describes his distress, with wasting grief, sorrow, and sighing, and the contempt and slander of his neighbors and adversaries. He declares his trust in the LORD, saying his times are in the LORD's hand, and asks that the wicked and lying lips be silenced. He praises the LORD's goodness stored up for those who fear and take refuge in him, admits he once said in haste that he was cut off, and notes that the LORD heard his cry. The psalm closes by urging the saints to love the LORD and all who hope in him to be strong.

Chapter 32

Chapter 32 is a contemplative psalm attributed to David about the blessing of forgiven sin. David describes how keeping silent about his sin made his bones waste away under the heavy hand of the LORD, until he acknowledged his transgressions and the LORD forgave him. He urges the godly to pray to the LORD while he may be found, and declares the LORD to be his hiding place who surrounds him with songs of deliverance. The LORD then promises to instruct and counsel, warning against being like a horse or mule that needs bit and bridle. The psalm closes by contrasting the many sorrows of the wicked with the loving kindness surrounding those who trust the LORD, and calls the righteous to rejoice.

Chapter 33

Chapter 33 calls the righteous and upright to rejoice in the LORD, thank him with the lyre and ten-stringed harp, and sing him a new song. It praises the LORD's right word, faithful work, and love of righteousness and justice, and says the heavens were made by his word and the sea's waters gathered by him. All the earth is told to fear him, since he spoke and it was done. The LORD overturns the counsel of the nations while his own counsel stands forever, and he sees all humankind from heaven, having fashioned their hearts. Kings and horses cannot save by army or strength, but the LORD's eye is on those who fear and hope in him, delivering them from death and famine, and the people close by declaring trust in him as their help and shield and asking for his loving kindness.

Chapter 34

Chapter 34 is a psalm attributed to David, written for the occasion when he pretended to be insane before Abimelech, who drove him away. David vows to bless the LORD at all times, tells how he sought the LORD and was delivered from his fears, and invites the humble to magnify the LORD with him. He then teaches children the fear of the LORD, urging them to keep their tongues from evil, depart from evil, do good, and pursue peace. The psalm contrasts the righteous, whom the LORD hears, delivers, and protects, with those who do evil, whose memory the LORD will cut off. It closes with the assurance that the LORD redeems his servants and that none who take refuge in him will be condemned.

Chapter 35

Chapter 35, attributed to David, is a prayer for deliverance from enemies who pursue him without cause. David asks the LORD to fight for him, to shame and drive back his pursuers with the LORD's angel, and to let their hidden nets and pits catch them instead. He recounts that he mourned and fasted for these people when they were sick, yet they rejoiced at his adversity, gathered against him, and slandered him with false witness. He appeals to the LORD to stop looking on, wake up, and vindicate him according to his righteousness. He vows to give thanks in the great assembly and to speak of the LORD's righteousness and praise all day.

Chapter 36

Chapter 36 is a psalm by David, the servant of the LORD, addressed to the Chief Musician. David first describes the wicked man, who has no fear of God, flatters himself, speaks iniquity and deceit, plots evil on his bed, and does not abhor evil. He then turns to praise the LORD, whose loving kindness reaches the heavens, whose faithfulness reaches the skies, whose righteousness is like the mountains of God, and whose judgments are like a great deep. The children of men take refuge under the LORD's wings and are satisfied from his house, for with him is the spring of life. David asks the LORD to continue his loving kindness to those who know him and to keep the foot of pride and the hand of the wicked from him, and he notes that the workers of iniquity have fallen and cannot rise.

Chapter 37

Chapter 37, attributed to David, urges the reader not to fret or envy evildoers who seem to prosper, because they will soon be cut down like grass. It calls the righteous to trust in the LORD, delight in him, commit their way to him, wait patiently, give up anger, and do good. The chapter repeatedly promises that the humble and righteous will inherit the land while the wicked are cut off, their weapons turned against themselves. David says that in his long life he has never seen the righteous forsaken or their children begging for bread. It closes by affirming that the LORD upholds, helps and rescues those who take refuge in him, while transgressors are destroyed.

Chapter 38

Chapter 38 is a psalm of David, written for a memorial, in which the speaker pleads with the LORD not to rebuke or chasten him in wrath. He describes severe suffering in his body and spirit, which he links to his sin and iniquity, and says friends and kinsmen stand far from him while enemies lay snares and speak deceit. He remains silent like a deaf and mute man, yet hopes in the LORD to answer him. He declares he will confess his iniquity and be sorry for his sin, notes that his enemies are many and vigorous, and closes by asking the LORD not to forsake him and to hurry to help him.

Chapter 39

Chapter 39 is a psalm by David, written for the Chief Musician and Jeduthun, in which David describes resolving to guard his tongue and stay silent before the wicked. His silence deepens his sorrow until his heart burns and he speaks to the LORD. He asks to know the measure of his days and confesses that life is brief, that every man is like a breath and a shadow, and that people busy themselves in vain, heaping up wealth without knowing who will gather it. He places his hope in the LORD, asks for deliverance from his transgressions and for the LORD's scourge to be removed, and acknowledges the LORD's rebuke. He closes by pleading for his prayer to be heard and for mercy, calling himself a stranger and foreigner with the LORD, as all his fathers were, so that he may recover strength before he departs and exists no more.

Chapter 40

In Psalm 40, a psalm of David for the Chief Musician, the speaker recounts waiting patiently for the LORD, who heard his cry, lifted him out of a horrible pit and miry clay, set his feet on a rock, and put a new song of praise in his mouth. He declares the blessedness of trusting the LORD and marvels that the LORD's wonderful works and thoughts toward us are too many to count. He says the LORD desired no sacrifice or offering but opened his ears, and he affirms that he delights to do God's will and has proclaimed the LORD's righteousness, faithfulness, salvation, loving kindness and truth in the great assembly. Yet he is surrounded by innumerable evils and overtaken by his own iniquities, so he pleads for deliverance and asks that those who seek his soul and mock him be shamed, while those who seek the LORD rejoice. He closes by calling himself poor and needy and asking his God not to delay.

Chapter 41

Chapter 41 is a psalm attributed to David for the Chief Musician. It opens by declaring blessed the one who considers the poor, whom the LORD will deliver, preserve, and sustain on his sickbed. David then asks the LORD for mercy and healing, confessing that he has sinned. His enemies speak evil of him, wishing for his death, while visitors speak falsehood and whisper that an evil disease has afflicted him, and even his own familiar friend has lifted his heel against him. David asks the LORD to raise him up, takes his enemy's failure to triumph as a sign of the LORD's delight, and closes with a blessing of the LORD, the God of Israel.

Chapter 42

Psalm 42, a contemplation by the sons of Korah for the Chief Musician, is the lament of a speaker whose soul thirsts for the living God like a deer panting for water. The psalmist remembers leading crowds to God's house with joy and praise, and now weeps while adversaries taunt, "Where is your God?" From the land of the Jordan, the heights of Hermon, and the hill Mizar, the psalmist feels swept over by waves and billows and asks God, the rock, why he has been forgotten. Twice the psalmist addresses his own despairing soul, urging it to hope in God, for he will still praise him. The LORD is said to command his loving kindness by day, with his song accompanying the psalmist at night.

Chapter 43

In Chapter 43, the speaker appeals to God to vindicate him and plead his cause against an ungodly nation and against deceitful and wicked men. He calls God the God of his strength, yet asks why God has rejected him and why he must mourn under the enemy's oppression. He asks God to send out his light and truth to lead him to God's holy hill and tents. He says he will then go to the altar and praise God on the harp. The chapter ends with the speaker questioning his own despair and telling his soul to hope in God, his Savior, helper, and God.

Chapter 44

Chapter 44 is a contemplative psalm by the sons of Korah, addressed to the Chief Musician. The speakers recall how God, by his own hand and not by their ancestors' sword, drove out nations and planted his people in the land, and they affirm that they trust God rather than their bows. They then lament that God has now rejected them, no longer goes out with their armies, scattered them like sheep for slaughter, and made them a byword and reproach among the nations. They protest that they have not forgotten God or broken his covenant, and they say God knows the secrets of the heart. The psalm ends with an urgent plea for God to wake, rise up, help, and redeem them for the sake of his loving kindness.

Chapter 45

Chapter 45 is a wedding song, set to "The Lilies," written as a contemplation by the sons of Korah for the Chief Musician. The speaker, whose heart overflows with a noble theme, recites verses for the king, praising him as the most excellent of men, blessed forever by God, and urging him to ride out victoriously for truth, humility, and righteousness. The king's throne is addressed as everlasting, and God, his God, has anointed him with the oil of gladness above his fellows because he loved righteousness and hated wickedness. The queen stands at his right hand in gold of Ophir and is told to forget her people and her father's house and to honor the king as her lord. The princess is led to the king in embroidered, gold-woven clothing with her virgin companions, and the speaker promises the king's sons will become princes in all the earth and that his name will be remembered in all generations.

Chapter 46

Chapter 46 is a psalm for the Chief Musician by the sons of Korah, set according to Alamoth. It declares that God is a refuge, strength, and ever-present help, so that his people will not fear even when the earth shakes and the seas roar. It describes a river that gladdens the city of God, where God dwells and helps at dawn, so that the city will not be moved. The nations rage and kingdoms are shaken, but God lifts his voice and the earth melts. The psalm calls people to see God's works, who ends wars, breaks the bow and spear, and burns the chariots, and records God's command to be still and know that he will be exalted among the nations and on the earth. The refrain that the LORD of Armies, the God of Jacob, is with us and is our refuge closes the psalm.

Chapter 47

Chapter 47 is a psalm by the sons of Korah, written for the Chief Musician, that calls all nations to clap and shout to God in triumph. It proclaims the LORD Most High as an awesome, great King over all the earth who subdues nations and chooses Jacob's inheritance. God is described as having gone up with a shout and the sound of a trumpet. The psalm repeatedly commands singing praises, urging that this be done with understanding. It ends with God reigning over the nations on his holy throne, the princes of the peoples gathered with the people of the God of Abraham, and the shields of the earth belonging to God, who is greatly exalted.

Chapter 48

Chapter 48 is a song, a psalm by the sons of Korah, praising the LORD and his city on Mount Zion. It describes Zion as beautiful, the joy of the whole earth, and the city of the great King, where God has shown himself as a refuge. Kings assembled and passed by together, but on seeing the city they were amazed, dismayed, and trembling, and hurried away. The people reflect on God's loving kindness in his temple, and the daughters of Judah are called to rejoice at his judgments. The psalm closes by urging people to walk around Zion, count its towers, and tell the next generation that this God is their God forever and will guide them even to death.

Chapter 49

Chapter 49 is a psalm by the sons of Korah, addressed to all peoples of the world, in which the psalmist speaks wisdom and a riddle on the harp. The psalmist asks why he should fear evil days, and he turns to those who trust in their wealth. No one can redeem another or pay God a ransom, because the price of life is too costly and wise and foolish alike die and leave their wealth to others. The rich and foolish are likened to animals that perish, appointed as a flock for Sheol with Death as their shepherd. The psalmist expects God to redeem his soul from Sheol and receive him, and urges listeners not to fear the rich, since a man carries nothing away at death and goes to the generation of his fathers.

Chapter 50

Psalm 50, a psalm of Asaph, depicts God, the Mighty One, speaking from Zion and summoning heaven and earth to witness as he judges his people. He addresses Israel, saying he does not rebuke their sacrifices but has no need of them, since every animal and the whole world already belong to him. He calls them instead to offer the sacrifice of thanksgiving, pay their vows to the Most High, and call on him in trouble. He then turns to the wicked, who recite his statutes while hating instruction, consenting to thieves, joining adulterers, and practicing deceit and slander against their own kin. God warns that his past silence was misread as likeness to them, and that he will rebuke and tear apart those who forget him, while the one who offers thanksgiving glorifies him and will see his salvation.

Chapter 51

Chapter 51 is a psalm of David, written when Nathan the prophet came to him after he had gone in to Bathsheba. David pleads with God for mercy, asking to be washed and cleansed from his transgressions, which he acknowledges are constantly before him. He confesses that he has sinned against God alone, asks for a clean heart and a right spirit, and begs not to be cast from God's presence or have the Holy Spirit taken from him. He vows to teach transgressors God's ways and to praise him once restored, and states that God desires a broken and contrite heart rather than sacrifice. The psalm closes with a prayer for Zion and Jerusalem, after which righteous sacrifices and bulls on the altar will please God.

Chapter 52

Chapter 52 is a contemplation by David, composed when Doeg the Edomite told Saul that David had come to Ahimelech's house. David addresses a boastful 'mighty man' whose deceitful tongue plots destruction and who loves evil more than good and lying more than truth. He declares that God will destroy this man forever, pluck him from his tent and root him out of the land of the living. The righteous will see it, fear, and laugh, noting that the man trusted in his riches and not in God. David contrasts himself as a green olive tree in God's house, trusting God's loving kindness and promising to give thanks and hope in God's name before the saints.

Chapter 53

Chapter 53 is a psalm attributed to David, addressed to the Chief Musician and set to the tune of "Mahalath." It declares that the fool says in his heart there is no God, and that people are corrupt, doing abominable iniquity, with no one doing good. God looks down from heaven on the children of men to see whether any understand and seek him, and finds that everyone has gone back and become filthy together. The workers of iniquity are accused of lacking knowledge, eating up God's people as they eat bread, and not calling on God. They are then said to be in great fear where no fear was, because God has scattered the bones of the one who encamps against his people and has rejected them, and the psalm closes with a wish that the salvation of Israel would come out of Zion, so that Jacob and Israel would rejoice when God brings his people back from captivity.

Chapter 54

Chapter 54 is a prayer attributed to David, set in the context of the Ziphites telling Saul that David was hiding among them. David asks God to save and vindicate him by his name and might, and to hear his prayer. He describes strangers and violent men who have risen against him and do not set God before them. David affirms that God is his helper and sustainer, asks that evil be repaid to his enemies, and promises a free will offering and thanksgiving. He closes by declaring that God has delivered him from all trouble and that he has seen triumph over his enemies.

Chapter 55

In Chapter 55, a contemplation attributed to David for the Chief Musician, David prays to God in anguish over the oppression of the wicked, feeling terror and wishing he could fly away like a dove to the wilderness. He asks God to confuse his enemies, describing violence, strife, malice and lies within the city. He reveals that the deepest wound comes from a companion and familiar friend who shared sweet fellowship with him and walked with him in God's house, but who violated his covenant with smooth words that were really drawn swords. David calls for sudden death on his enemies, resolves to call on God morning, noon and evening, and urges others to cast their burden on the LORD, trusting that God will bring the bloodthirsty and deceitful down into the pit.

Chapter 56

Chapter 56 is a poem by David, written for the Chief Musician to the tune "Silent Dove in Distant Lands," when the Philistines seized him in Gath. David asks God for mercy because his many enemies attack him all day, twist his words, and lurk to take his life. He repeatedly declares that he will trust God and not be afraid, asking what flesh or man can do to him. He asks God to cast down the peoples in anger, and notes that God counts his wanderings and keeps his tears. He closes by affirming that God is for him, promising thank offerings, and praising God for delivering his soul from death so he may walk before God in the light of the living.

Chapter 57

Chapter 57 is a poem by David, written when he fled from Saul and hid in the cave, set for the Chief Musician to the tune of "Do Not Destroy." David asks God for mercy and takes refuge in the shadow of God's wings until disaster passes. He describes himself as among lions and people whose teeth are spears and arrows and whose tongues are sharp swords. His enemies have set a net and dug a pit for him but fall into it themselves. David declares his heart steadfast, vows to sing and give thanks among the peoples and nations, and twice asks that God be exalted above the heavens and his glory be over all the earth.

Chapter 58

Chapter 58 is a poem by David, addressed to the Chief Musician and set to the tune of "Do Not Destroy." It challenges the "silent ones" and "sons of men," accusing them of plotting injustice and committing violence on the earth. It describes the wicked as wayward and lying from birth, with poison like a deaf cobra's that will not heed charmers. The speaker asks God to break their teeth, make them vanish like water, and be like a melting snail or a stillborn child. It ends with the righteous rejoicing at vengeance, and people saying there is a reward for the righteous and a God who judges the earth.

Chapter 59

Chapter 59 is a poem by David, written when Saul sent men to watch his house in order to kill him. David asks God to deliver him from bloodthirsty enemies who lie in wait for him though he has done no wrong. He asks God to punish the nations and the wicked traitors, and he describes his enemies as howling dogs who prowl the city at evening. He asks that they be scattered and consumed so that people will know God rules in Jacob. David ends by vowing to sing of God's strength and loving kindness, calling God his high tower and refuge.

Chapter 60

Chapter 60 is a teaching poem by David, set when he fought Aram Naharaim and Aram Zobah and Joab killed twelve thousand of Edom in the Valley of Salt. The speaker laments that God has rejected and broken his people, made the land tremble, and made them drink staggering wine, yet asks God to restore them. God is quoted as claiming Shechem, Succoth, Gilead, Manasseh, Ephraim, and Judah as his own, and declaring Moab his wash basin, Edom his to throw a sandal on, and Philistia a land over which he shouts in triumph. The speaker asks who will bring him into the strong city and to Edom, notes that God does not go out with their armies, and pleads for help against the adversary. The poem ends by affirming that through God the people will do valiantly and that he will tread down their adversaries.

Chapter 61

Chapter 61 is a prayer attributed to David, written for the Chief Musician and a stringed instrument. The speaker cries out to God from the end of the earth with an overwhelmed heart and asks to be led to a higher rock. He recalls that God has been a refuge and strong tower against the enemy, and he longs to dwell in God's tent and shelter under his wings. He affirms that God has heard his vows and given him the heritage of those who fear his name. The psalm asks for the king's life to be prolonged and for him to be enthroned before God forever, preserved by God's loving kindness and truth, and closes with a vow to sing praise daily.

Chapter 62

Chapter 62 is a psalm by David, addressed to the Chief Musician and Jeduthun, in which the psalmist declares that his soul rests in God alone as his rock, salvation, and fortress. He asks how long a group will assault him like a leaning wall, noting that they plan to throw him down from his lofty place, delight in lies, and bless with their mouths while cursing inwardly. He urges his own soul to wait in silence for God and calls on the people to trust God at all times and pour out their hearts to him. He declares that men of low and high degree are only a breath, warns against trusting in oppression, robbery, or riches, and closes by saying that power and loving kindness belong to God, who rewards each person according to his work.

Chapter 63

Chapter 63 is a psalm attributed to David, written when he was in the desert of Judah. The speaker addresses God as his God, saying his soul thirsts and his flesh longs for him in a dry and weary land. He recalls seeing God's power and glory in the sanctuary, declares that God's loving kindness is better than life, and vows to bless and praise him, lifting up his hands. He remembers God on his bed in the night watches, says God has been his help, and that God's right hand holds him up. The psalm ends by declaring that those who seek his soul to destroy it will go into the lower parts of the earth, be given to the sword and become jackal food, while the king rejoices in God and the mouths of liars are silenced.

Chapter 64

Chapter 64 is a psalm attributed to David, addressed to the Chief Musician. The psalmist cries out to God in complaint, asking to be preserved from fear of the enemy and hidden from the conspiracy of the wicked. He describes the evildoers as sharpening their tongues like swords and shooting deadly words at innocent men from ambush, while encouraging one another in secret snares and asking who will see them. The psalm then turns to say God will shoot them suddenly with an arrow, their own tongues will ruin them, and onlookers will shake their heads. All mankind will fear and declare God's work, and the righteous will be glad in the LORD, take refuge in him, and praise him.

Chapter 65

Psalm 65, a song of David for the Chief Musician, praises God in Zion as the hearer of prayer who atones for sins and draws chosen people near to live in his courts. It then declares him the hope of the whole earth, including those far away on the sea, who forms the mountains by his power and stills the roaring seas and the turmoil of the nations. People in faraway places are afraid at his wonders. The psalm closes by celebrating his care for the earth: he waters it, enriches it with the river of God, softens it with showers, and crowns the year with a bounty of grain and flocks, so that the hills, pastures, and valleys shout and sing for joy.

Chapter 66

Psalm 66 is a song for the Chief Musician that calls all the earth to shout joyfully to God and praise his awesome deeds. It recalls that God turned the sea into dry land and that his people crossed the river on foot, and it says he rules by might and watches the nations. The speakers say God tested and refined 'us' like silver through prison, burdens, men riding over their heads, fire and water, and then brought them to abundance. The psalmist promises to enter God's temple with burnt offerings and to pay the vows made in distress. He then tells those who fear God that God heard his prayer, and he blesses God for not turning away his prayer or loving kindness.

Chapter 67

Chapter 67 is a song for the Chief Musician, to be played with stringed instruments. The speakers, a community, ask God to be merciful to them, bless them, and make his face shine on them. They want this so that God's way and salvation will be known among all nations on earth. The song calls on all peoples and nations to praise God and sing for joy, because he will judge them with equity and govern them. It closes by noting that the earth has yielded its increase, and that God will bless the community so that all the ends of the earth fear him.

Chapter 68

Psalm 68, a psalm of David for the Chief Musician, calls on God to rise and scatter his enemies while the righteous rejoice. It praises God as father of the fatherless and defender of widows, who sets the lonely in families and freed prisoners, and recalls his march through the wilderness to Sinai and the defeat of kings. God is said to choose a mountain to reign on forever, in contrast to the envious mountains of Bashan, and to ascend on high, leading captives and receiving gifts. The psalm describes a procession of singers and tribal princes into the sanctuary, and looks ahead to kings bringing presents to Jerusalem and Egypt, Ethiopia, and all kingdoms singing to God. It ends by declaring God awesome in his sanctuaries and the giver of strength and power to his people Israel.

Chapter 69

In Chapter 69, a psalmist attributed to David cries to God for rescue from overwhelming waters and mire, describing exhaustion, hatred without cause, and shame borne for God's sake. He is a stranger to his own family, mocked in the gate and by drunkards, and given poison and vinegar when he seeks comfort. He asks God to deliver, answer, and redeem him, then calls for severe judgment on his persecutors, including desolation and being blotted out of the book of life. He closes by vowing to praise God with song and thanksgiving, and declares that God hears the needy and will save Zion and build the cities of Judah for those who love his name.

Chapter 70

Chapter 70 is a short prayer headed "For the Chief Musician. By David. A reminder." David urgently asks the LORD to hurry and deliver him and help him. He asks that those who seek his soul and desire his ruin be disappointed, confounded, and turned back in disgrace, including those who say "Aha! Aha!" He asks that all who seek God rejoice and be glad in God and continually say "Let God be exalted!" He closes by describing himself as poor and needy and calling God his help and deliverer, asking God not to delay.

Chapter 71

In Chapter 71, the speaker, an aging psalmist, asks the LORD for refuge, rescue, and deliverance from wicked and cruel enemies. He recalls relying on God from his youth and even from the womb, and he begs not to be rejected or forsaken in old age when his strength fails. His enemies conspire, saying God has forsaken him and no one will rescue him, and he prays that they be disappointed and covered with disgrace. He vows to keep hoping and to declare God's righteousness, salvation, and mighty works to the next generation. The chapter ends with the speaker promising to praise God with harp, lyre, and voice, trusting that those who want to harm him will be confounded.

Chapter 72

Chapter 72, attributed to Solomon, is a prayer asking God to give the king his justice and righteousness. It describes the royal son judging God's people and the poor with righteousness, saving the needy, and breaking the oppressor in pieces. His reign is pictured as lasting as long as the sun and moon, bringing prosperity, peace, and abundant grain, with dominion from sea to sea and kings of Tarshish, the islands, Sheba, and Seba bringing tribute. The king pities and redeems the poor and needy from oppression and violence, and men pray for and bless him, and his name endures forever. The chapter closes with praise to the LORD God, the God of Israel, who alone does marvelous deeds, and ends with a note that these are the prayers by David, the son of Jesse.

Chapter 73

Chapter 73 is a psalm by Asaph in which he confesses that he nearly stumbled in envy when he saw the prosperity of the wicked, who are arrogant, violent, free from burdens, and who mock God by asking how he could know. Asaph felt that keeping his heart clean had been in vain because he was plagued and punished daily, yet he refrained from speaking this aloud so as not to betray God's children. His understanding changed when he entered God's sanctuary and considered the end of the wicked, whom God sets in slippery places and suddenly destroys. Asaph then admits he was senseless like a brute beast, affirms that God holds his right hand, guides him, and will receive him to glory, and declares God his strength, portion, and refuge. He concludes that those far from God will perish and that it is good to draw near to God and tell of his works.

Chapter 74

In Chapter 74, a contemplation by Asaph, the speaker asks God why he has rejected his people, the sheep of his pasture, and urges him to remember Mount Zion, his congregation and inheritance. The speaker describes how adversaries roared in the assembly, set up their standards, hacked down the carved work with axes and hammers, burned the sanctuary, and burned all the places of worship in the land. The people see no signs and have no prophet, and the speaker asks how long the enemy will blaspheme. Recalling that God is King of old, who divided the sea, broke the heads of Leviathan, and set the day, night, boundaries of the earth, summer and winter, the speaker appeals to God to remember the mockery, honor his covenant, protect his dove and his poor, and arise to plead his own cause.

Chapter 75

Chapter 75 is a psalm of Asaph, written for the Chief Musician to the tune of "Do Not Destroy." The speaker gives thanks to God, whose Name is near, and speaks of God's appointed time to judge blamelessly while holding the earth's pillars firm. The speaker warns the arrogant and wicked not to boast or lift up their horns, since exaltation comes from no direction but from God, who puts down one and lifts up another. A cup of foaming, spiced wine in the LORD's hand is poured out for the wicked of the earth to drink to the dregs. The speaker vows to praise the God of Jacob forever, and declares that the horns of the wicked will be cut off while the horns of the righteous will be lifted up.

Chapter 76

Psalm 76, a song of Asaph for the Chief Musician on stringed instruments, celebrates God as known in Judah and great in Israel, with his dwelling in Salem and Zion. It describes God breaking the bows, shields, swords and weapons of war, and leaving valiant men plundered and unable to lift their hands. At the rebuke of the God of Jacob, chariot and horse fall into a dead sleep. God is presented as to be feared, pronouncing judgment from heaven so that the earth is silent, and arising to save the afflicted of the earth. The psalm ends by urging people to make and fulfill vows and bring presents to him, noting that he cuts off the spirit of princes and is feared by the kings of the earth.

Chapter 77

Chapter 77 is a psalm by Asaph, addressed to the Chief Musician and Jeduthun, in which the psalmist cries to God in a night of trouble and cannot be comforted. He asks whether the Lord will reject his people forever, whether his loving kindness and promise have failed, and whether God has forgotten to be gracious. He then resolves to appeal to the years of the right hand of the Most High and to remember and meditate on the LORD's deeds and wonders of old. He praises God as the one who does wonders and who redeemed the sons of Jacob and Joseph with his arm. The psalm closes by describing the waters, storm and thunder at the sea, where God's unseen path led his people like a flock by the hand of Moses and Aaron.

Chapter 78

In this contemplation by Asaph, the speaker calls his people to hear a parable so that each generation will teach the next to trust God and keep his commandments. He recounts God's wonders for Israel in Egypt and the wilderness, including the sea split, the cloud and fire, water from the rock, manna and quail, and the plagues, alongside the people's repeated rebellion, disbelief, and insincere repentance. God punished them yet often showed mercy, remembering they were but flesh. After he brought them into the land, they turned to high places and idols, and he abandoned Shiloh and gave his people over to the sword. He then rejected Joseph's tent and Ephraim, chose Judah, Mount Zion, and David the shepherd, who led Israel with integrity and skill.

Chapter 79

In this psalm of Asaph, the speaker laments that the nations have invaded God's inheritance, defiled the holy temple, and laid Jerusalem in heaps. The bodies of God's servants lie unburied, their blood shed like water, and the survivors have become an object of scorn to their neighbors. The speaker asks how long God will be angry, and calls on God to pour out wrath on nations that do not know him because they devoured Jacob. The speaker asks God to disregard the forefathers' iniquities, to show mercy, to help and forgive for the sake of his name, and to avenge his servants' blood, preserve the condemned, and repay the neighbors sevenfold. The psalm ends with a promise that the people, the sheep of God's pasture, will thank and praise him forever.

Chapter 80

Chapter 80 is a psalm by Asaph, set to the tune of "The Lilies of the Covenant," in which the people of Israel appeal to the Shepherd of Israel, the LORD God of Armies, to restore them. They say he has fed them the bread of tears and made them a source of contention to their neighbors, while their enemies laugh. Using the image of a vine brought out of Egypt, planted after the nations were driven out, and spreading from the mountains to the sea and the River, they lament that its walls are broken and it is ravaged, burned, and cut down. They ask God to look down, visit the vine, and put his hand on the man of his right hand, the son of man he made strong. The refrain "Turn us again... cause your face to shine, and we will be saved" recurs in verses 3, 7, and 19, and the people promise to call on God's name if revived.

Chapter 81

Chapter 81, a psalm for the Chief Musician by Asaph, opens with a call to sing aloud to God and to play tambourine, lyre, harp and trumpet at the New Moon, full moon and feast day, which is described as a statute and ordinance for Israel. God then speaks, recalling how he freed his people from burdens in Egypt, answered them in trouble, and tested them at the waters of Meribah. He commands that Israel have no strange or foreign god, reminding them that he is the LORD who brought them out of Egypt. Because his people did not listen and Israel desired none of him, he let them follow their own stubborn counsels. God laments that they do not walk in his ways and says that if they did, he would subdue their enemies and feed them with the finest wheat and honey from the rock.

Chapter 82

Chapter 82 is a psalm of Asaph in which God presides in the great assembly and judges among the gods. God rebukes them for judging unjustly and showing partiality to the wicked, and commands them to defend the weak, the poor, the fatherless, and the oppressed, and to rescue the needy from the wicked. The text says the judged ones neither know nor understand, walk in darkness, and the foundations of the earth are shaken. God declares that though they are gods and sons of the Most High, they will die like men and fall like one of the rulers. The psalm closes with a plea for God to arise and judge the earth, since God inherits all the nations.

Chapter 83

In this psalm of Asaph, the speaker appeals to God not to remain silent because His enemies are stirring up and conspiring against His people. A coalition of nations, including Edom, the Ishmaelites, Moab, the Hagrites, Gebal, Ammon, Amalek, Philistia, Tyre, and Assyria, plots to destroy Israel so its name is remembered no more. The speaker asks God to treat them as He did Midian, Sisera, Jabin, Oreb, Zeeb, Zebah, and Zalmunna. He asks that they be scattered like tumbleweed and chaff and pursued by tempest and storm like fire through a forest. The psalm ends with the hope that they will be confounded and seek God's name, and will know that the LORD alone is the Most High over all the earth.

Chapter 84

Chapter 84 is a psalm by the sons of Korah, written for the Chief Musician, in which the psalmist expresses deep longing for the courts of the LORD of Armies. The psalmist notes that even the sparrow and swallow find nests near God's altars, and declares those who dwell in God's house and those who set their hearts on pilgrimage to be blessed. Pilgrims passing through the valley of Weeping turn it into a place of springs and go from strength to strength until each appears before God in Zion. The psalmist prays for God to hear and to look on his anointed, and states that a day in God's courts is better than a thousand elsewhere. The psalm closes by calling the LORD a sun and a shield who gives grace and glory and withholds no good thing from those who walk blamelessly, and by blessing the one who trusts in him.

Chapter 85

Psalm 85, a psalm by the sons of Korah for the Chief Musician, recalls how the LORD has favored his land, restored Jacob's fortunes, forgiven his people's iniquity, and turned from his fierce anger. The psalmist then asks God of salvation to turn them again, end his indignation, revive his people, and show them loving kindness and salvation. The speaker resolves to hear what God will say, expecting peace for his saints, who must not return to folly. The psalm closes with a vision of salvation near those who fear him, mercy, truth, righteousness and peace meeting, the land yielding its increase, and righteousness going before the LORD to prepare the way for his steps.

Chapter 86

Chapter 86 is a prayer by David in which he pleads with the LORD to hear him because he is poor and needy. He asks for preservation, mercy, joy, and strength, and declares that the Lord is good, forgiving, and abundant in loving kindness. He praises the Lord as incomparable among the gods, foresees all nations worshiping him, and asks to be taught the Lord's way and given an undivided heart. David says the Lord has delivered him from the lowest Sheol, while proud and violent men have risen against him without regard for God. He closes by asking the Lord to turn to him, save him, and show a sign of goodness so that those who hate him will be shamed.

Chapter 87

Psalm 87, a psalm by the sons of Korah and a song, celebrates Zion as the city of God. The LORD's foundation is in the holy mountains, and he loves the gates of Zion more than all the dwellings of Jacob. Glorious things are spoken about the city. The LORD says he will record Rahab, Babylon, Philistia, Tyre, and Ethiopia among those who acknowledge him, each described as born there. The Most High himself will establish Zion, and singers and dancers declare, "All my springs are in you."

Chapter 88

Chapter 88 is a lament attributed to Heman, the Ezrahite, and to the sons of Korah, addressed to the LORD, the God of his salvation. Heman says he has cried to the LORD day and night while his soul is full of troubles and his life nears Sheol. He describes himself as counted among the dead in the pit, afflicted by God's wrath and terrors, and deprived of friends who now find him abominable. He asks whether the LORD shows wonders, loving kindness, or faithfulness to the dead in the grave. The psalm ends without relief, with Heman still crying out, asking why the LORD rejects him, and saying lover and friend have been put far from him into darkness.

Chapter 89

Chapter 89, a contemplation by Ethan the Ezrahite, opens by praising the LORD's loving kindness and faithfulness, exalting him above all heavenly beings and as ruler of the sea, Rahab, the heavens, and the earth. It recalls the LORD's covenant with David, his chosen servant, promising him an everlasting offspring and throne, a fatherly relationship, the status of firstborn and highest of kings, and discipline but not abandonment if his children forsake the law. The tone then turns to lament: the LORD is accused of rejecting and being angry with his anointed, renouncing the covenant, defiling the crown, ruining strongholds, and letting enemies triumph. The speaker asks how long the LORD will hide himself, reminds him of human brevity and of the reproach borne by his servants, asks where his former loving kindnesses are, and ends with a blessing of the LORD, Amen and Amen.

Chapter 90

Chapter 90 is a prayer attributed to Moses, the man of God, addressed to the Lord as the dwelling place of his people through all generations. It contrasts the Lord's everlasting existence before the mountains and the earth with the brevity of human life, which is compared to grass that sprouts in the morning and withers by evening. The speaker says people are consumed by the Lord's anger and that their iniquities and secret sins are set before him, so that life of seventy or eighty years is full of labor and sorrow and passes quickly. The prayer then asks the Lord to teach them to count their days and gain a heart of wisdom. It closes with pleas that the Lord relent, show compassion, satisfy them with loving kindness, make them glad for as many days as he afflicted them, show his work and glory to their children, and establish the work of their hands.

Chapter 91

Chapter 91 describes the security of the person who dwells in the secret place of the Most High and makes the LORD their refuge. The speaker declares the LORD to be a refuge and fortress, who delivers from the fowler's snare, pestilence, terror, arrows, and destruction, covering the faithful with his feathers and wings. Chapter 147

Chapter 148

Chapter 148 is a call for all creation to praise the LORD. The chapter ends by noting that he has lifted up the horn of his people, the children of Israel, a people near to him.

Chapter 149

Chapter 149 calls on the assembly of the saints to praise the LORD with a new song. Chapter 150

Chapter 150 is a closing call to praise the LORD. It tells the reader to praise God in his sanctuary and in his heavens, for his acts of power, his mighty acts, and his excellent greatness.
