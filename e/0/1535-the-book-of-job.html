The Book of Job

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

This document is the book of Job, a dialogue-driven narrative about a blameless, wealthy man in the land of Uz and the question of why he suffers. In the opening chapters the LORD allows Satan to test Job. Job loses his livestock, servants and all ten children, then is struck with painful sores. He refuses his wife's urging to renounce God and does not sin with his lips. Three friends, Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar, come to comfort him and sit silently with him for seven days. Job then breaks the silence by cursing the day of his birth and longing for death. The middle of the book is three cycles of speeches. The friends argue that God is just, that the wicked are punished and the innocent do not perish, and so Job must have sinned and should repent. Job rejects this. He maintains his integrity, notes that the wicked often prosper, accuses God of wearing him down, longs for an umpire or a hearing with God, and declares confidence that his Redeemer lives and that he will see God. Bildad's final reply is brief, and Job answers with praise of God's power. He then insists on his righteousness, offers a hymn that wisdom is found only in the fear of the Lord, recalls his former honor, laments his present humiliation, and swears a long series of oaths of innocence, demanding that the Almighty answer. A new speaker, Elihu, angry at Job for justifying himself and at the friends for failing to answer him, argues that God is just and greater than man, speaks through dreams and suffering to turn people from the pit, and reveals his power in nature. The LORD then answers Job out of the whirlwind with questions about creation, the heavens, and wild animals, which show the limits of Job's knowledge. Job, after a first reply that he is of small account, hears more about behemoth and Leviathan. He acknowledges that the LORD can do all things, admits he spoke of what he did not understand, and repents in dust and ashes. The LORD rebukes Eliphaz and his two friends for not speaking rightly as Job did, and has them offer sacrifices while Job prays for them. The LORD restores Job's prosperity at twice what he had before, along with seven sons and three daughters. Job lives one hundred forty more years and dies old and full of days.

Chapter 1

Chapter 1 introduces Job, a blameless and upright man in the land of Uz who fears God, has seven sons and three daughters, and is the greatest of all the children of the east by virtue of his great wealth. Job regularly offers burnt offerings for his children in case they have sinned. When God's sons present themselves before the LORD, Satan comes among them and, after the LORD praises Job, argues that Job fears God only because he is blessed, and the LORD allows Satan to take all that Job has but not to touch Job himself. Four messengers then report in quick succession that the Sabeans took the oxen and donkeys, fire from the sky burned the sheep, the Chaldeans took the camels, and a great wind killed Job's children. Job tears his robe, shaves his head, and worships, saying the LORD gave and has taken away, and the text says he did not sin or charge God with wrongdoing.

Chapter 2

On another day when God's sons present themselves before the LORD, Satan comes among them and the LORD points to Job as blameless and still faithful despite Satan's earlier incitement. Satan argues that a man will give everything for his own life and says Job will renounce God if his bone and flesh are touched. The LORD places Job in Satan's hand but forbids him to take Job's life, and Satan strikes Job with painful sores from foot to head. Job sits among ashes scraping himself with a potsherd, rejects his wife's urging to renounce God and die, and does not sin with his lips. His three friends, Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar, come to comfort him, weep and tear their robes on seeing him, and sit silently with him on the ground for seven days and nights.

Chapter 3

In Chapter 3, Job breaks his silence and curses the day of his birth and the night of his conception, wishing them darkness, barrenness, and erasure from the calendar. He blames that night for not shutting the doors of his mother's womb or hiding trouble from his eyes. Job asks why he did not die at birth, imagining the rest he would have in death alongside kings, princes, the wicked, the weary, prisoners, and servants. He then asks why light and life are given to the miserable and bitter in soul who long for death, and to a man whose way is hidden and whom God has hedged in. He ends by describing his sighing, groaning, fear, and lack of rest, as trouble comes upon him.

Chapter 4

Eliphaz the Temanite answers Job, asking whether Job will be grieved by his speaking and noting that Job has previously instructed and strengthened others but now faints when trouble touches him. Eliphaz asks whether Job's piety and integrity are not his confidence and hope, and argues from what he has seen that the innocent do not perish while those who plow iniquity and sow trouble reap the same. He says such people perish by the breath of God, likening them to lions whose teeth are broken and whose cubs are scattered. Eliphaz then recounts a night vision in which a spirit passed before his face and a voice spoke out of silence. The voice asks whether mortal man can be more just or pure than God, says God does not trust his servants and charges his angels with error, and concludes that those who dwell in houses of clay are destroyed and die without wisdom.

Chapter 5

In Chapter 5 a speaker addresses a listener, asking whether any of the holy ones will answer him, and warns that resentment and jealousy kill the foolish and simple. He recounts seeing a foolish man take root and then cursing his dwelling, with the man's children crushed in the gate and his harvest eaten by the hungry. He says affliction does not spring from the ground, but man is born to trouble as sparks fly upward. The speaker says he would seek God and commit his cause to him, praising God for marvelous works, for giving rain, for lifting the lowly, for frustrating the crafty, and for saving the needy so the poor have hope. He closes by calling happy the man whom God corrects, promising the listener deliverance in famine, war, and trouble, peace with the animals, a secure tent, great offspring, and a full age, and says this has been researched and is true.

Chapter 6

In Chapter 6, Job answers his friends, saying his anguish is heavier than the sand of the seas and that this is why his words have been rash. He describes the arrows and terrors of the Almighty as being within him, and he longs for God to crush him and cut him off, taking consolation that he has not denied the words of the Holy One. He rebukes his friends for lacking kindness, comparing his brothers to brooks that vanish in the dry season and leave caravans from Tema and Sheba confounded. He challenges the friends to teach him his error, accuses them of reproving the words of a desperate man and of treating a friend as merchandise, and insists that his cause is righteous and that there is no injustice on his tongue.

Chapter 7

In Chapter 7 the speaker, Job, reflects on the hardship and brevity of human life, comparing it to the toil of a hired hand and describing his own months of misery, sleepless nights, and afflicted skin covered with worms and dust. He says his days pass swiftly and without hope, and that death, going down to Sheol, is final, with no return to his house. Vowing not to keep silent, he complains in the bitterness of his soul and addresses God directly. He asks whether he is a sea or sea monster that God must guard him, and accuses God of terrifying him with dreams and visions when he seeks rest. He says he loathes his life, asks why God magnifies and tests man so closely, and asks why God does not pardon his disobedience, since soon he will lie in the dust and God will seek him but he will not be.

Chapter 8

Bildad the Shuhite responds to the one he addresses, rebuking him for speaking like a mighty wind and insisting that God does not pervert justice. He suggests that the addressee's children died because of their own sin, and urges him to seek God and be pure and upright so that God would restore him and make his latter end greater. Bildad appeals to the wisdom of past generations and uses images of papyrus and rushes withering without water, and a spider's web, to show that the hope of those who forget God perishes. He closes by asserting that God will not cast away a blameless man or uphold evildoers, and that the addressee will again have laughter while his haters are clothed with shame and the tent of the wicked is no more.

Chapter 9

In Chapter 9, Job answers a previous speaker by asking how a man can be just with God. He describes God's overwhelming power over mountains, earth, sun, stars, heavens and sea, and says God passes by him unseen and cannot be challenged. Job says he could not argue his case or trust that God would hear him, since God breaks him with a storm and multiplies his wounds without cause. He claims to be blameless, yet says God destroys the blameless and the wicked alike and lets the earth fall into the hands of the wicked. He laments that his days are fleeing, that no washing can make him clean before God, and that there is no umpire between them, and he wishes God's rod and terror removed so he could speak without fear.

Chapter 10

In Chapter 10, the speaker, weary of his life, resolves to voice his bitter complaint to God and asks not to be condemned. He questions whether God, who does not see as mortals do, needs to hunt for his sin when God knows he is not wicked. He recalls that God's hands formed him like clay, knit him with skin, flesh, bones and sinews, and granted him life and loving kindness, yet God now seems to destroy him. He says that whether he is wicked or righteous he is filled with disgrace, and that God hunts him like a lion. He wishes he had died at birth, and asks God to leave him alone for a little comfort before he goes to the dark land of the shadow of death.

Chapter 11

Zophar, the Naamathite, answers the previous speaker in Chapter 11, accusing him of being full of talk, mocking, and claiming his doctrine is pure and that he is clean in God's eyes. Zophar wishes God would speak against him and says God exacts less than his iniquity deserves. He describes the mystery of God as higher than heaven, deeper than Sheol, and broader than the sea, so that no one can oppose God. He then urges the addressee to set his heart aright, stretch out his hands to God, and put away iniquity, promising security, clarity, and rest. He closes by saying the eyes of the wicked will fail and their hope will be the giving up of the spirit.

Chapter 12

In Chapter 12, Job answers his friends with sarcasm, saying they think wisdom will die with them while he has understanding equal to theirs. He complains that he, a just and blameless man who called on God and was answered, has become a joke to his neighbor, while robbers' tents prosper and those who provoke God are secure. He tells them to ask the animals, birds, earth, and fish, which all know that the LORD's hand has done this and that every living thing's life and breath is in his hand. Job then declares that wisdom, might, counsel, and understanding belong to God, and describes how God breaks down what cannot be rebuilt, withholds and sends the waters, and strips counselors, judges, kings, priests, and princes of their power. He adds that God raises and destroys nations and takes understanding from the chiefs of the earth so that they grope in the dark and stagger like drunkards.

Chapter 13

The speaker, who is in a dispute with God, tells his friends that he knows as much as they do and is not inferior to them. He calls them forgers of lies and worthless physicians, and accuses them of speaking deceitfully and showing partiality on God's behalf, warning that God will reprove them. He resolves to speak and present his case to God even at the risk of his life, saying that he has set his cause in order and knows he is righteous. He then asks God to withdraw his hand and terror from him, and to either call or answer him, and he demands to know his iniquities and sins. He closes by lamenting that God hides his face, treats him as an enemy, writes bitter things against him, confines his feet, and leaves him decaying like a rotten thing or a moth-eaten garment.

Chapter 14

In Chapter 14 the speaker laments the brevity and frailty of human life, describing man as born of woman, short-lived, full of trouble, and fleeting like a flower or shadow. He asks God why He brings such a creature into judgment, noting that man's days and bounds are fixed by God. The speaker contrasts man with a tree, which can sprout again after being cut down, whereas man dies and does not rise. He wishes God would hide him in Sheol until His wrath passed and then remember him, and wonders whether a dead man might live again. He ends by saying God counts his steps and seals up his sin, destroys man's hope like water wearing away stones, and leaves the dead ignorant of their sons while the flesh feels pain and the soul mourns.

Chapter 15

Eliphaz the Temanite answers his addressee, rebuking him for speaking empty, unprofitable words that undermine fear and devotion before God. He asks whether the addressee is the first man born, holds God's secret counsel, or knows more than the aged and gray-headed among them, and he says the addressee's own mouth condemns him. He argues that no man is clean or righteous before God, whose holy ones and heavens are not pure in his sight. Eliphaz then declares what he has seen and what wise men received from their fathers: the wicked man suffers terror and pain all his days, is pursued by the sword and darkness, and loses his wealth because he defied the Almighty. He closes by saying the godless company will be barren, their tents consumed by fire, as they conceive mischief and produce iniquity.

Chapter 16

In Chapter 16, Job answers his friends, calling them miserable comforters and saying he could speak as they do if their places were reversed, though he would strengthen rather than accuse them. He describes his grief as unrelieved whether he speaks or stays silent, and he says God has worn him out, shriveled him, and made his company desolate. Job portrays God as an adversary who tears, breaks, and targets him with archers, and who hands him over to the wicked. He says he wears sackcloth and weeps, yet insists there is no violence in his hands and his prayer is pure. He appeals to the earth not to cover his blood, claims a witness in heaven who vouches for him, and weeps to God, asking that God maintain his right, since he will soon go the way of no return.

Chapter 17

In Chapter 17 the speaker declares his spirit consumed and his days extinct, with the grave ready for him, and says mockers surround him. He asks to be given a pledge, since no one will strike hands with him, and says that the one addressed has hidden his opponents' hearts from understanding. He describes being made a byword, spat upon, and dimmed in eye by sorrow, while upright men are astonished and the righteous hold to their way. He tells his opponents to come back, saying he will find no wise man among them. He ends by saying his days and plans are broken, and asks where his hope is if he makes Sheol his house, calling corruption his father and the worm his mother and sister, and whether his hope will go down to Sheol's gates and into the dust.

Chapter 18

In Chapter 18, Bildad the Shuhite answers by rebuking the person he addresses for hunting for words and treating his companions as unclean animals. He insists the earth will not be forsaken nor the rock moved for the angry listener. Most of the speech describes the fate of the wicked: his light and lamp are put out, his steps shortened, and he is caught in nets, snares, traps, and a hidden noose. Terrors chase him, death devours his body, and he is rooted out of his tent and brought to the king of terrors. His roots dry up, his memory perishes, he leaves no son or grandson, and later generations are astonished at his day, as such are the dwellings of those who do not know God.

Chapter 19

In Chapter 19, Job answers his friends, accusing them of tormenting him with words and reproaching him repeatedly. He insists that God has wronged and besieged him, stripping his glory, breaking down his hope, and counting him as an adversary. He laments that his brothers, relatives, friends, servants, wife, and even young children have turned from him, and that he has barely escaped with his life. He pleads for pity from his friends and wishes his words were engraved in rock forever. He declares his confidence that his Redeemer lives and that he will see God in his flesh, and warns his friends to fear the sword of judgment.

Chapter 20

In Chapter 20, Zophar the Naamathite responds to the reproof he has heard by insisting that the triumph of the wicked is brief and the joy of the godless lasts only a moment. He describes how a wicked man, though he rises to the heavens, will perish like his own dung, vanish like a dream, and be forgotten by those who saw him. His ill-gotten riches, sweet in his mouth, turn to cobra venom; he must vomit them up, and he is not allowed to enjoy them because he oppressed and forsook the poor and seized houses. God casts his wrath on him, and he is struck by weapons, terrors, and unfanned fire, while heaven and earth testify against him. Zophar concludes that this is the portion and heritage God appoints for a wicked man.

Chapter 21

In Chapter 21, Job answers his friends, asking them to listen to him and let that be their consolation before they resume mocking him. He challenges their claim that the wicked are punished in this life by asking why the wicked live long, grow powerful, see their children established, and enjoy safe houses, thriving livestock, music, and prosperity until they go down to Sheol. He notes that such people tell God to depart, yet Job says their prosperity is not in their own hand and he rejects their counsel. He argues that one man dies at ease and another in bitterness, yet both lie alike in the dust, and that the evil man is honored at his grave with no one repaying him. He ends by calling his friends' comfort nonsense and their answers falsehood.

Chapter 22

Eliphaz the Temanite answers the man he addresses, arguing that God gains nothing from human righteousness and that the man's suffering must come from great wickedness. He accuses him of taking pledges for nothing, stripping the naked, withholding water and bread, and sending widows away empty. He says snares, sudden fear, darkness and floods surround the man as a result. Eliphaz then describes God as high above the heavens and warns against following the old way of wicked men who were snatched away before their time. He closes by urging the man to acquaint himself with God, return to the Almighty, and put away unrighteousness, promising treasure, answered prayer, light on his ways, and deliverance through clean hands.

Chapter 23

In Chapter 23, Job answers by saying his complaint is still rebellious because God's hand weighs heavily on him despite his groaning. He longs to find God and come to his seat so he can set out his cause and fill his mouth with arguments, believing God would listen rather than overpower him. Job says he cannot find God in the east, west, north, or south, yet God knows his way and, once he has been tried, Job expects to come out like gold. He affirms that he has kept God's way and treasured his commands, but he also describes God as unopposable and doing whatever he desires. Job ends by saying he is terrified and faint of heart before God, and that he was not cut off before the darkness came.

Chapter 24

In Chapter 24 the speaker asks why the Almighty does not set times of judgment and why those who know him do not see his days. He describes the wicked who move landmarks, seize flocks, drive off the donkey and ox of the fatherless and widow, and push the needy aside, so that the poor hide, scavenge for food like wild donkeys, go naked and exposed to the cold and rain, and labor hungry and thirsty for others. He says men groan in the city and the wounded cry out, yet God does not regard the folly. He then describes those who rebel against the light, including murderers, thieves and adulterers who act in darkness and fear the morning, and says they will be cursed, consumed by Sheol, forgotten, and broken like a tree. He adds that God preserves the mighty by his power and gives them security while watching their ways, until they are brought low and cut off like ears of grain, and he closes by challenging anyone to prove him a liar.

Chapter 25

In Chapter 25, Bildad the Shuhite gives a brief reply in which he extols God's dominion, the fear that attends him, and the peace he makes in his high places. He asks whether God's armies can be counted and on whom his light does not arise. He then asks how man can be just with God, or how one born of a woman can be clean. He points out that even the moon lacks brightness and the stars are not pure in God's sight, concluding that man, a worm and the son of man, is far less so.

Chapter 26

In Chapter 26, Job answers a previous speaker with sarcastic questions about how that speaker has helped the powerless, the weak, and the unwise, and whose spirit spoke through him. Job then describes God's dominion over the realm of the dead, where departed spirits tremble and Sheol and Abaddon lie uncovered before God. He praises God's power over creation: stretching the north over empty space, hanging the earth on nothing, binding the waters in clouds, setting a boundary on the waters, and shaking the pillars of heaven. Job says God stirs the sea, strikes through Rahab, adorns the heavens by his Spirit, and pierces the swift serpent. He concludes that all this is only the outskirts of God's ways, a small whisper, and that no one can understand the thunder of his power.

Chapter 27

In Chapter 27, Job resumes speaking and swears by the living God, whom he says has taken away his right and made his soul bitter, that he will not speak unrighteousness or deceit. He refuses to justify his friends and vows to hold fast to his righteousness and integrity until he dies. He wishes his enemy to be treated as the wicked and asks what hope the godless have when God takes their life, and whether God will hear their cry. Job then promises to teach about the hand of God, describing the portion of the wicked: their children die by the sword or go hungry, their widows do not mourn, and their riches pass to the just and innocent. He closes by describing the wicked man's sudden end, as terrors, a storm, and the east wind sweep him from his place while men clap and hiss at him.

Chapter 28

Chapter 28 opens by describing how man mines silver, gold, iron, copper, and precious stones, breaking open shafts, cutting channels in rock, overturning mountains, and bringing hidden things to light along paths no bird or beast knows. It then asks where wisdom and understanding can be found, answering that man does not know its price and it is not in the land of the living. The deep and the sea deny having it, and no gold, silver, onyx, sapphire, coral, crystal, rubies, or topaz can purchase or equal it. Destruction and Death have only heard a rumor of it. God alone understands its way and place, having seen it when he set the wind, waters, rain, and lightning, and he tells man that the fear of the Lord is wisdom and departing from evil is understanding.

Chapter 29

Job continues his parable by longing for the months of old, when God watched over him, his children were around him, and his life was blessed with abundance. He recalls the honor he received at the city gate, where young men hid, the aged stood, and princes and nobles fell silent. He explains that this esteem came from his righteousness: he delivered the poor, the fatherless, and the dying, caused the widow's heart to sing, and broke the jaws of the unrighteous. He remembers expecting to die in his own house with his glory renewed, and recalls how men waited for his counsel like rain, while he sat as chief and lived like a king who comforts mourners.

Chapter 30

In Chapter 30, Job laments his fall from honor. He describes how men younger than himself, whose fathers he once thought unworthy to work with his sheep dogs, now mock him; they are portrayed as gaunt outcasts who live in wastelands and were driven out of the land. These men make Job their song and byword, spit in his face, and press destruction upon him. Job then turns to God, saying he cries out and gets no answer, that God has become cruel and persecutes him, and that he expects death. He ends by recalling that he wept for the troubled and needy, yet evil and darkness came when he looked for good and light, leaving him mourning, ill, and isolated.

Chapter 31

In Chapter 31, Job concludes his defense with a series of oaths declaring his innocence. He says he made a covenant with his eyes against lust, and denies falsehood, adultery, despising his servants, withholding help from the poor, widow and fatherless, trusting in gold, worshiping the sun or moon, rejoicing at an enemy's ruin, turning away travelers, hiding transgressions, and wronging his land. For each claim he names a self-imposed penalty, such as losing his harvest, his wife being given to others, or his arm being broken. He acknowledges that God sees his ways and that calamity from God terrifies him. He ends by demanding that the Almighty answer him and write out an indictment, which he would wear like a crown, and the narrator notes that the words of Job are ended.

Chapter 32

Job's three friends stop answering him because he remains righteous in his own eyes. Elihu the son of Barachel, the Buzite, becomes angry at Job for justifying himself rather than God, and at the three friends for condemning Job without finding an answer. Elihu explains that he held back because the others were older, but argues that understanding comes from the Spirit of the Almighty rather than from age or greatness. He declares that he will now give his own answer, saying he is full of words and ready to burst like a wineskin. He vows to show no partiality and to give no flattering titles, lest his Maker take him away.

Chapter 33

The speaker addresses Job directly, asking him to listen and answer, and affirms that he too was made by God's Spirit and formed from clay, so he is no terror to Job. He recalls Job's claims that he is clean and innocent and that God treats him as an enemy, and replies that Job is not just in this because God is greater than man and owes no account of his matters. He explains that God speaks to people through dreams, visions, and the chastening pain of a sickbed, in order to turn man from his purpose and keep his soul from the pit. If an angel or interpreter, one in a thousand, shows a man what is right, God is gracious, finds a ransom, restores his flesh and righteousness, and brings him to sing of his sin and redemption. The speaker closes by urging Job to listen, to answer if he has anything to say since he desires to justify him, or otherwise to stay silent and be taught wisdom.

Chapter 34

In Chapter 34, Elihu addresses the wise men and men of understanding, asking them to weigh his words and choose together what is right. He quotes Job as claiming to be righteous while God has taken away his right, and accuses Job of keeping company with the wicked and saying it profits nothing to delight in God. Elihu insists that God cannot do wickedness or pervert justice, that He owes no one an account for governing the earth, and that He sees all human ways, breaks the mighty, and hears the cry of the poor. He argues that no one can condemn God when He gives quietness, and that no one has truly told God they would repent. He closes by saying Job speaks without knowledge and wishes Job were tried to the end for adding rebellion to his sin.

Chapter 35

In Chapter 35, Elihu continues his speech by answering Job and his companions. He challenges Job's claim that his righteousness exceeds God's and his question about what profit there is in not sinning. Elihu tells Job to look at the skies to see that God is higher than he is, so that neither sin nor righteousness affects God but only other people. He says people cry out under oppression yet do not seek God their Maker, so God does not answer the cry of the proud and does not hear an empty cry. He concludes that Job speaks empty talk and multiplies words without knowledge.

Chapter 36

Elihu continues speaking, asking for patience as he claims to speak on God's behalf and to declare his Maker righteous. He says God is mighty and despises no one, that he denies the wicked life, gives justice to the afflicted, and exalts the righteous, and that when people are bound in affliction he shows them their pride and calls them to turn from iniquity. Those who listen prosper, while those who do not perish without knowledge. Elihu then turns to the person he addresses, warning him against being enticed by riches or bribes, against desiring the night, and against choosing iniquity over affliction. He closes by praising God's greatness, who draws up water into rain, spreads the clouds, thunders, and commands the lightning, which judges peoples and gives food in abundance.

Chapter 37

In Chapter 37, a speaker describes God's power as seen in nature, beginning with his heart trembling at the sound of God's voice. He portrays God's thundering voice and lightning reaching the ends of the earth, and God commanding snow, rain, storm, cold, and ice, so that animals take shelter and people are made to know his work. The speaker says God directs the clouds for correction, for his land, or for loving kindness. He then addresses Job directly, telling him to stand still and consider God's wondrous works, and asks a series of questions about whether Job understands the clouds, the lightning, or can spread out the sky. He closes by saying the Almighty cannot be reached, is exalted in power, and does not oppress in justice and great righteousness, so men revere him and he does not regard those who are wise of heart.

Chapter 38

In Chapter 38 the LORD answers Job out of the whirlwind, challenging him as one who darkens counsel by words without knowledge and telling him to brace himself to be questioned. The LORD asks where Job was when the earth's foundations were laid, when the morning stars sang, and when the sea was shut in with doors and bounds. He then asks whether Job has commanded the morning, entered the springs of the sea, seen the gates of death, or knows the way to the dwelling of light and darkness. The LORD questions Job about snow, hail, lightning, rain, dew, ice, and frost, and about binding the Pleiades and Orion and knowing the laws of the heavens. The chapter closes with questions about who gives wisdom and who counts the clouds, and about who hunts prey for the lioness and provides food for the raven and its young.

Chapter 39

In Chapter 39, the speaker poses a series of rhetorical questions about wild animals to show the limits of human knowledge and control. He asks about mountain goats and does giving birth, the wild donkey living free in the wilderness, the wild ox that will not serve or be harnessed, the ostrich that neglects her eggs and lacks wisdom, the powerful war horse that charges into battle, and the hawk and eagle that soar and nest on high cliffs. Each creature is presented as living by capacities and instincts that the listener did not give and cannot command.

Chapter 40

In Chapter 40, the LORD challenges Job, asking whether one who argues with the Almighty will answer. Job replies that he is of small account, lays his hand on his mouth, and says he will proceed no further. Out of the whirlwind the LORD tells Job to brace himself, asks whether he will annul his judgment and condemn him to be justified, and challenges him to deck himself with majesty, bring down the proud and the wicked, so that the LORD would admit his own right hand can save him. The LORD then directs Job to behemoth, which he made as well as Job, describing its grass-eating, its strength, bones like bronze and iron, its calm in the marsh and the swelling Jordan, and asking whether anyone can capture it.

Chapter 41

In Chapter 41, the speaker challenges the addressee with a series of rhetorical questions about Leviathan, asking whether he can catch, tame, sell, or fight it. The speaker declares that no one is fierce enough to stir Leviathan up, and asks who then can stand before the speaker, since everything under the heavens belongs to the speaker. The speaker then describes Leviathan's limbs, strength, and frame in detail: his tightly sealed scales, fiery breath, firm heart, and terrifying jaws. Weapons of every kind fail against him, and he churns the deep like a boiling pot. The chapter ends by calling Leviathan without equal on earth, made without fear, and king over all the sons of pride.

Chapter 42

In Chapter 42, Job answers the LORD, acknowledging that the LORD can do all things and that he himself spoke of things he did not understand. He says he had heard of the LORD by hearing but now sees him, and he abhors himself and repents in dust and ashes. The LORD then tells Eliphaz the Temanite that his wrath is kindled against him and his two friends because they did not speak rightly of him as Job did, and directs them to offer seven bulls and seven rams as a burnt offering while Job prays for them. After the friends obey, the LORD accepts Job and restores his prosperity at twice what he had before. Job's relatives and acquaintances comfort him and give him gifts, he has fourteen thousand sheep, six thousand camels, a thousand yoke of oxen, a thousand female donkeys, seven sons and three daughters, and he lives one hundred forty more years before dying old and full of days.
