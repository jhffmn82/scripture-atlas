Abstract written by a model

Laban is a Syrian man of Haran, son of Bethuel and Nahor's line, brother of Rebekah, and thus Jacob's mother's brother. He fathers Rachel and Leah, owns flocks and household gods (teraphim), and invokes the God of Abraham and Nahor. In Chapter 24 he runs to the spring at the sight of the ring and bracelets, welcomes Abraham's servant, and with Bethuel says the matter is from the LORD and lets Rebekah go. In Chapter 29 he welcomes Jacob as his bone and flesh, offers wages, deceives him by giving Leah instead of Rachel, and secures seven more years of service. In Chapter 30 he asks Jacob to stay, having divined that the LORD blessed him for Jacob's sake, agrees to Jacob's wages, then removes the marked animals to his sons, setting three days' distance between them. In Chapter 31 his favor turns; he pursues the fleeing Jacob seven days to Gilead, is warned by God in a dream, accuses Jacob of stealing his gods, finds nothing, proposes a covenant, blesses his children, and returns home.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 24

Laban, Rebekah's brother, runs to the spring after seeing the ring and bracelets and hearing his sister's words. He welcomes the servant into the house and provides for the camels, listens to his message, and with Bethuel says the matter proceeds from the LORD and gives Rebekah to go.

Chapter 29

Laban, Rebekah's brother and Jacob's mother's brother, welcomes Jacob to his house, calls him his bone and flesh, and offers him wages. He deceives Jacob by bringing Leah to him instead of Rachel, justifying it by saying the younger is not given before the firstborn, and secures seven more years of service from Jacob. He also gives servants Zilpah to Leah and Bilhah to Rachel.

Chapter 30

Laban asks Jacob to stay because he has divined that the LORD blessed him for Jacob's sake, and agrees to Jacob's proposed wages. He then has the streaked, spotted, and black animals removed and given to his sons, setting three days' journey between himself and Jacob.

Chapter 31

Laban's face is no longer favorable toward Jacob, and he pursues the fleeing Jacob for seven days to Gilead with his relatives. Warned by God in a dream not to speak to Jacob either good or bad, he nonetheless accuses Jacob of deceit and of stealing his gods, searches the tents without finding them, and then proposes a covenant. He kisses and blesses his sons and daughters and returns to his place.
