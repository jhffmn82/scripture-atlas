Proverbs

Abstract written by a model

These records cover the whole Book of Proverbs, chapters 1 to 31, a collection of wisdom sayings and instructions. It opens by naming the proverbs of Solomon, son of David, and stating their purpose: to give wisdom, instruction and prudence to the simple and young. The central theme is that the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, and that wisdom brings life while folly brings ruin. Chapters 1 to 9 are mainly a father's speeches to his son. He urges him to heed instruction, avoid violent sinners, and guard himself against the strange woman or adulteress, whose house leads to death and Sheol. Wisdom is personified as a woman who calls in the streets and gates, promises security and life, and in chapter 8 describes herself as present with the LORD at creation. Chapter 9 sets Wisdom's feast against the foolish woman's invitation, which leads to the depths of Sheol. Chapters 10 to 22 form a long series of short, contrasting sayings about the righteous and the wicked, the wise and the fool, and the diligent and the lazy. They treat speech, correction, wealth and poverty, honest weights, kings and justice, family discipline, and friendship. The LORD is shown as weighing hearts, directing steps, and ruling over human plans. Chapter 22 shifts toward a section of counsel from the wise, claiming thirty sayings so that trust may be in the LORD. Chapters 23 to 24 return to warnings addressed to a son: restraint with rulers, avoiding greed, prostitutes and drunkenness, not envying evil men, rescuing those led to death, and fearing the LORD and the king. Chapter 24 ends with the sluggard's overgrown field. Chapters 25 to 29 are described as further proverbs of Solomon copied by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah. They cover kings, neighbors, fools, sluggards, gossips, boasting, friendship, and the contrast between wicked and righteous rulers, ending with the claim that justice comes from the LORD. The last two chapters are attributed to others. Chapter 30 gives the words of Agur son of Jakeh, who confesses ignorance, affirms that every word of God is flawless, prays to be kept from both poverty and riches, and offers numerical sayings. Chapter 31 gives the words of King Lemuel, taught by his mother, who warns against women and wine and urges just judgment for the poor. The book closes with a poem praising a worthy woman who works diligently, provides for her household, and gives to the poor, concluding that charm and beauty are vain but a woman who fears the LORD shall be praised.

Proverbs / Chapter 1

Proverbs chapter 1 opens by naming the proverbs of Solomon, son of David, king of Israel, and stating their purpose: to give wisdom, instruction, prudence and understanding to the simple and the young, and to increase the learning of the wise. It declares that the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge while fools despise wisdom and instruction. A father urges his son to heed parental teaching and refuse sinners who entice him to join in ambush, bloodshed and plunder, warning that such greed destroys those who pursue it. Wisdom then calls aloud in the streets and city gates, rebuking the simple, mockers and fools for refusing her counsel, and warns that disaster will come and she will not answer them. She promises that whoever listens to her will dwell securely and without fear of harm.

Chapter 2

Chapter 2 is addressed to 'my son' and sets out a conditional path to wisdom: if he receives the speaker's words, seeks understanding like silver and hidden treasure, then he will understand the fear of the LORD and find the knowledge of God. The LORD is the giver of wisdom, knowledge and understanding, and acts as a shield and guard for the upright who walk in integrity. Wisdom and discretion will protect the son by delivering him from evil men who forsake uprightness and delight in perverseness, and from the strange woman, a foreigner who flatters, forsakes the friend of her youth and forgets the covenant of her God. Her house leads to death and none who go to her return. The chapter closes by urging the son to walk with good men, since the upright will dwell in the land while the wicked and treacherous will be cut off and rooted out.

Chapter 3

In Chapter 3, a father figure addresses his son, urging him to hold onto his teaching, kindness, and truth, and promising long life, peace, and favor in return. The son is told to trust the LORD fully rather than his own understanding, to honor the LORD with his substance, and to accept the LORD's discipline as a father's correction. The chapter praises wisdom as more valuable than silver, gold, or rubies, a tree of life that brings long days, riches, honor, and peace, and credits it with the LORD's founding of the earth. The son is then told to keep wisdom so he may walk securely and sleep without fear, and to do good to his neighbor, avoid needless strife, and not envy violent men. It closes by contrasting how the LORD treats the perverse, wicked, and mockers with how he treats the upright, righteous, and humble, and the wise inherit glory while fools receive shame.

Chapter 4

In Chapter 4, a father urges his sons to heed his instruction and not forsake his law, recalling that his own father taught him to get wisdom and understanding at any cost. He promises that wisdom, if loved and embraced, will preserve, exalt, and honor them, and that instruction will lengthen their lives and keep their steps unhindered. He warns them away from the path of the wicked, who cannot sleep unless they do evil and who live on wickedness and violence. He contrasts this with the path of the righteous, which is like growing dawn light, while the way of the wicked is darkness in which they stumble unknowingly. He closes by telling his son to guard his heart, keep his mouth and lips free of corruption, look straight ahead, and turn neither right nor left but away from evil.

Chapter 5

In Chapter 5, a father addresses his son (and sons), urging him to heed his wisdom and keep discretion. He warns that an adulteress's speech is smooth as honey and oil but ends bitter and deadly, leading to death and Sheol, and he tells the son to stay far from her house lest he lose his honor, years, and wealth to strangers. He pictures the son groaning in the end, lamenting that he despised instruction and ignored his teachers. He then urges faithfulness, comparing marital love to drinking from one's own cistern and well, and telling the son to rejoice in the wife of his youth and be captivated by her love. He closes by stating that the LORD sees all human paths, and that the wicked are ensnared by their own sin and die for lack of instruction.

Chapter 6

Chapter 6 gives a father's warnings to his son on four dangers. The son is told to free himself at once if he has pledged collateral for a neighbor or stranger, and to learn diligence from the ant, in contrast to the sluggard whose sleep brings poverty. It describes the worthless person with a perverse mouth who sows discord and faces sudden calamity, and lists six things, indeed seven, that the LORD hates. It closes by urging the son to keep his father's commandment and his mother's teaching so they guard him from the immoral woman, and warns that adultery with a neighbor's wife brings burning, disgrace, and a husband's unrelenting vengeance.

Chapter 7

In Chapter 7, a father addresses his son and urges him to keep his words and commandments, to treat them as precious, and to call wisdom his sister so that she will protect him from the strange woman. The speaker then describes watching from his lattice window as a young man void of understanding walks toward the strange woman's house at twilight. She meets him dressed as a prostitute, kisses him, and persuades him with talk of her peace offerings, her perfumed bed, and her husband being away on a long journey until the full moon. The young man follows her at once, like an ox to slaughter, and the speaker warns his sons not to let their hearts turn to her ways, since her house is the way to Sheol and the rooms of death.

Chapter 8

In Chapter 8, wisdom is personified as a woman who calls out at high places, city gates, and crossroads to the simple, the fools, and all the sons of mankind. She says her words are true, righteous, and better than silver, gold, or rubies, and that she hates evil, pride, and arrogance. She claims that kings and princes rule by her, that she loves those who love her, and that she gives riches, honor, and enduring wealth. She describes herself as possessed by the LORD at the beginning, born before the depths, mountains, and earth, and present as a craftsman and delight by his side when he established the heavens, the sea's boundary, and the earth's foundations. She closes by urging her sons to listen and watch at her gates, saying that whoever finds her finds life and favor from the LORD, while those who sin against her wrong their souls and those who hate her love death.

Chapter 9

Chapter 9 personifies Wisdom, who builds her house with seven pillars, prepares meat and wine, sets her table, and sends out her maidens to call the simple and those void of understanding to eat her bread, leave their simple ways, and live. The chapter then gives teaching on how mockers and wicked people respond to correction compared with wise and righteous people, who grow in wisdom and learning. It states that the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom and that being wise or mocking falls on the individual. In contrast, the foolish woman, loud, undisciplined, and ignorant, sits at her door in the high places of the city and calls the same simple passersby with the claim that stolen water is sweet. The chapter ends by warning that her guests do not know they are among the departed spirits in the depths of Sheol.

Chapter 10

Chapter 10 opens the proverbs of Solomon, a series of contrasting sayings about the righteous and the wicked, the wise and the foolish. It contrasts diligence with laziness, honest and restrained speech with slander, violence and chatter, and shows the LORD blessing and protecting the righteous while the wicked lose hope, memory and a place in the land. Wealth and poverty are treated both as results of work and as sources of security or ruin. Love covers wrongs while hatred stirs strife, and the fear of the LORD prolongs life while the years of the wicked are shortened. Wisdom is shown in accepting correction and commandments and in careful, life-giving speech.

Chapter 11

Chapter 11 is a series of proverbs contrasting the righteous with the wicked. It opens with the LORD's abomination of false balances and delight in accurate weights, and it sets humility and wisdom against pride and shame. The righteous are said to be guided by integrity, delivered from trouble and death, and repaid on earth, while the wicked are destroyed by their own perverseness, trapped by evil desires and left with hollow hopes. The chapter also covers the effect of each group on the city, warns against gossip, collateral for strangers and trust in riches, and contrasts those who scatter generously with those who withhold.

Chapter 12

Chapter 12 is a series of contrasting proverbs about the righteous and the wicked, the wise and the fool, and the diligent and the lazy. It says that those who love correction love knowledge, while those who hate reproof are stupid. It teaches that the righteous will be established, rescued, and kept from trouble, while the wicked are overthrown and filled with evil. It stresses the power of speech: lying lips are an abomination to the LORD, truthful words last forever, and the tongue of the wise heals. It also praises hard work, which brings bread and rule, and condemns laziness and chasing fantasies.

Chapter 13

Chapter 13 is a series of proverbs contrasting the wise, righteous, and diligent with scoffers, the wicked, fools, and the sluggard. It teaches that listening to instruction, rebuke, and correction brings honor, reward, and life, while refusing them brings poverty, shame, and ruin. It addresses the guarding of one's speech, the pursuit of desire and longing, and the nature of wealth, which is dwindling if gained dishonestly and growing if gathered steadily. It also observes that some pretend to be rich or poor, that companions shape a person's character, and that a father who loves his son disciplines him. The chapter closes by contrasting the satisfied righteous with the hungry wicked.

Chapter 14

Chapter 14 contrasts the wise and the foolish, the upright and the wicked, through a series of proverbs. The wise build, think carefully, shun evil, and are slow to anger, while fools tear down, deceive, mock, and act recklessly. The upright fear the LORD and find a secure refuge, while the house of the wicked is overthrown. The chapter also addresses the treatment of the poor, saying that despising a neighbor is sin, while kindness to the needy honors their Maker. It closes with the claims that righteousness exalts a nation, sin disgraces a people, and the king favors a wise servant but is wrathful toward one who shames.

Chapter 15

Chapter 15 of Proverbs contrasts the wise, the righteous, and the upright with fools, the wicked, and the proud through short sayings. It commends gentle answers, timely words, a cheerful heart, humility, and the fear of the LORD, while warning against harsh words, anger, greed, and scorning correction. The LORD is described as watching everywhere, detesting the sacrifices, ways, and thoughts of the wicked, delighting in the prayer of the upright, and hearing the righteous while being far from the wicked. Those who heed reproof are said to live and gain understanding, while those who refuse it despise their own souls and face death.

Chapter 16

Chapter 16 is a collection of proverbs about the relationship between human planning and the LORD's sovereignty, the conduct of kings, and the contrast between wisdom and pride. The LORD is portrayed as weighing motives, directing steps, ordering all things for their end, and deciding the outcome of the lot. Kings are described as those whose throne rests on righteousness, who delight in truthful lips, and whose wrath or favor means death or life. The chapter praises getting wisdom and understanding above gold and silver, and warns that pride precedes destruction and a fall. It also describes the righteous, the wise, and the wicked through their speech and ways, ending with the claim that ruling one's spirit is better than taking a city.

Chapter 17

Chapter 17 is a series of proverbs contrasting wise and foolish conduct, with attention to strife, justice, family, and speech. It states that quietness is better than a feast with strife, that the LORD tests hearts, and that those who justify the wicked or condemn the righteous are an abomination to him. It describes the fool as lacking understanding, grieving his parents, and unable to use money to buy wisdom, yet counted wise when silent. It praises the person of understanding, who responds to rebuke, spares words, and keeps an even temper, and it commends covering offenses, steady friendship, and a cheerful heart. It warns that wicked people take bribes, seek rebellion, and repay evil for good, and that strife should be stopped early like a breach in a dam.

Chapter 18

Chapter 18 is a collection of proverbs contrasting the fool with the wise and the righteous, with a strong focus on the power of speech. It warns that the isolated man pursues selfishness and that a fool delights only in airing his own opinions, so his mouth brings strife, beatings, and destruction. It praises the LORD's name as a strong tower where the righteous find safety, in contrast with the rich man's imagined wall of wealth. It counsels humility, listening before answering, and seeking knowledge, and observes that the tongue holds the power of death and life. It closes on relationships: an offended brother is hard to win back, the poor plead while the rich answer harshly, and a true friend sticks closer than a brother.

Chapter 19

Chapter 19 gathers proverbs contrasting the poor who walk in integrity with fools, liars, and the lazy. It warns that false witnesses will be punished and perish, and that wealth and gifts attract friends while the poor are shunned by relatives and friends. It praises those who gain wisdom, take counsel, restrain anger, and overlook offenses, and notes that the LORD's counsel prevails over human plans. It urges disciplining a son, warns against a son who robs his parents or ignores instruction, and describes the sluggard who will not feed himself. It closes by stating that penalties and beatings are prepared for scoffers and fools.

Chapter 20

Chapter 20 is a collection of proverbs contrasting wisdom and folly in everyday conduct. It warns against wine and beer, quarreling, laziness, gossip, rash vows, and cursing one's parents, and it praises integrity, counsel, and faithfulness. Kings are described as fearsome figures who judge, winnow out the wicked, and are kept safe by love and faithfulness. The LORD is said to detest differing weights and measures, to have made the hearing ear and seeing eye, to direct a man's steps, and to be the one to wait for rather than taking revenge. The chapter also notes that the human spirit is the LORD's lamp and that the sluggard who will not plow in winter will have nothing at harvest.

Chapter 21

Chapter 21 is a series of proverbs contrasting the wicked with the righteous and the wise, and showing the LORD's sovereignty over human affairs. The LORD holds the king's heart, weighs the hearts of all, and prefers righteousness and justice to sacrifice. The wicked are shown as proud, violent, deceitful, and merciless, and they meet ruin, rejection of their sacrifices, and being ransoms for the righteous. The righteous and wise find joy in justice, life, honor, and treasure, while the sluggard, the pleasure-lover, and the contentious are warned against. The chapter ends by stating that no wisdom or counsel prevails against the LORD and that victory belongs to him, not to the prepared horse.

Chapter 22

Chapter 22 collects proverbs on reputation, wealth, and conduct. It values a good name over riches, notes that the LORD made both rich and poor, and warns that the rich rule the poor and the borrower serves the lender. It urges training a child in the right way, using discipline to drive out folly, and contrasts the prudent who avoid danger with the simple who suffer. The speaker then urges the reader to listen to the wise, claiming to have written thirty excellent things of counsel so that trust may be in the LORD. The chapter closes with commands not to exploit the poor, not to befriend the hot-tempered, not to co-sign debts, and not to move ancient boundary stones, and it praises the man skilled in his work who will serve kings.

Chapter 23

Chapter 23 is a series of warnings and exhortations addressed to a son. It cautions him to be restrained when dining with a ruler, to avoid the food of a stingy host, and not to wear himself out chasing wealth that flies away like an eagle. It urges him to respect boundary stones and the fatherless, to accept discipline and correction, and to buy truth and wisdom. It calls on him to make his father and mother glad, and to avoid prostitutes and the wayward wife. It ends with a long warning against wine and drunkenness, describing the woe, poverty, confusion, and craving they bring.

Chapter 24

Chapter 24 gathers proverbs urging the addressee, called 'my son', not to envy or join evil men, whose hearts plot violence, and instead to build life on wisdom, knowledge, and wise counsel. It calls for rescuing those led to death, warns that the one who weighs hearts will repay each person according to his work, and forbids gloating over an enemy's fall or taking revenge. The righteous man falls seven times and rises again, while the wicked are overthrown by calamity and their lamp is snuffed out. The son is told to fear the LORD and the king, to judge impartially, and to prepare his fields before building his house. The chapter ends with a narrator passing the sluggard's overgrown field and vineyard and learning that a little sleep brings poverty like a robber.

Chapter 25

Chapter 25 opens with proverbs of Solomon that the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out. It contrasts God's glory in concealing a matter with the glory of kings in searching it out, and says the hearts of kings are unsearchable. It advises how to behave before the king, removing the wicked from his presence and not exalting oneself, and how to treat a neighbor: don't rush to court, don't betray confidences, and don't visit too often. It praises fitting words, wise reproof, faithful messengers, patience, and a soft tongue, while warning against boasting, false testimony, unreliable confidants, and singing to a heavy heart. It ends with kindness to a hungry or thirsty enemy, and images of restraint, such as not eating too much honey and a man without self-control being like a broken-down city.

Chapter 26

Chapter 26 is a series of proverbs about fools, sluggards, and those who harm others with words. It opens with the fool, who is unfit for honor, needs a rod for his back, and is paired with contradictory advice about whether to answer him according to his folly. Honoring a fool, sending a message by him, or hiring him is compared to harmful or useless acts, and he repeats his folly like a dog returning to its vomit. The man wise in his own eyes is said to be less hopeful than a fool, and the sluggard is mocked for his excuses about a lion, his turning in bed like a door on hinges, and his laziness at the dish. The chapter ends with warnings about meddlers, deceivers who say they are joking, whisperers, contentious men who kindle strife, and malicious men who hide evil behind charming speech but will be exposed in the assembly and fall into their own pits.

Chapter 27

Chapter 27 is a series of proverbs on boasting, praise, relationships, and prudent living. It warns against boasting about tomorrow and praising oneself, and says a fool's provocation is heavier than stone or sand. It values faithful friends, open rebuke, and a near neighbor over a distant brother, and notes that iron sharpens iron and that a contentious wife is like continual dripping. It also observes that Sheol and a man's eyes are never satisfied, that a fool cannot be cured of foolishness even when ground in a mortar, and that a man is refined by his praise. It closes by urging the reader to know the state of his flocks and herds, since riches do not last forever, and describes how they supply clothing, land, and food for the household and servant girls.

Chapter 28

Chapter 28 contrasts the wicked and the righteous through a series of proverbs. The wicked flee without pursuit, lead lands into disorder, and fall into their own traps, while the righteous are bold as lions and are kept safe. Wicked rulers are likened to roaring lions and charging bears over helpless people, whereas rulers who hate ill-gotten gain have long days. Those who keep the law are called wise, and those who turn from it have even their prayers rejected. The chapter also weighs poverty against riches, valuing the poor person of integrity over the perverse rich, warning against hasty or oppressive pursuit of wealth, and promising no lack to those who give to the poor.

Chapter 29

Chapter 29 of Proverbs contrasts the righteous and the wicked, the wise man and the fool, and the effects of each on society. It warns that those who stiffen their necks against rebuke will be destroyed, and that people rejoice when the righteous thrive but groan when the wicked rule. It describes the duties of a king, who stabilizes the land by justice and fairly judging the poor but ruins it by taking bribes or listening to lies. It urges correcting children and servants, warns against anger, haste in speech, pride, and the fear of man, and says that those who trust the LORD are kept safe and that justice comes from the LORD.

Chapter 30

Chapter 30 presents the words of Agur the son of Jakeh, who addresses Ithiel and Ucal, confessing his own ignorance and asking who has ascended to heaven and established the earth. He affirms that every word of God is flawless and warns against adding to it, then prays to be kept from falsehood and from both poverty and riches. He describes several wicked generations that curse parents, are self-righteous, haughty, or devour the poor. The chapter then lists numerical sayings about things never satisfied, things too amazing to understand, things that make the earth tremble, small but wise creatures, and stately marchers. It closes by urging restraint of foolish pride and noting that forcing wrath produces strife.

Chapter 31

Chapter 31 opens with the words of King Lemuel, which his mother taught him as a revelation. She warns him not to give his strength to women or to drink wine and strong drink, lest he forget the law and pervert justice for the afflicted, and she urges him to speak for the mute and judge righteously for the poor and needy. The chapter then turns to a poem praising a worthy woman, who works eagerly with wool, flax, a field and vineyard, and merchandise, rises early to feed her household and servant girls, and gives generously to the poor. Her husband trusts her and is respected in the gates among the elders, and her children and husband praise her as excelling all other women. The chapter ends by declaring that charm and beauty are vain but a woman who fears the LORD shall be praised, and that her works should praise her in the gates.
