Abstract written by a model

In Sutrakritanga (Sûtrakritâṅga), Book I, Karman is described as an evil force and accumulated consequence of actions that causes suffering, rebirth, and bondage, and is destroyed through renunciation, austerity, restraint, purity, and abstention from sin. In Sutrakritanga, Book II, demerit is described as bad Karman present in sinful people through harmful, impure, and unrighteous conduct, causing hell and continued wandering through Samsâra. In Sutrakritanga, Book II, bad Karman is described as accruing from sinful and harmful conduct, including violence, lying, theft, wrath, pride, deceit, greed, and harmful occupations or actions against living beings and property. In Sutrakritanga, Book II, Karman is described as a binding force acquired through activity that contacts the soul, determines beings’ existence, form, lifespan, and mobility, and is annihilated through careful religious conduct and avoidance of sinful actions. In Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Karman is described as the force produced by sinful thoughts and actions that determines births, misery, and future existence, and is overcome through careful conduct, penance, self-control, austerity, and renunciation. In Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Karman is described as an eightfold cause of bondage, suffering, and transmigration whose influx can be prevented and which can be destroyed through observance of the Law, self-purification, control, austerity, renunciation, and meditation. In Uttaradhyayana Sutra, bad Karman is described as being stopped by prescribed abstentions and virtues, while austerities gradually destroy the Karman previously acquired by a monk. In Kalpa Sutra, fourfold Karman is described as including Vedanîya and as being suppressed and exhausted by Mahâvîra through exertion and meditation before his final liberation.

Karman in Sutrakritanga (Sûtrakritâṅga), Book I

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where Karman first appears

Abstract written by a model

Karman is an evil force and the accumulated consequence of actions. It increases through killing, causing or approving killing, greed, pride, deceit, wrath, impure thought, violence, carelessness, passions, and sinful acts; it is not acquired when new acts are avoided. It impels wrongdoing even when its consequences are known, causes suffering through the fruit of works, determines punishment when bad Karman ripens in hell, and keeps beings in the Circle of Births and Samsâra. It has six sources, though the Akriyâvâdins deny it. The wise understand its influx and stoppage. Karman is removed through renunciation that knows the value of life, and annihilated through control, austerities, detachment, penance, restraint, purity, and abstention from sinful actions. A monk who controls actions and senses, avoids violence, and suppresses Karman and will can destroy bad Karman. Mahâvîra annihilated all his Karman through knowledge, virtue, faith, and austerity. The Great Hero without Karman is neither born again nor dies.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

11 sections; each heading opens the text

Sutrakritanga (Sûtrakritâṅga), Book I / First Book. / First Lecture, Called the Doctrine. / First Chapter. .. Fourth Chapter.

Karman increases through killing, causing killing, approving killing, greed, pride, deceit, wrath, and impure thought. A person becomes free from Karman by abandoning passions and living with restraint and purity.

Second Lecture, Called the Destruction of Karman. / First Chapter. .. Third Chapter.

Karman causes living beings to experience the consequences of their own deeds and to remain in the Circle of Births. It is annihilated through control, austerities, detachment, and abstention from sinful actions.

Fourth Lecture, Called Knowledge of Women. / First Chapter. .. Second Chapter.

Karman is said to impel people to do wrong even when they know the consequences and have learned what has been demonstrated in the Strîvêda.

Fifth Lecture, Called Description of the Hells. / First Chapter. .. Second Chapter.

Bad Karman takes effect through the torments inflicted on sinners and through the suffering of those whose bad Karman has ripened. It determines the consequences endured for harmful deeds.

Sixth Lecture, Called Praise of Mahâvîra.

Mahâvîra annihilated all his Karman through knowledge, virtue, faith, and austerity. He also practised austerities for the removal of pain and conquered the passions that defile the soul.

Seventh Lecture, Called Description of the Wicked.

Harming living beings and committing sins acquire Karman. Karman causes suffering and repeated existence in the Samsâra, and a monk should annihilate it.

Eighth Lecture, Called on Exertion.

Karman arises from carelessness and passions. A pious monk annihilates his bad Karman, while a man who guards himself and subdues his senses abhors sins connected with past, present, and future Karman.

Tenth Lecture, Called Carefulness.

Karman accumulates through violent deeds. On decease, one who has accumulated it meets with really distressing misery.

Twelfth Lecture, Called the Creed.

It has six sources, and the Akriyâvâdins deny it. The wise understand its influx and stoppage, while pious men annihilate its effects by abstention from works.

Thirteenth Lecture, Called the Real Truth.

Karman subjects a wrathful or quarrelsome person to harm and passion. A monk should suppress his Karman and will, and knowing the truth about movable and immovable beings should exert himself.

Fifteenth Lecture, Called the Yamakas.

Karman is not acquired by one who undertakes no new acts. Control destroys Karman, and the Great Hero who has no Karman is not born again and does not die.

Karman in Sutrakritanga, Book II

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where Karman first appears

Abstract written by a model

Karman is portrayed as a binding force, represented by water in a lotus-pool, that is acquired through activity and comes into contact with the soul. It brings beings into existence, determines their form and duration of life, actuates and changes them, and helps determine whether they are movable or immovable. Sinful actions acquire Karman that generally bears beings downward into hell, while residual good Karman can lead meritorious beings among the gods. Beings continue wandering through sentient and senseless bodies when they fail to shake off, annihilate, or destroy it. Yet Karman exists with effects that can be experienced and annihilated; bondage occurs through it. Monks seek its annihilation by avoiding killing, acquisition, sinful actions, improper food, passions, and wrong opinions. Careful religious activity allows it to arise and be destroyed across three moments, while Mahâvîra acquires no new Karman and annihilates old Karman. Avoiding it is part of the blessed life of Sramanas.

Karman in Uttaradhyayana Sutra

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where Karman first appears

Abstract written by a model

Karman is the force produced by sinful thoughts and by living beings’ actions. It causes births in different forms, misery, separation, and a good or bad next existence; it follows its doer and must be borne alone. It drives the careless soul through Samsâra and burdens the ignorant sinner at death. The lectures present its overcoming through careful conduct, non-injury, penance, ascetic practice, right exertion, self-control, tranquillity, austerities, and renunciation. An ascetic sustains the body only to annihilate Karman, while Harikêsa treats it as fuel for an inner sacrifice and Nami likens it to a foe’s mail pierced by penance. As Karman ceases, beings may attain a pure state and birth as men; when its remnant is shaken off, they may become an eternal Siddha.

Hermann Jacobi · the work’s page · read it where Karman first appears

Abstract written by a model

Karman is a powerful, eightfold cause of bondage, suffering, worldly existence, birth and death. It binds souls to transmigration and is connected with merit and demerit: it may be meritorious or sinful, while bad Karman arises from love, hatred, delusion, and sinful, uncontrolled, or worldly activity. Originating in delusion, it also produces love and hatred, six Lêsyâs, and the conditions by which the soul is affected by sins. Acquired across millions of births, its eight kinds keep the soul turning in the Circle of Births, with their subdivisions, atoms, places, durations, and development explained. Yet its influx through bad channels can be prevented, and it can be destroyed by observance of the Law, self-purification, conduct, control, study, austerities, renunciation, concentration, pure meditation, self-control, and penance. With no bad influx and through dispassion, the soul annihilates Karman and becomes exempt from transmigration.
