Abstract written by a model

In Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra), Book I, life fades through aging, sickness, and sensory decline, yet all beings hold it dear. In Uttaradhyayana Sutra, human life is finite and precarious, urging virtue, self-conquest, repentance, and practice of the Law. In The Tao Teh King, or The Tao and Its Characteristics, life is valued above fame and wealth, with contentment and restraint supporting longevity. In Chuang Tzŭ, life is a bounded, destined span shaped by natural transformation, which should be preserved through moderation and care for body and vitality. In Mencius, Book VI (Gaozi), life is valued below righteousness and should not be preserved through improper means.

human life in Uttaradhyayana Sutra

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where human life first appears

Abstract written by a model

Human life is finite, fleeting, and precarious: it cannot be prolonged indefinitely and soon ends like a fallow leaf or dew-drop. Compared to capital entrusted to a merchant, it can be restored or increased through virtue, but lost through sin; its brevity urges conquering the will, wiping off sins, and practising the Law.

Tenth Lecture.

Human life is compared to a fallow leaf and a dew-drop, because it comes to its close quickly and lasts only a short time. Its fleeting and precarious nature is a reason to wipe off sins and practise the Law.

life in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where life first appears

Abstract written by a model

Life is a bounded, destined span: a quickening of the physical body by the soul, granted when its time comes and ending in death. It is likened to a loan that adds dust and dirt to existence, one phase in transformations that returns to the great Scheme. Life comes and goes beyond human control; coming cannot be declined and going cannot be stopped. Because life is limited while knowledge is limitless, pursuit of the limitless through the limited is fatal. Its proper course is to avoid fame and disgrace, follow a middle way, respect natural principles, and care for both the physical frame and inner vitality. TAO gives life form, toil in manhood, repose in old age, and rest in death. Attachment to life may be a delusion, yet preserving it matters more than empire, wealth, power, office, and gain.

Love of life may be a delusion, and those who cling to life may later regret it. The text compares attachment to life with a dream from which awakening changes one's understanding.

CHAPTER III.

Life has a limit, while knowledge is without limit, and pursuing the limitless through the limited is fatal. Life should be preserved by avoiding fame and disgrace, following a middle course, and respecting natural principles.

CHAPTER VI.

Life is an allotted span in which a person should complete his course without perishing in mid-career. TAO gives life its form, toil in manhood, repose in old age, and rest in death.

Nan Po Tzŭ K'uei

Life is described as the backbone of existence and as a state granted when its time has come. TAO gives life its form, toil in manhood, repose in old age, and rest in death.

CHAPTER XVIII.

Life is described as a loan in which the borrower adds more dust and dirt to existence. It is presented as one phase in a sequence of transformations and as something that returns to the great Scheme.

CHAPTER XIX.

Life comes and goes beyond human control, so nourishing the physical frame cannot by itself preserve it. The text says that life is sustained through simultaneous care of the body and the inner vitality.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

Preservation of life is treated as more important than the empire, territory, office, wealth, and political success. Those who respect life avoid sacrificing it to worldly gain, while those who value gain wear out their bodies and lives.
