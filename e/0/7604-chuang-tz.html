Chuang Tzŭ

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

This unit is an introduction and extended presentation of Chuang Tzŭ’s writings, set against Lao Tzŭ, Confucius, the Confucian and Mihist schools, and later philosophical comparisons with Heracleitus, Socrates, Aristotle, and Christianity. It explains the disputed origins and transmission of Chuang Tzŭ and The Tao-Tê-Ching, while rejecting their treatment as sacred books. Its central theme is TAO: the formless, eternal, unconditioned principle underlying heaven, earth, God, nature, life, death, and all change. Inaction, repose, spontaneity, inward freedom, and acceptance of destiny are repeatedly opposed to coercion, fixed judgment, ambition, reputation, moral schemes, knowledge, ceremony, government, and artificial social distinctions. The work begins with transcendental freedom, relativity, the unity of contraries, and the uncertainty of perception, notably in Chuang Tzŭ’s butterfly dream. It develops these ideas through parables of immense creatures, useless trees, artisans, cooks, deformity, illness, mourning, dreams, animals, rivers, rulers, sages, and wanderers. Confucius often appears as a learner, critic, or representative of practical moral teaching; Lao Tzŭ, Chuang Tzŭ, and figures such as the Yellow Emperor more often articulate detachment and harmony with TAO. Chapters on government argue that rulers should perfect themselves and govern invisibly, allowing people and nature to flourish without force, laws, rewards, punishment, war, or moral imposition. The text repeatedly revalues usefulness: what is useless, weak, obscure, or physically imperfect may escape exploitation and possess deeper virtue. Later chapters stress preserving life rather than pursuing office, wealth, empire, fame, or debate; accepting death as transformation; and recognizing that language and knowledge cannot finally contain TAO. It closes by surveying competing schools and praising Kuan Yin, Lao Tzŭ, and Chuang Tzŭ as fuller interpreters of the universal principle, while presenting Hui Tzŭ as a brilliant but ultimately incomplete dialectician.

_Introduction._

This unit introduces Chuang Tzŭ, a thinker of the feudal age whose allegorical writings followed Lao Tzŭ and challenged the Confucian and Mihist schools. It explains Lao Tzŭ’s teachings about TAO, or The Way, and especially Inaction, contrasting them with Confucius’s practical ethics and materialism. It discusses the disputed origins, transmission, editing, and classification of Chuang Tzŭ’s work and The Tao-Tê-Ching. It also argues that neither work should properly be treated as a sacred book.

_Note on the Philosophy of Chaps._ i-vii.

The Note on the Philosophy of Chaps. i-vii., written by the REV. AUBREY MOORE, compares Chuang Tzŭ’s philosophy with Greek philosophy, especially the thought of Heracleitus. It discusses relativity, the limitations of sense knowledge, the Identity of Contraries, and the movement from idealism toward mysticism. It presents Tao as an eternal principle comparable in some respects to Heracleitus’s Λόγος, while contrasting Taoism with Confucianism and relating Taoist inwardness to Christianity. The note also considers the roles of Aristotle, Socrates, Emperor Yao, Confucius, and Lao Tzŭ in these philosophical comparisons.

The triumph of Confucianism in the centuries which intervened between

The unit examines the relationship between Confucianism and Taoism, especially how Confucius is portrayed in the writings of Chuang Tzŭ and Lieh Tzŭ. It proposes that Lao Tzŭ and Confucius may have been philosophically closer than later Taoism and Confucianism, with one emphasizing the ideal and inward TAO and the other practical social duties. It argues that the two traditions diverged over time, with Confucianism becoming established and Taoism responding as a mystical and dissenting movement. The writer also notes a possible parallel between Chuang Tzŭ's teaching and Heracleitus, while acknowledging dependence on translation and the risk of Western interpretive bias.

CHAPTER I.

The chapter presents transcendental bliss as freedom from dependence on self, action, reputation, and worldly distinctions. It contrasts the vast journeys of the Leviathan and Rukh with the limited understanding of smaller creatures, showing that magnitude and knowledge are relative. Emperor Yao, Hsü Yu, Chien Wu, Lien Shu, Hui Tzŭ, and Chuang Tzŭ illustrate different attitudes toward government, reputation, usefulness, and uselessness. The chapter concludes that things considered useless, such as a large gourd or an irregular tree, may possess greater freedom and value when viewed differently.

CHAPTER II.

Tzŭ Ch'i of Nan-kuo explains to Yen Ch'êng Tzŭ Yu that the music of Heaven works through the natural distinctions and sounds of the universe. He argues that subjective and objective, affirmation and negation, and all contraries merge in TAO, whose axis lies where all distinctions become one. The true Sage follows Heaven by placing himself in subjective relation with external things, using the light of nature rather than fixed judgments. The unit also examines speech, mind, soul, Hui Tzŭ, and the rival claims of the Confucian and Mihist schools.

Of old, the Emperor Yao said to Shun, "I would smite the Tsungs, and

The Emperor Yao considers attacking the Tsungs, Kueis, and Hsü-aos, but Shun argues that virtue should prevail through its influence rather than force. Yeh Ch'üeh and Wang I debate whether anything can be known, questioning standards of habitat, taste, beauty, and virtue. Chang Wu Tzŭ explains the Sage's unity beyond opposites, including right and wrong, life and death, and identifies dependence on Another as dependence upon God. The butterfly dream of Chuang Tzŭ illustrates uncertainty about whether he is a man dreaming of being a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming of being a man.

CHAPTER III.

“Nourishment of the Soul” concerns the limits of life, the pursuit of knowledge, freedom, and the acceptance of death. Prince Hui’s cook explains how devotion to Tao and following natural openings preserve his chopper, teaching the Prince how to care for life. Lao Tzŭ’s death prompts Ch’in Shih to argue that birth and death occur according to eternal principles and should not provoke excessive grief. The unit concludes that a properly nourished soul may become immortal and return to the Great Unknown.

CHAPTER IV.

Chapter IV, “Man among men,” concerns how people should conduct themselves among others through passivity, self-reformation, and resignation to TAO. Yen Hui seeks to reform the Prince of Wei, but Confucius teaches him that direct moral intervention will fail and instructs him in the fasting of the heart. Tzŭ Kao receives similar advice before a mission to the State of Ch'i: he should serve without attachment to success or failure and follow Destiny and Duty. Yen Ho is advised by Chü Poh Yü to adapt outwardly while preserving his inner standard when tutoring the eldest son of Prince Ling of the Wei State.

A certain artisan was travelling to the Ch'i State. On reaching

An artisan dismisses a sacred li tree as worthless timber, but the tree explains in a dream that its uselessness preserved it from harm and enabled it to reach old age. Tzŭ Ch'i of Nan-poh sees a large tree on Shang mountain whose uselessness has allowed it to grow large. The unit contrasts useful trees and people, who are cut down, conscripted, or exploited, with useless or deformed ones who escape danger. Confucius encounters Chieh Yü, who warns that when TAO does not prevail, people should preserve themselves and that the use of useless things is not understood.

CHAPTER V.

Chapter V concerns the evidence of complete virtue, showing how inward virtue can produce outward influence while making outward form insignificant. Wang T'ai, Shên T'u Chia, No-toes, and Ai T'ai T'o are physically imperfect yet attract followers or command respect because of their inner virtue. Confucius teaches that the immortal soul endures beyond life and death, that all things are one, and that virtue arises from inner harmony with Destiny. The chapter concludes that the truly wise wear human forms without being ruled by human passions, following TAO and cultivating the divine.

CHAPTER VI.

The unit concerns God, Man, TAO, and the relationship between the human and the divine. It describes the pure men of old as acting spontaneously, accepting life and death without passion, and harmonizing with all creation. It teaches that life, death, birth, and transitions belong to destiny, while TAO is the ultimate, formless principle underlying heaven, earth, the universe, and all existence. It presents human form and the countless transitions beyond it as sources of joy because the truly wise look toward what cannot be lost.

Nan Po Tzŭ K'uei

Nan Po Tzŭ K'uei asks Nü Yü how he remains youthful, and Nü Yü explains that TAO was transmitted through progressive detachment from distinctions, the external world, and personal existence. Four friends accept Inaction, Life, and Death as the conditions of friendship, and their illnesses and deaths are treated as transformations governed by God, Nature, and TAO. Confucius explains to Tzŭ Kung that certain men live beyond conventional rules, identifying TAO as what sustains human beings. The unit also presents Mêng Sun Ts'ai's natural mourning, Yen Hui's gradual abandonment of charity, duty, ceremony, music, body, and mind, and Tzŭ Sang's reflection that poverty comes from Destiny.

CHAPTER VII.

This unit concerns how to govern through inaction, natural laws, and TAO rather than coercive laws and personal effort. Yeh Ch'üeh, Chien Wu, T'ien Kên, Yang Tzŭ Chü, and others learn that the truly wise ruler governs invisibly by perfecting himself and following natural order. Hu Tzŭ demonstrates different manifestations of TAO to Chi Han, leading Lieh Tzŭ to abandon artificiality and practice passivity. Shu and Hu kill Hun Tun by boring holes in him, illustrating the perils of action.

CHAPTER VIII.

The chapter argues that joined toes and extra fingers are natural but functionally superfluous additions, and uses them to criticize artificial charity and duty to one's neighbour. It presents TAO as natural perfection that should not be imposed through ceremonies, music, argument, or moral instruction. The chapter concerns Confucianism and claims that pursuing reputation, wealth, fame, or moral ideals can injure human nature. It concludes that true perfection lies in cultivating TAO and understanding oneself rather than imitating others.

CHAPTER IX.

This unit concerns horses, Poh Loh, the people, Sages, and the government of the empire. It contrasts horses’ natural lives with the harmful effects of branding, bridling, shackling, and other artificial management imposed by Poh Loh. It argues that government makes the same mistake when it suppresses natural instincts through charity, duty to one’s neighbour, music, and ceremonies. The text presents natural integrity and Tao as preferable to the systems introduced by artisans and Sages.

CHAPTER X.

The unit argues that artificial restrictions, measures, laws, and wisdom protect strong thieves rather than preventing theft. It concerns T'ien Ch'êng Tzŭ's seizure of the State of Ch'i, Robber Chê's use of the Sages' principles, and the harmful effects of charity, duty, knowledge, and disputation. It concludes that abolishing imposed wisdom and restrictions would allow people to return to natural integrity and Tao.

CHAPTER XI.

The unit contrasts letting alone with government, arguing that coercion and over-refinement disturb mankind’s natural conditions and virtue. It presents Inaction and TAO as the means by which rulers can support the empire without interfering with the natural goodness of the human heart. The Yellow Emperor learns from Kuang Ch'êng Tzŭ that self-preservation, stillness, and harmony with heaven and earth lead toward perfect TAO. The Spirit of the Clouds learns from the Vital Principle that government causes disorder and that feeding the people with one’s heart and resting in inaction allows the world to flourish. The unit concludes by distinguishing the TAO of God from the TAO of man and describing how the perfect man and Sage act without premeditated interference.

CHAPTER XII.

This unit concerns the universe, TAO, and the relation of God, virtue, inaction, and all things. It describes how the empire was once governed through inaction and natural order, and defines the true Sage as one who embodies complete virtue and follows TAO. It contrasts spiritual realization through Nothing with the limitations of knowledge, sight, speech, intelligence, wealth, and worldly power. It also recounts teachings and encounters involving Lao Tzŭ, Yao, and others, explaining how cultivation of nature leads back to virtue and the unconditioned.

Chiang Lü Mien went to see Chi Ch'ê,

Chiang Lü Mien asks Chi Ch'ê about governing, and Chi Ch'ê explains that the Sage influences people according to their natural dispositions. Tzŭ Kung meets an old gardener who rejects a well-sweep as contrary to purity and Tao, then reports the encounter to Confucius. Chun Mang describes the government of the Sage, the man of perfect virtue, and the divine man to Yüan Fêng. Mên Wu Kuei and Ch'ih Chang Man Chi discuss the Great Yü, the Golden Age, the errors of the world, and the loss of original nature through sensory and social distractions.

CHAPTER XIII.

The chapter teaches that TAO operates ceaselessly through repose, stillness, and inaction, from which movement, order, and accomplishment arise. It explains that the Sage and wise ruler should model themselves on GOD, cultivating essentials rather than accidental forms such as ceremonies, laws, rewards, and punishments. Yao, Shun, Confucius, Lao Tzŭ, Shih Ch'êng Ch'i, Duke Huan, and a wheelwright illustrate the limits of human action, moral doctrines, written words, and knowledge that cannot be conveyed. The chapter concludes that TAO is vast, unconditioned, and not fully expressible through forms, names, books, or speech.

CHAPTER XIV.

This unit concerns TAO, virtue, charity, duty to one's neighbour, and the encounters among Chuang Tzŭ, Confucius, and Lao Tzŭ. Chuang Tzŭ teaches that perfect charity does not admit of love for the individual and that TAO does not admit of sub-division. Lao Tzŭ criticizes Confucius's reliance on charity, duty to one's neighbour, and the ancient canons, while Confucius eventually recognizes Lao Tzŭ's wisdom and later attains TAO. The Yin and Yang principles appear as part of the cosmic order and as a harmony expressed in perfect music.

CHAPTER XV.

The chapter concerns self-conceit, the Sage, and the way of TAO through passivity, repose, and freedom from worldly attachment. It rejects social withdrawal, moral preaching, government, bodily regimens, and other efforts as incomplete substitutes for spiritual calm. The Sage is described as passive, untroubled by happiness or misfortune, and guided by the laws of God. The chapter concludes that preserving pure spirituality enables unity with God and re-absorption into the eternity from which the soul came.

CHAPTER XVI.

The chapter concerns TAO, Virtue, and the decline of human harmony from primeval unity into coercion, government, intellectualism, and confusion. Primeval man lived in tranquillity, with knowledge arising from repose and all things existing as one without being deliberately made so. As Virtue declined, rulers introduced systems, moral reform, art, learning, and government, causing natural instinct and original integrity to be lost. The Sages and retired scholars preserved themselves by avoiding worldly display, remaining within their natural spheres, and seeking complete happiness rather than office.

CHAPTER XVII.

During autumn floods, the Spirit of the River reaches the ocean and learns from the Spirit of the Ocean that his former sense of greatness was limited. The Spirit of the Ocean explains that space and time are without limits, dimensions and values are relative, and TAO is the unconditioned principle in which all things are equal. The dialogue contrasts the natural and the artificial and teaches that virtue consists in following the natural without sacrificing it to will or fame. The unit concerns the Spirit of the River, the Spirit of the Ocean, TAO, and the changing relations among all things.

The walrus envies the centipede;

The walrus, centipede, snake, wind, eye, and mind illustrate how each being has different natural qualifications, while the wind says that excelling in great things belongs only to the Sages. Confucius remains cheerful when surrounded because he regards failure as fate, success as opportunity, and danger without fear. Kung Sun Lung seeks Mou of Wei’s explanation of Chuang Tzŭ’s grandeur, but is compared to a frog confined to an old well and leaves humbled. Chuang Tzŭ rejects office, rebukes Hui Tzŭ’s suspicion, and argues with Hui Tzŭ about whether he can know the pleasure of minnows from the bridge over the Hao.

CHAPTER XVIII.

The chapter asks whether perfect happiness exists and argues that the world’s pursuit of wealth, rank, comfort, and reputation does not secure it. It identifies inaction as true pleasure and connects it with the workings of Heaven and Earth, from which all changes arise. Chuang Tzŭ explains his acceptance of his wife’s death as a natural transformation, while stories of a hunchback, a skull, Confucius, and Lieh Tzŭ illustrate changing views of life, death, and happiness. The chapter also presents destiny as a pre-arrangement that limits what beings can become.

CHAPTER XIX.

The unit explains that life depends on nourishing both the physical body and the inner principle of vitality, while true perfection comes through harmony with TAO and God. Kuan Yin teaches Lieh Tzŭ that the perfect man achieves invulnerability through absolute purity, unity, and freedom from artificiality. Stories involving Confucius, skilled artisans, boatmen, fighters, and sages illustrate concentration, adaptation, inner security, and the need to care for both internal and external aspects of life. The unit concludes that natural spontaneity and freedom from self-conscious striving bring one near perfection.

CHAPTER XX.

Chuang Tzŭ reflects on usefulness and uselessness through a huge tree and a goose, concluding that only alignment with TAO escapes the troubles of mortality. I Liao advises the prince of Lu to discard worldly power and human hindrances, while Pei Kung Shê explains that non-interference enabled the bells to be completed. Confucius undergoes the siege between Ch'ên and Ts'ai, learns about adaptation, God, and the dangers of fame, and is counseled by Tzŭ Sang Hu. Chuang Tzŭ also learns from a strange bird and from Yang Tzŭ that self-conscious virtue, pursuit of gain, and forgetfulness of one's nature lead to trouble.

CHAPTER XXI.

The unit presents TAO as beyond words, formal morality, worldly distinctions, and deliberate cultivation, and as the source of inner freedom, happiness, and security amid change and death. T'ien Tzŭ Fang praises his tutor Tung Kuo Shun Tzŭ to Prince Wên of Wei, while Confucius, Lao Tzŭ, and others illustrate how TAO is recognized through conduct rather than speech. Episodes involving Wên Po Hsüeh Tzŭ, Duke Ai of Lu, Wên Wang, Lieh Yü K'ou, Sun Shu Ao, and the Princes of Ch'u and Fan show the effects of TAO or its absence on scholarship, government, courage, office, rank, and political survival.

CHAPTER XXII.

Knowledge asks Do-nothing Say-nothing, All-in-extremes, and the Yellow Emperor how TAO may be known, approached, and attained; the Yellow Emperor teaches that it is found through no thoughts, no following, and no pursuit, while knowledge itself is treated as a barrier. The unit presents TAO as omnipresent, formless, nameless, spontaneous, and beyond speech, while all things contain it and arise, change, and return through it. It describes the universe and creation as beautiful, ordered, and governed by principles that human intellect cannot fully reach. Through teachings attributed to the Yellow Emperor, Chuang Tzŭ, Lao Tzŭ, Confucius, and others, the unit contrasts external adaptation and activity with internal constancy and passivity.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Kêng Sang Ch'u, a disciple of Lao Tzŭ, benefits the people of Wei-lei but rejects their attempt to exalt him, fearing that this departs from TAO. Nan Yung Ch'u seeks guidance first from Kêng Sang Ch'u and then from Lao Tzŭ, who teaches him to preserve life by maintaining inner unity, repose, and freedom from disturbances. Lao Tzŭ describes TAO as the source of TÊ, life, Nature, and all divisions, while Non-Existence and existence are ultimately one. The unit also examines the subjective point of view, the obstacles to TAO, and the spontaneity required for virtue and action.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Hsü Wu Kuei visits Wu Hou of Wei and argues that passion, moral schemes, love for the people, and duty towards one's neighbour obstruct natural virtue and lead to disorder and war. He teaches that rulers should abstain from aggression and govern like the boy cares for horses, allowing natural conditions to remain unharmed. The Yellow Emperor meets the boy, who directs him toward TAO and teaches that government requires no more than preventing harm. Chuang Tzŭ disputes Hui Tzŭ and the competing schools over moral standards, then mourns the loss of Hui Tzŭ as a worthy interlocutor.

Kuan Chung being at the point of death, Duke Huan went to see him.

Kuan Chung advises Duke Huan to entrust the administration of the State to Hsi P'êng rather than Pao Yü, who would conflict with the prince and people. The prince of Wu learns from the monkeys that flaunting superiority invites destruction, while Yen Pu I studies under Tung Wu and abandons reputation. Tzŭ Chi of Nan-poh reflects on the dangers of reputation, worldly fortune, and unnatural success, while Confucius praises wordless utterances, inaction, and undivided TAO. Yeh Ch'üeh and Hsü Yu discuss the dangers of charity, duty to one's neighbour, wisdom, enthusiasm, and artificial interference with nature, concluding with reflections on knowledge and God.

CHAPTER XXV.

Tsê Yang seeks an audience with the prince of Ch'u but is advised that Kung Yüeh Hsiu, a reclusive man without virtue but with knowledge, may be more suited to influence him. The text describes the true Sage as one who acts through concealed virtue, treats all things as one, and remains in harmony with TAO. It contrasts rulers' violence and social disorder with the all-encompassing nature of TAO, using the dispute between Prince Hui of Wei and Ch'i State as an example. Tai Chin Jen, T'ai Kung Tiao, and others discuss society, material existence, and why both Chance and Predestination fail to explain the infinite.

CHAPTER XXVI.

The unit explains that external contingencies such as life, death, loyalty, filial piety, and worldly success are uncertain, while the inner self is the proper sphere of control. Chuang Tzŭ illustrates this through the stickleback, Jên Kung Tzŭ’s great fish, and discussions with Hui Tzŭ about usefulness, wisdom, and withdrawing from the world. Lao Lai Tzŭ advises Confucius to abandon dogmatism, while Confucius uses the fate of a divine tortoise to show that wisdom and spirituality have limits. The unit concludes that language is only a means to express ideas and should be set aside once the ideas are understood.

CHAPTER XXVII.

The unit examines language, distinguishing speech put into others’ mouths, speech based on authority, and natural speech that overflows in accord with God. It discusses the relation of possibilities, opposites, recurring forms, and Destiny, arguing that ultimate causes cannot be known. Chuang Tzŭ recounts Confucius’s changing opinions, while Hui Tzŭ is described as skilled in debate but not in winning hearts. Tsêng Tzŭ’s feelings about salaries are explained, Yen Ch'êng Tzŭ Yu describes his gradual spiritual development to Tung Kuo Tzŭ Chi, and Lao Tzŭ transforms Yang Tzŭ Chü’s overbearing manner through instruction.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

The unit concerns the value of preserving life and following TAO rather than pursuing power, wealth, office, or empire. Yao and Shun offer the empire to several people who decline it, while T'ai Wang Shan Fu abandons territory rather than let it injure life. Tzŭ Hua Tzŭ, Yen Ho, Lieh Tzŭ, Yüeh, Yüan Hsien, Tsêng Tzŭ, Yen Hui, and Confucius illustrate indifference to gain, hardship, and worldly success. T'ang's attack on Chieh and the rise of the Chou dynasty are judged by Pien Sui, Wu Kuang, Po I, and Shu Ch'i according to TAO and preservation of life.

CHAPTER XXIX.

Confucius visits Robber Chê to urge him to abandon violence and accept rulership, despite Liu Hsia Chi’s warning. Robber Chê angrily rejects Confucius’s teachings, condemns the doctrines of ancient rulers and Confucius’s failures, and argues that people should preserve their natural lives and desires. Confucius leaves humiliated and later admits to Liu Hsia Chi that he nearly put himself in danger by approaching the “tiger.”

Tzŭ Chang asked Man Kou Tê,

Tzŭ Chang and Man Kou Tê debate whether reputation, wealth, virtue, or confidence should guide human conduct. They argue over social distinctions, historical rulers, and whether moral principles accord with political success. Wu Yoh says that setting aside both wealth-seeking and reputation-seeking leads to the same result, and urges adherence to TAO. Discontent and Complacency then debate wealth, health, happiness, and the dangers of excessive ambition, with Complacency maintaining that contentment is the source of happiness.

CHAPTER XXX.

Wên Wang of Chao loves sword-play and keeps more than three thousand swordsmen, whose bouts leave many killed or wounded while the State of Chao declines. The Heir Apparent Li sends for Chuang Tzŭ to persuade the prince to dismiss the swordsmen. Chuang Tzŭ presents three symbolic swords and shames the prince for favoring the sword of the People over the swords of the Son of Heaven and the Princes. The prince accepts Chuang Tzŭ’s teaching, remains in his palace for three months, and the swordsmen die in their homes.

CHAPTER XXXI.

Confucius meets an old fisherman at Apricot Altar in the Black Forest and seeks instruction from him. The old fisherman criticizes Confucius for trying to regulate ceremonies, music, and human relationships while neglecting his own original purity. He explains the blemishes and obstacles that disrupt human affairs and teaches that TAO and unalloyed truth should guide conduct. Confucius recognizes the fisherman as a Sage and later explains to Tzŭ Lu why the fisherman deserved respect.

CHAPTER XXXII.

The unit concerns Lieh Yü K'ou, whose outward brightness and influence alarm him because they may attract authority and followers. It contrasts natural virtue and TAO with artificial learning, self-display, ambition, and reliance on external standards. Chuang Tzŭ criticizes Confucianism and Confucius as outward and artificial, describes tests of character, and rejects political office. The unit ends with Chuang Tzŭ accepting a natural burial and affirming that intuitive understanding surpasses dependence on the senses.

CHAPTER XXXIII.

The unit explains TAO as the universal principle that unites creation, supports government, and is embodied most fully by the true Sage. It contrasts the ancients and their ideals with specialists and schools whose partial doctrines fragment TAO. Mih Tzŭ and Ch'in Hua Li emphasize universal love and austerity; Sung Hsing and Yin Wên pursue peace and public welfare; P'êng Mêng, T'ien P'ien, and Shên Tao teach comprehensive passivity; Kuan Yin, Lao Tzŭ, and Chuang Tzŭ offer further interpretations of TAO. The text praises Kuan Yin, Lao Tzŭ, and Chuang Tzŭ while judging several other systems incomplete or impractical.

Hui Tzŭ was a man of many ideas. His works would fill five carts. But

This unit concerns Hui Tzŭ, his paradoxical doctrines, and his reputation as a subtle dialectician. He advanced numerous contradictory theses about space, time, objects, perception, and names, which dialecticians argued over without reaching an end. Although Hui Tzŭ claimed great wisdom and investigated all creation, the text judges him morally weak, physically violent, and lacking a sound system of TAO. It concludes that his talents brought him the reputation of a sophist rather than success in understanding TAO.
