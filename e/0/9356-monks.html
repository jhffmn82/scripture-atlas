Abstract written by a model

Monks are defined by careful self-control: they avoid harming living creatures, practise chastity, austerities, and the three Guptis, and remain free from passions. Their disciplined conduct produces only subtle îryapathika Karman, immediately destroyed. They abstain from specially prepared food and harmful speech. Through long ascetic practice they attain Kêvala, then absolute perfection, enlightenment, deliverance, and final beatitude. By contrast, uncontrolled monks harm others; those who eat seeds, use cold water, and beg for a living are reborn repeatedly and do not end the Circle of Births.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Second Lecture, Called on Activity.

Monks who carefully control their conduct and protect their souls from passions acquire only subtle îryapathika Karman, which is destroyed immediately. Some heretical monks are described as uncontrolled, indulgent, and willing to direct harm toward others.

Now the explanation of the second subject, viz. merit, is as follows:

Monks carefully avoid causing the death of living creatures, practise chastity, austerities, and the three Guptis, and remain free from anger, pride, deceit, and greed. After long ascetic practice, they attain Kêvala and absolute perfection, enlightenment, deliverance, and final beatitude.

Sixth Lecture, Called .

Monks are expected to control themselves, avoid injuring living beings, abstain from food specially prepared for them, and refrain from empty or harmful speech. Monks who eat seeds, use cold water, and beg for a living are said to be reborn repeatedly.
