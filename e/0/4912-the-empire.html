the Empire in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where the Empire first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Empire is a domain of paramount importance whose peace, unity, and proper administration depend on TAO, natural laws, inaction, virtue, and invisible influence. In its early or Golden Age, it was under the sovereignty of inaction and governed by wise rulers including Sui Jen, Fu Hsi, Shên Nung, Huang Ti, Yao, and Shun. Under Yao and Shun it had no failures: duties and officials accorded with TAO, people acted freely and virtuously, and the Empire rallied around the sovereign. Its unity is achieved when the ruler acts in accord with TAO and TAO spreads through the world while focused in the Middle Kingdom. Decline begins when music, ceremonies, charity, duty, knowledge, rewards, punishments, coercion, refinement, art, learning, and government systems are introduced or misused. These produce division, competition, force, instability, and confusion. The Empire can flourish again when rulers abandon imposed schemes and care for it naturally, like horses, allowing things to return to their original constitution. Although it is a supreme trust, it is less important than the body and only the surplus or dregs of TAO.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

10 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VII.

It is best governed through natural laws, inaction, and invisible influence rather than coercion or imposed artificiality.

CHAPTER IX.

The empire was divided against itself when Sages introduced music, ceremonies, charity, and duty to one’s neighbour. Its people later developed a taste for knowledge and competed for gain under the influence of these teachings.

CHAPTER XI.

The empire becomes unstable when rulers pursue rewards, punishments, knowledge, coercion, and excessive refinement. It can flourish when those in power rest in inaction and allow things to return to their original constitution.

CHAPTER XII.

The empire was once described as being under the sovereignty of inaction and TAO, with clear duties, proper officials, and harmony among all things. When rulers relied on reward and punishment instead of virtue, the decline of virtue and the reign of force began.

Chiang Lü Mien went to see Chi Ch'ê,

The empire is described as peaceful under the Sage when officials perform their functions, ability is employed, and the people's voice is heard. In the Golden Age, people acted freely and virtuously without conscious duties or conventions, leaving no trace in history.

CHAPTER XIII.

The empire rallies around the sovereign whose TAO operates ceaselessly. It is administered through the ruler’s inaction and the ruled’s action, and it becomes united when the ruler acts in accord with TAO.

CHAPTER XVI.

The empire was once governed in natural unity and peace. Later, competing minds, art, learning, and systems of government failed to settle it and produced confusion.

CHAPTER XXIV.

The empire is presented as something that should be governed without elaborate schemes or imposed moral action. The boy compares its proper government to looking after horses and preventing harm.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

The empire is repeatedly offered as a supreme trust but is declined by people who will not sacrifice life or inner virtue for it. The unit says that the empire is less important than the body and that governing it is only the surplus or dregs of TAO.

CHAPTER XXXIII.

The Empire is unified and benefited by TAO, which reaches throughout the world while being focused in the Middle Kingdom.
