The Letter to the Hebrews

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

The document is the Letter to the Hebrews, thirteen chapters of exhortation and argument addressed to 'brothers' who are tempted to drift or shrink back. Its central figure is Jesus, the Son of God, whom the author presents as superior to every earlier mediator. Chapter 1 opens by saying God, who spoke through the prophets, has now spoken through his Son, who is heir of all things, radiance of God's glory, and better than the angels, as a chain of quoted statements shows. Chapter 2 warns against neglecting so great a salvation. Jesus was made lower than the angels, tasted death for everyone, freed those enslaved by fear of death, and became a merciful and faithful high priest. Chapters 3 and 4 set Jesus above Moses, who was a servant in the house while Christ is Son over it. They recall the wilderness generation that failed to enter God's rest through unbelief, and say a Sabbath rest remains for God's people. Jesus is a great high priest who sympathizes with weakness, so believers may approach the throne of grace. Chapters 5 to 7 develop his priesthood. He was called by God as Aaron was, a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek, who blessed Abraham and received tithes from him. The author rebukes readers for needing milk instead of solid food, urges them on to maturity, warns about falling away, and offers hope as an anchor of the soul. Jesus holds an unchangeable priesthood and offered himself once for all. Chapters 8 to 10 contrast the old and new covenants. The earthly tabernacle and repeated sacrifices were copies and shadows. Christ entered the heavenly tabernacle through his own blood, mediates a better covenant with laws written on hearts and sins remembered no more, and will appear a second time to save those awaiting him. Readers are urged to draw near, hold fast, encourage one another, and not shrink back. Chapter 11 defines faith and surveys examples, from Abel, Noah, and Abraham to Moses and unnamed sufferers, none of whom received the promise, since God provided something better for 'us.' Chapter 12 calls readers to run with perseverance looking to Jesus, to accept hardship as a Father's discipline, and to avoid Esau's example. They have come to Mount Zion rather than the terrifying mountain, and are to serve God with reverence because he is a consuming fire. Chapter 13 closes with practical commands: brotherly love, hospitality, honoring marriage, freedom from the love of money, and obedience to leaders. Since Jesus is the same yesterday, today, and forever, readers should go out to him outside the camp. The letter ends with a request for prayer, news of Timothy, greetings, and a blessing.

The Letter to the Hebrews / Chapter 1

Hebrews chapter 1 says that God, who once spoke to the fathers through the prophets in many times and ways, has now spoken at the end of these days through his Son. The Son is appointed heir of all things, made the worlds, is the radiance of God's glory and the image of his substance, upholds all things by his word, purified us of our sins, and sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high. The chapter argues that the Son is better than the angels, having inherited a more excellent name. It supports this with a series of quoted statements in which God calls the Son his Son, commands the angels to worship the firstborn, calls the Son God with an eternal throne, and invites him to sit at his right hand. The angels, by contrast, are described as winds and flames of fire and as serving spirits sent to serve those who will inherit salvation.

Chapter 2

Chapter 2 urges readers to pay greater attention to what they have heard so they do not drift away, arguing that neglecting so great a salvation, spoken through the Lord and confirmed by those who heard and by God's signs, wonders, and gifts of the Holy Spirit, is more serious than disobeying the word spoken through angels. The world to come was not subjected to angels; a quoted psalm says man was made a little lower than the angels and crowned with glory, with all things put under his feet, though this is not yet seen. Jesus, made a little lower than the angels, is crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death, tasting death for everyone and being made perfect through sufferings as the author of salvation. Because the children share flesh and blood, Jesus partook of the same so that through death he might bring the devil, who had the power of death, to nothing and free those held in bondage by fear of death. He helps the offspring of Abraham rather than angels, and was made like his brothers to be a merciful and faithful high priest who makes atonement and can help those who are tempted.

Chapter 3

Chapter 3 urges the holy brothers, partakers of a heavenly calling, to consider Jesus, the Apostle and High Priest of their confession, who was faithful to God who appointed him just as Moses was faithful in all his house. Jesus is counted worthy of more glory than Moses because he who built the house has more honor than the house; Moses was a servant in the house, while Christ is a Son over it, and believers are his house if they hold fast their confidence. Quoting the Holy Spirit's words about 'Today if you will hear his voice,' the chapter recalls how the fathers rebelled, tested God in the wilderness for forty years, displeased him, and were sworn not to enter his rest. The readers are warned against an evil heart of unbelief, told to exhort one another daily so they are not hardened by sin's deceitfulness, and reminded that they are partakers of Christ if they hold firm to the end. The chapter concludes that those who came out of Egypt under Moses, who sinned and were disobedient, could not enter the rest because of unbelief.

Chapter 4

Chapter 4 urges its readers to fear falling short of the promise of entering God's rest. It explains that those who first heard the good news did not profit because they lacked faith and disobeyed, while believers do enter that rest. Citing God's rest on the seventh day and David's "Today" passage, it argues that a Sabbath rest still remains for God's people, since Joshua did not give the rest meant. It describes the word of God as living, active, and able to discern the heart, and closes by presenting Jesus, the Son of God, as a great high priest who sympathizes with human weakness and by encouraging believers to approach the throne of grace.

Chapter 5

Chapter 5 describes the role of a high priest, who is taken from among men to offer gifts and sacrifices for sins, can deal gently with the ignorant because he shares their weakness, and must offer sacrifices for himself as well as the people. No one takes this honor on himself; he is called by God, as Aaron was. Christ did not glorify himself to become high priest but was named so by God, who called him Son and a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. In the days of his flesh Christ prayed with strong crying and tears, was heard for his godly fear, learned obedience through suffering, and, made perfect, became the author of eternal salvation to those who obey him. The writer then rebukes the readers as dull of hearing, still needing milk rather than solid food, and says solid food belongs to the full grown who have trained their senses to discern good and evil.

Chapter 6

The author urges readers to leave behind the first principles of Christ, such as repentance, faith, baptisms, laying on of hands, resurrection, and judgment, and press on to perfection. He warns that those who were enlightened and partook of the Holy Spirit but fell away cannot be renewed to repentance, and illustrates this with land that bears either a crop and blessing or thorns and a curse. He then tells the beloved readers he is persuaded of better things for them, since God will not forget their work serving the saints, and urges them to persevere like those who inherited the promises. He cites God's promise and oath to Abraham as two immutable things that give strong encouragement, and describes hope as an anchor of the soul that reaches within the veil, where Jesus entered as forerunner and high priest forever after the order of Melchizedek.

Chapter 7

Chapter 7 argues that Jesus' priesthood is superior to the Levitical priesthood by way of Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of God Most High, who met Abraham, blessed him, and received a tenth from him. Because the lesser is blessed by the greater, and because Levi was figuratively in Abraham when he paid tithes, the text concludes that Melchizedek's priesthood surpasses that of Levi. Since perfection did not come through the Levitical priesthood, a priest of another order arose from Judah, which changes the law and annuls a former commandment because of its weakness. Jesus was made priest with an oath, lives forever, and so holds an unchangeable priesthood, able to save completely those who draw near to God through him. Unlike the high priests who offer daily sacrifices for their own sins and the people's, he offered himself once for all, and is appointed as a Son perfected forever.

Chapter 8

Chapter 8 states that the main point is that believers have a high priest who sat down at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens, serving in the true tabernacle pitched by the Lord, not by man. Because every high priest must offer gifts and sacrifices, this high priest must also have something to offer, and he would not be a priest on earth, where priests serve a copy and shadow of heavenly things according to the law, as Moses was warned to follow the pattern shown on the mountain. The high priest has obtained a more excellent ministry as mediator of a better covenant founded on better promises. Finding fault with the first covenant, the Lord promises through a quoted passage a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah, unlike the one made with their fathers when he led them out of Egypt, in which he will put his laws in their minds, write them on their hearts, and be merciful to their unrighteousness and remember their sins no more. By calling it new, the text concludes, he has made the first covenant obsolete and near to vanishing away.

Chapter 9

Chapter 9 compares the first covenant's earthly worship with Christ's work. The first covenant had an earthly sanctuary whose tabernacle was divided into the Holy Place and the Holy of Holies, where priests served continually and the high priest entered alone once a year with blood for himself and the people's errors. Christ, as high priest of the coming good things, entered the greater and more perfect tabernacle once for all through his own blood, obtaining eternal redemption and cleansing the conscience. Because a death occurred for the transgressions under the first covenant, he is mediator of a new covenant, just as the first covenant was dedicated with blood by Moses and nearly everything is cleansed with blood. Christ was offered once to bear the sins of many and will appear a second time to save those who eagerly wait for him.

Chapter 10

Chapter 10 argues that the law, a mere shadow of good things to come, could never perfect worshipers through its repeated yearly sacrifices, since the blood of bulls and goats cannot take away sins. Jesus Christ, quoted as coming to do God's will with a prepared body, takes away the first order and establishes the second by offering his body once for all, then sits at God's right hand awaiting his enemies' subjection. The Holy Spirit testifies to the new covenant, in which God writes his laws on hearts and remembers sins no more, so no further offering for sin remains. The writer urges the brothers to draw near with faith through the blood of Jesus, hold fast their hope, and encourage one another, warns of fearful judgment for willful sin and for trampling the Son of God, and calls the readers to recall their past endurance and not shrink back but live by faith.

Chapter 11

Chapter 11 defines faith as assurance of things hoped for and proof of things not seen, and says the elders obtained approval by it. It surveys people who acted by faith: Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses, the Israelites who crossed the Red Sea and circled Jericho, and Rahab. Abraham is given the most attention: he obeyed the call without knowing where he went, lived as an alien in tents, looked for a city built by God, and offered up Isaac believing God could raise the dead. Moses refused to be called Pharaoh's daughter's son, left Egypt, and kept the Passover. The chapter closes with unnamed believers who conquered or suffered, and says none of them received the promise, since God provided something better so that they would be made perfect together with us.

Chapter 12

Chapter 12 urges its readers, surrounded by a cloud of witnesses, to lay aside every weight and sin and run with perseverance while looking to Jesus, who endured the cross and sat down at the right hand of God's throne. It reframes the readers' hardships as the discipline of a loving Father, which is painful for the present but yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness and shares God's holiness. The readers are told to lift their hands and knees, make straight paths, pursue peace and sanctification, and guard against falling short of God's grace, as Esau did when he sold his birthright and found no place for a change of mind. The chapter contrasts the terrifying mountain that burned with fire, where Moses was terrified, with Mount Zion, the city of the living God, where Jesus mediates a new covenant and his blood of sprinkling speaks better than Abel's. It closes by warning the readers not to refuse the God who speaks from heaven, who promises to shake earth and heavens once more, and urges them to serve God with reverence and awe because they receive a Kingdom that can't be shaken and because God is a consuming fire.

Chapter 13

Chapter 13 closes the letter with practical exhortations to its readers: continue brotherly love, show hospitality to strangers, remember prisoners and the ill-treated, honor marriage, and be free from the love of money, trusting God's promise never to forsake them. Readers are told to remember and imitate their leaders' faith, to obey them, and to avoid strange teachings about foods, since Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever. The text says Jesus suffered outside the gate to sanctify the people with his blood, so the readers are urged to go out to him outside the camp, bearing his reproach, and to offer a sacrifice of praise and of doing good and sharing. The author asks for prayer, hopes to be restored to the readers soon, and mentions that Timothy has been freed. The chapter ends with a blessing from the God of peace, greetings from the Italians, and a prayer for grace.
