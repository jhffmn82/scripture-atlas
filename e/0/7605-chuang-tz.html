Chuang Tzŭ

Abstract written by a model

Chuang Tzŭ is a philosopher, idealist, mystic, teacher, and principal illustrative figure of TAO, living in the feudal age and native to Mêng, where he held a petty official post at Ch'i-yüan. Contemporary with Prince Hui and Prince Hsüan, he followed Lao Tzŭ while developing original doctrines and criticizing Confucian and Mihist teachings. Though highly skilled in literature and dialectic, he rejects office, wealth, ambition, artificial speech, external standards, materialism, and sense knowledge, preferring freedom and a life ordered by TAO. His teaching moves from skepticism about ordinary distinctions toward absorption in TAO. He teaches relativity of size, space, and time; the identity of contraries; abstraction from self; inaction; acceptance of natural change; and the usefulness of being useless. The butterfly dream unsettles the boundary between man and butterfly, while his discussions of passions, charity, death, language, and happiness challenge fixed judgments. He presents TAO as indivisible and as the path from seeming to truth, with true virtue doing nothing. His life illustrates these ideas: he accepts his wife’s death as transformation, remains poor without misery, borrows grain for his family, declines princely service rather than become a sacrificial ox, and rebukes devotion to the sword of the People. He debates Hui Tzŭ, later mourns the loss of that interlocutor, roams with TAO, and withdraws from the world after losing sight of his real self. Near death he chooses a simple natural burial, while the concluding portrayal presents him as an enthusiastic follower of TAO whose bold, mysterious language seeks harmony between man and higher powers.

_Introduction._

Chuang Tzŭ belonged to the third and fourth centuries before Christ and held a petty official post at Ch'i-yüan in Mêng. His teachings followed Lao Tzŭ but extended into original philosophical speculations, and he criticized the Confucian and Mihist schools. He refused an invitation to become Prime Minister, choosing freedom over office.

_Note on the Philosophy of Chaps._ i-vii.

Chuang Tzŭ is presented as an idealist and mystic who criticizes ordinary judgments, sense knowledge, utilitarian standards, external appearances, and individual selfhood. His philosophy teaches withdrawal from action and reputation, absorption in Tao, and the identity of apparent contraries.

The triumph of Confucianism in the centuries which intervened between

Chuang Tzŭ is described as treating Confucius with contempt and ridicule while also presenting Confucius as giving Taoist teachings. The unit proposes a remarkable parallel between Chuang Tzŭ's teaching and Heracleitus.

Chuang Tzŭ recounts dreaming that he was a butterfly and awakening as himself. He cannot determine whether he was a man dreaming of being a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming of being a man.

CHAPTER V.

Chuang Tzŭ argues that a man can have no passions in the sense of not allowing good and evil to disturb his internal economy. He says that TAO gives man expression and God gives him form, and criticizes Hui Tzŭ for devoting his intelligence to externals.

CHAPTER XIII.

Chuang Tzŭ describes the happiness of GOD as destroying, benefiting, supporting, and shaping all things without claiming cruelty, charity, age, or skill.

CHAPTER XIV.

Chuang Tzŭ teaches Tang that tigers and wolves possess charity through the natural love between parents and offspring. He explains that perfect charity does not admit of love for the individual and that TAO does not admit of sub-division.

The walrus envies the centipede;

Chuang Tzŭ is portrayed as surpassing Kung Sun Lung in grandeur and moving beyond ordinary distinctions, arguments, and the four points. He rejects administrative office by choosing to wag his tail in the mud, answers Hui Tzŭ’s suspicion with the story of the phœnix and owl, and debates Hui Tzŭ about knowing the pleasure of fishes.

CHAPTER XVIII.

Chuang Tzŭ accepts his wife’s death as a natural change from one phase to another and therefore refrains from weeping and wailing. He also questions a skull about the causes of its death and later hears it describe the happiness of death.

CHAPTER XX.

Chuang Tzŭ says that a huge tree survives by being good for nothing, but later rests halfway between usefulness and uselessness. He is advised to roam with TAO, reflects on mortality and the human element, and learns from a strange bird that creatures injure one another through the pursuit of gain. He later concludes that he lost sight of his real self and withdraws from the world.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Chuang Tzŭ questions whether universal standards of right can exist when each person has a separate standard. He uses stories about tuned lutes, a lost child, and a quarrelsome boatman to criticize Hui Tzŭ's arguments, and later praises Hui Tzŭ as an irreplaceable interlocutor.

CHAPTER XXVI.

Chuang Tzŭ, being poor, asks the prince of Chien-ho for grain and angrily rejects a delayed promise of silver by comparing it to offering a stickleback water only after it has reached the dried-fish shop. He discusses the useless, the limits of human withdrawal, the unobstructed mind, and the need to ignore language once its idea has been expressed.

CHAPTER XXX.

Chuang Tzŭ refuses the thousand ounces of silver but agrees to approach the prince, disguising himself as a swordsman. He describes the sword of the Son of Heaven, the sword of the Princes, and the sword of the People, then rebukes the prince for favoring the last of these.

CHAPTER XXXII.

Chuang Tzŭ teaches that TAO is obscured by artificial speech, ambition, external standards, and reliance on the senses. He mocks Ts'ao Shang's political rewards, rejects an invitation to serve as a sacrificial ox, and chooses a simple natural burial when death approaches.

CHAPTER XXXIII.

Chuang Tzŭ became an enthusiastic follower of TAO and expressed his thoughts through strange, bold, and far-reaching language. He sought harmony between man and higher powers, judged not the rights and wrongs of mankind, and is described as endlessly suggestive, mysterious, and not fully explored.
