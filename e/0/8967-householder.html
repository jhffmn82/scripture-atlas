householder

Also called: a householder, another, householders, owner's, the householder, the other

Abstract written by a model

A householder is a resident whose home and conduct shape a mendicant’s alms and care. Their abode should not be entered during milking or before cooked food is distributed, and mendicants must not visit relatives in expectation of food. A householder may procure, prepare, permit, or knowingly give certain food, subject to rules on âdhâkarmika food and salt. They may also offer bodily care, ornaments, charms, and plant-cutting treatments to mendicants.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

4 sections; each heading opens the text

Fourth Lesson.

A householder’s abode should not be entered while milch cows are being milked or food is being cooked and has not yet been distributed. A householder may have kin or relations in a family that a mendicant is tempted to visit before begging, but doing so for anticipated food is sinful.

Ninth Lesson.

A householder may procure or prepare food for a mendicant, but food prepared for the mendicant is not to be accepted if it is âdhâkarmika. Food placed before a door may be accepted only if the householder permits it or gives it to the mendicant.

Tenth Lesson.

A householder may invite a mendicant to accept meat or fish with many bones or may bring fossil salt or sea salt in a bowl. If salt was given without full knowledge, the householder may, after being questioned, knowingly give it for consumption or division.

Thirteenth Lecture.

The householder may wipe, wash, treat, groom, adorn, or perfume the mendicant's feet and body, remove pus, blood, sweat, dirt, splinters, thorns, nits, or lice, and treat wounds or other bodily conditions. The householder may also fasten necklaces, collars, diadems, garlands, or a golden string, and may attempt to cure the mendicant by charms or by cutting and digging up living plants.
