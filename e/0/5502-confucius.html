Abstract written by a model

In Chuang Tzŭ, Confucius is portrayed as a teacher and sage who combines practical ethics with Taoist teaching and criticism. In Chuang Tzŭ, Confucius is presented as a Sage and scholar who seeks TAO, endures persecution, and learns the limits of reform and conventional virtue. In The Analects of Confucius, the Master teaches perseverance, friendship, filial conduct, sincerity, love, principled association, and self-knowledge. In The Analects of Confucius, Book II (Wei Chang): Confucius teaches that people submit when the upright are advanced and the crooked are set aside. In The Analects of Confucius, Book III (Pa Yih): Confucius criticizes the head of the Chi family for displaying eight rows of pantomimes and condemns improper sacrifices, empty ceremony, and abuses of rank. In The Analects of Confucius, Book IV (Le Jin): The Master teaches that virtue should govern a person's choices, relationships, conduct, and response to poverty, danger, and advantage. In The Analects of Confucius, Book V (Kung-Ye Ch'Ang), the Master teaches, judges character, praises virtue, and emphasizes conduct over words. In The Analects of Confucius, Book VI (Yung Yey): Confucius is presented as a superior man and teacher whose central concern is virtue expressed in conduct, learning, government, and care for others.

Confucius in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where Confucius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Confucius is a person, teacher, sage, prophet, and seeker of TAO whose portrayal combines practical ethics with Taoist teaching and criticism. He is introduced as teaching duty to one’s neighbour, charitableness, justice, sincerity, and fortitude, while knowing nothing of God, soul, or an unseen world. Yet the records also place Taoist sayings and doctrines in his mouth: he teaches fasting of the heart, inward stillness, harmony, restraint, acceptance of circumstances, concentration, silence, inaction, and undivided TAO. He seeks instruction from Wang T'ai and Lao Tzŭ, recognizes sages concealed by humble appearances, and eventually attains TAO after years of study. His encounters teach him natural adaptation, freedom from attachment, and the limits of speech and conventional distinctions. He worries for Yen Yüan, advises disciples about official life and danger, and deepens his attachment to them after hardship. During hunger and siege between Ch'ên and Ts'ai, he fears death but learns to avoid fame, adapt to circumstances, preserve life, and see adversity as a test of inner cultivation. He is criticized for dogmatism, specious knowledge, fixed customs, and lack of sense; he later changes his opinions, rejects former formulations of perseverance and wisdom, and recognizes that what he once thought right may be wrong. By the end, he teaches that success and failure do not affect one who holds fast to TAO, while his portrayal remains marked by tension between Confucian practical duty and Taoist transcendence.

_Introduction._

Confucius taught practical ethics centered on duty to one's neighbour, charitableness, justice, sincerity, and fortitude. He did not address a God, a soul, or an unseen world, and Chuang Tzŭ placed sayings found in The Tao-Tê-Ching into Confucius's mouth.

_Note on the Philosophy of Chaps._ i-vii.

Confucius appears as a teacher associated with Confucian duty and practical wisdom, but in one passage he speaks as a disciple of Lao Tzŭ and teaches the fasting of the heart. Chuang Tzŭ also contrasts Confucius with a mutilated criminal judged to be the greater man.

Confucius sends Tzŭ Kung to mourn Tzŭ Sang Hu, then explains that Tzŭ Sang Hu's companions live beyond conventional rules and distinctions. He teaches that man is born in TAO and that human beings can live in peace by obtaining TAO.

Confucius is criticized for trying to apply ancient customs and teachings without adapting them to changing circumstances. After speaking with Lao Tzŭ, he recognizes Lao Tzŭ as a Dragon whom he could not admonish, and he later attains TAO.

Confucius is sad when Yen Yüan travels to the Ch'i State. He teaches that destiny and form impose limits and warns that failing to understand a ruler’s nature can be fatal.

Confucius learns from a hunchback that singleness of purpose concentrates the faculties, and from a boatman’s account that skill requires freedom from attachment to external stakes. Confucius is trapped between Ch'ên and Ts'ai and passes seven days without food. He learns from Jen and Tzŭ Sang Hu about avoiding fame, adapting to surroundings, friendship, God, and the limits of human knowledge. He later abandons his studies, returns home, and deepens his love for his disciples.

Confucius teaches that wordless utterances, inaction, and silence can accomplish more than speech. He praises the capacity to rest in undivided TAO and avoid divisive argument.

CHAPTER XXV.

Confucius explains that a hidden Sage may live among ordinary people without fame. He recognizes a servant as I Liao of Shih-nan but prevents Tzŭ Lu from approaching him, knowing the man would despise a toady.

Confucius is told by Lao Lai Tzŭ to abandon dogmatism and specious knowledge before he can become a superior man or enter upon TAO. He later observes that a divine tortoise can give seventy-two faultless omens yet cannot escape being caught and cut to pieces.

Confucius changed his opinions during his life, eventually regarding as wrong what he had previously regarded as right. He discarded perseverance and wisdom as formulated doctrines, but said that man should speak in accordance with established laws and bring submission into men’s hearts.

Abstract written by a model

Confucius is presented as a Sage, scholar, teacher, student, Master of Tzŭ Kung, and member of the K'ung family. He devotes himself to loyalty, truth, charity, duty to one's neighbour, ceremonies, music, and distinguishing human relationships, while seeking success through long struggle and enduring four persecutions. He teaches that TAO existed before the universe and that there is no past, present, beginning, or end; he also values natural intelligence, internal constancy, unspoken language, and action without deeds. On traveling west to place his works in the Imperial library, he defends charity and duty as rooted in original nature, but Lao Tzŭ rejects this teaching. Surrounded at K'uang by men of Wei who mistake him for Yang Hu, he plays and sings without ceasing, remains fearless, and sees failure as fate and success as opportunity. He later tries unsuccessfully to reform Robber Chê, who denounces him as deceptive; Confucius departs shaken. At Apricot Altar, he follows and reveres an old fisherman, learning that attempts to reform and regulate others have drawn him from original purity. Finally, he is criticized as outwardly showy and specious, though the text attributes to him demanding tests of character and superior manhood.

Confucius travels west to place his works in the Imperial library of the House of Chou. He defends charity and duty to one's neighbour as rooted in man's original nature, but Lao Tzŭ rejects his account as confusing the mind of man.

When Confucius visited K’uang, men of Wei surrounded him because they mistook him for Yang Hu. He continued playing and singing, explaining that failure is fate, success is opportunity, and the Sage remains fearless in great danger; the troops later apologized and withdrew.

CHAPTER XXII.

Confucius discusses perfect TAO with Lao Tzŭ and teaches Jen Ch'iu that before the universe existed there was TAO. He also teaches Yen Yüan that people should modify themselves externally while remaining internally constant, and that the best language is unspoken and the best action is without deeds.

CHAPTER XXIX.

Confucius attempts to reform Robber Chê by offering him rulership and appealing to virtue, filial piety, and brotherly love. Robber Chê denounces him as a deceptive thief whose teachings have brought harm, and Confucius leaves shaken and unable to answer.

CHAPTER XXXI.

Confucius plays the lute and sings at Apricot Altar, then follows the old fisherman and prostrates himself before him. He learns that his efforts to reform others and regulate human affairs have led him away from original purity, and he recognizes the fisherman as a Sage.

CHAPTER XXXII.

Confucius is criticized as a man of outward show and specious words who mistakes accessories for fundamentals and may lead people from the true to the false. The text also attributes to him an account of testing character through distance, proximity, difficult missions, money, danger, wine, and disreputable surroundings.

Confucius in The Analects of Confucius, Book VI (Yung Yey)

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where Confucius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Confucius is presented as a superior man and teacher whose central concern is virtue expressed in conduct, learning, government, and care for others. He helps people in distress without enriching those already rich, appoints and supports officials, and permits Yuan Sze to distribute grain. He assesses disciples and others according to their qualities: Yung may occupy a prince’s place; Yen Hui loves learning and avoids repeated faults; several disciples are fit for government; Mang Chih-fan does not boast. He visits the ill Po-niu with compassion and urges Yen Ch'iu not to limit himself. His teaching distinguishes the superior man from the mean man, urges people to follow proper ways, blend solid qualities with accomplishments, preserve uprightness, and study extensively while observing propriety. He says wisdom is devotion to duties toward people while keeping aloof from spiritual beings, and perfect virtue puts difficulty before success. He also stresses the Constant Mean, though it is rare, and teaches that a perfectly virtuous person establishes and enlarges others as himself. Abstract written by a model

Confucius is a philosopher and Master characterized by learning, teaching, humility, ritual attentiveness, and moral demands. Though described as nearly a sage and endowed by Heaven, he says he is not knowledgeable; his low condition when young led him to acquire many arts while without office. When questioned by someone seemingly empty-like, he explains a matter exhaustively. He seldom speaks of profitableness, appointments of Heaven, or perfect virtue, and is entirely free of foregone conclusions, predeterminations, obstinacy, and egoism. He follows economical common practice concerning caps but rejects arrogant practice concerning bowing, continually bowing below the hall. He treats people in mourning, full dress, and blindness with marked respect. As a teacher, he leads through orderly method, enlarges Yen Yuan with learning, and teaches propriety as restraint; he praises Hui for never flagging. His sayings stress persistence in learning, sincerity, reform after admonition, steadfast will, virtue over beauty, and the distinct qualities of the wise, virtuous, and bold. Put in fear in K'wang, he sees himself as bearing the cause of truth after King Wan's death. In illness he rejects a false display of ministers, preferring death among disciples or even on the road. He advises selling a gem when the right buyer appears, wishes to live among eastern tribes, returns from Wei to Lu and reforms music, and reflects by a stream on ceaseless passing. His later sayings warn that promise may not flower or fruit, call for respect toward youth, and insist that distance comes from want of thought.

Confucius is a person portrayed through his conduct in village, court, ritual, entertainment, illness, and service to the prince. In his village he appears simple and sincere, almost unable to speak; in the prince’s ancestral temple and court he speaks minutely yet cautiously. Abstract written by a model

Perfect virtue begins with subduing oneself and returning to propriety: one must avoid improper looking, listening, speaking, and moving; act respectfully and considerately; and be cautious and slow in speech. The superior man has no anxiety or fear when self-examination finds nothing wrong, while intelligence resists slander and startling statements. Virtue rests on faithfulness, sincerity, and continual movement toward what is right, supported by extensive learning and propriety. In government, the people's confidence is most necessary, while affairs must remain in mind and be practised consistently. Benevolence means loving and knowing all men, employing the upright, and setting aside the crooked. The teaching ends with friendship: faithfully admonish and skillfully lead a friend, but stop rather than disgrace oneself if the friend is impracticable.

Abstract written by a model

The Master is a teacher and adviser chiefly concerned with government, virtue, and the formation of capable people. He begins by telling Tsze-lu that government requires leaders to set an example and labor for the people without weariness. He advises officials to employ worthy and talented officers, pardon small faults, and correct their own conduct before trying to govern others. His central political principle is the rectification of names: unless names accord with reality, truthful language, affairs, proprieties, music, and punishments all fail. He values practical ability over learning that cannot meet a governmental charge or mission, and says government should enrich people before teaching them, avoid haste and small advantages, and cultivate good rule over time. Though not in office, he says he would have been consulted on government business, and he believes employment by a prince could produce considerable results within twelve months. He holds that successive good government could transform the violently bad, though even a royal ruler would need a generation for virtue to prevail. Personally, he admits lesser skill than husbandmen and gardeners in their crafts, while teaching that propriety, righteousness, and good faith guide the people. He defines perfect virtue through gravity, reverence, and sincerity; praises firmness, endurance, simplicity, and modesty; and stresses constancy. Unable to find people pursuing the due medium, he turns to the ardent and cautiously decided. He closes by distinguishing the superior from the mean man and maintaining that a good man should teach the people for seven years before employing them in war.

Abstract written by a model

This record presents Confucius as a teacher and adviser whose sayings center on virtue, judgment, conduct, government, learning, and language. It begins with his declining to discuss military matters, departing the next day, and later facing exhausted provisions in Chan; he explains that his learning seeks a single unity all-pervading. He repeatedly teaches practical moral discipline: sincere speech, careful action, appropriate silence, preparation for work, foresight about distant concerns, self-demand rather than demands on others, reform of faults, and reciprocity. He defines the superior man as inward-looking, dignified without wrangling, sociable without partisanship, distressed by lack of ability rather than lack of recognition, and correctly firm. Virtue is treated as essential, personally incumbent on every person, and more important than water or fire. His political advice joins reverent governance, established ceremonial practice, avoidance of specious talk, and a minister’s dutiful service. He urges careful evaluation of people and words: do not promote someone merely for speech, reject good words because of their speaker, or follow the multitude without examination. In dealing with men, he blames evil and praises goodness, but says excess praise follows examination of the individual. The record also contains laments for declining practices, concern that few love virtue, and reflections on ineffective mere thinking. It ends with Confucius guiding the blind music-master Mien through a room and explaining the rule for those who lead the blind.

The Master in The Doctrine of the Mean

James Legge · the work’s page · read it where The Master first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Master is a speaker and teacher centered on the Mean: a path he knows but says is untrodden, difficult to understand, and difficult to sustain. Perfect virtue accords with it, yet it has long been rare. Knowledgeable, talented, and virtuous people exceed it, while stupid and worthless people fall short; even those claiming wisdom cannot escape dangers or keep the Mean for a month. Though kingdoms and families may be governed, rewards declined, and weapons faced, the Mean cannot simply be attained. He contrasts southern forbearance and gentleness without revenge with northern readiness to die under arms, and urges cultivation of the proper energy. He rejects obscurity, wonders, and future honor, and says he cannot stop halfway after proceeding on the right path. The path is near ordinary human consciousness; conduct remote from it is not the path. His teaching joins reciprocity, sincerity, service, superior governance, and harmony that brings parents complete complacence. He teaches about spiritual beings, whose unseen powers enter all things and shape sacrifice. He praises Shun’s filial virtue and Heaven’s appointment, and explains the far-reaching filial piety, ceremonies, and sacrifices of King Wu and the duke of Chau. Government depends on proper people, cultivated character, duty, benevolence, preparation, sincerity, and persistent practice. He warns against ignorant judgment, unauthorized direction, and returning to antiquity. Though he can describe Hsia ceremonies imperfectly and has learned Yin ceremonies, he follows Chau ceremonies; sound and appearances are only trivial influences in transforming people.
