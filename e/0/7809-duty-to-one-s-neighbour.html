duty to one's neighbour

topic · 1 work · 9 quoted facts · 4 connections
Also called: Duty to one's neighbour, charity and duty, charity and duty to one's neighbour, duty towards his neighbour, duty towards one's neighbour

Abstract written by a model

Duty to one's neighbour is a topic treated as an artificial, intentional virtue rather than part of natural or moral nature. It arises and springs from TAO in natural form, yet when cultivated it becomes an accidental of the spiritual and one studied effort among others. Chapters VIII and IX portray it, with charity, as a teaching imposed by Sages that changes natural life, brings sorrow, division, knowledge-seeking, and competition for gain. Confucius presents it as original and necessary for the superior man, while Lao Tzŭ regards it as only a temporary stopping place on the way to transcendental space. Its intentional cultivation may serve self-interest, reputation, wealth, government, or attracting people and benefits; many make capital from it. Most sharply, cultivation intended to end war is said to cause further fighting, as inward intention appears in outward action.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VIII.

Duty to one's neighbour is treated like intentional charity: an artificial virtue imposed on natural life. The chapter claims that pursuing it has caused sorrow, changed human nature, and distracted people from TAO.

CHAPTER IX.

Charity and duty to one’s neighbour are introduced by Sages as teachings used to shape government and satisfy people’s hearts. Their introduction disturbs natural life and contributes to division, knowledge-seeking, and competition for gain.

CHAPTER XIII.

Duty to one's neighbour is presented by Confucius as part of man's original nature and as necessary to make the superior man effective. Lao Tzŭ treats it as an accidental of the spiritual rather than an essential.

CHAPTER XIV.

Duty to one's neighbour is described as one of the studied efforts intended to aid the development of virtue. Lao Tzŭ says it is like a caravanserai where one may stop briefly on the way to transcendental space, but not remain for long.

CHAPTER XXIV.

Hsü Wu Kuei says that cultivating duty towards one's neighbour in order to end war originates further fighting. He treats it as an imposed standard whose inward intention becomes visible in outward action.

Kuan Chung being at the point of death, Duke Huan went to see him.

Duty to one's neighbour is paired with charity as a virtue that can attract people and provide benefits. Hsü Yu says few ignore these virtues, while many make capital out of them.

Duty towards one's neighbour is described by Tzŭ Chang as the true key when reputation or wealth is sought. The text contrasts this principle with examples of rulers who achieved power through acts that violated conventional duties.
