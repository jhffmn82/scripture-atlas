Mordecai

person · 1 work · 35 quoted facts · 10 connections
Also called: Mordecai our deliverer and perpetual benefactor, Mordecai the Jew, Mordecai the son of Jair, me

Abstract written by a model

Mordecai is a Jew of the tribe of Benjamin, son of Jair (also given as Jairus), living in Susa. He was among the captives carried from Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar and serves in the king's palace and courtyard under King Ahasuerus. He raised his foster child Esther as his own and charged her not to reveal her family. Early on he sees a vision of two serpents and the exaltation of the lowly, and he keeps it in his heart. He learns of two chamberlains' plot against the king and reports it; the deed is recorded in the royal library, but he is not rewarded at first. He refuses to bow to Haman, ignoring the officials who question him daily and disclosing that he is a Jew, so as not to set human glory above God's; he worships the Lord God exclusively. Haman's rage turns into a plot against Mordecai and his whole people, and a fifty-cubit gallows is prepared for him on the advice of Zeresh and Haman's friends. Mordecai responds with sackcloth, dust and a cry that an innocent nation is to be destroyed. He sends Hathach a copy of the decree, charges Esther to plead with the king and warns her she and her father's house will perish if she is silent. After she agrees, he organizes the fast and prays. The reversal comes when the king, finding his unrewarded service in the records, has him dressed in royal robes, mounted on the king's horse and paraded through the city. Haman's wife and friends say the living God is with him. The gallows meant for Mordecai is used to hang Haman. Mordecai receives the king's ring, is appointed by Esther over all that had been Haman's, and goes out in royal apparel with a golden crown and purple diadem while Susa rejoices. The king's letter calls him a deliverer and perpetual benefactor. Feared by officials, he writes the account, sends it to all Jews, and with Esther establishes Purim on the fourteenth and fifteenth of Adar as days of feasting and gifts for all generations. He ends as viceroy to Ahasuerus, a great man honored by the Jews and loved by his nation, declaring that these events came from God and interpreting his dream, identifying himself and Haman as the two serpents.

Esther (Greek) / Chapter 1

Mordecai, son of Jair, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Jew in Susa and one of the captives taken from Jerusalem, served in the king's palace and saw a vision of two serpents, battle among nations, and the exaltation of the lowly. He learned of the chamberlains' plot against the king and informed him, wrote an account of it, and was commanded to serve in the palace with gifts. He kept the vision in his heart, seeking to interpret it.

Chapter 2

Mordecai is a Jew in Susa, son of Jairus, son of Shimei, son of Kish, of the tribe of Benjamin, who was carried captive from Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar. He raised Esther as his own, charged her not to reveal her family, walked daily by the women's court to learn her fate, and served in the courtyard. He discovered the chamberlains' plot and told Esther, and the king ordered his goodwill recorded in the royal library as a commendation.

Chapter 3

Mordecai refuses to bow down to Haman despite the king's order, and he ignores the palace officials who question him daily. He has shown them that he is a Jew, and they report his resistance to Haman. His refusal provokes Haman's rage and the plot against his people.

Chapter 4

Mordecai tears his garments, puts on sackcloth and dust, cries out that a nation that has done no wrong is to be destroyed, and refuses the clothes Esther sends. He tells Hathach of Haman's promise of ten thousand talents, gives him a copy of the decree, and charges Esther to plead with the king, warning her that she and her father's house will perish if she keeps quiet. After Esther agrees, he organizes the fast and prays to the Lord, explaining that he refused to bow to Haman so as not to set the glory of man above the glory of God.

Chapter 5

Mordecai the Jew is seen in the court by Haman, whose rage at the sight leads him to plot against him. Zeresh and Haman's friends advise that Mordecai be hanged on a gallows the next morning.

Chapter 6

Mordecai's earlier report to the king about two chamberlains who sought to lay hands on Ahasuerus is found in the records, and the king learns he was never rewarded for it. He is dressed in the king's robe, mounted on the king's horse, and paraded through the city streets, then returns to the palace. Haman's wife and friends say that he is of the race of the Jews and that the living God is with him.

Chapter 7

Mordecai is mentioned by the chamberlain Bugathan as the man who spoke concerning the king and for whom Haman prepared a fifty-cubit gallows. He does not act in this unit, and the gallows meant for him is used to hang Haman.

Chapter 8

Mordecai is called before the king, who gives him the ring taken from Haman, and Esther appoints him over all that had been Haman's. He goes out in royal apparel with a golden crown and a diadem of fine purple linen, and the people of Susa rejoice. The king's letter calls him a deliverer and perpetual benefactor.

Chapter 9

Mordecai was feared by the officials, which led them to honor the Jews. He wrote the account in a book and sent it to all the Jews near and far, establishing the fourteenth and fifteenth of Adar as days of feasting and gifts. Together with Esther he confirmed the Purim decision, pledging their own well-being to it.

Chapter 10

Mordecai is called viceroy to King Ahasuerus, a great man honored by the Jews and loved by his whole nation. He declares that these events came from God and interprets his dream, identifying himself and Haman as the two serpents.
