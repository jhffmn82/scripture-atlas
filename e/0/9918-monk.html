Abstract written by a model

The monk is an ascetic and Sramana whose life is directed toward final liberation. He begins by renouncing domestic life, possessions, relations, worldly interests, sexual pleasures, and attachment despite family appeals and temptations. Living as a mendicant, he accepts only freely given and permitted alms, seeks secluded lodging, and practises control of mind, speech, body, and senses. What matters most is disciplined non-injury and the destruction of sin and Karman. The monk treats and protects living beings tenderly in thought, word, and act; avoids false speech, theft, deceit, greed, anger, pride, harmful food, careless conduct, and approval of slaughter. He practises chastity, silence, meditation, austerities, carefulness, and forbearance, bears pain, weather, hunger, abuse, and other hardships without anger, and cares for a sick brother according to the Law. His discipline also requires obedience to wise teachers, correction without hostility, knowledge of the Law, and truthful teaching of it. He distinguishes acceptable food from unacceptable food, avoids honour and fame, does not acquire property or build a house, and neither desires life nor death. The records warn that monks who act like householders, seek subsistence, vanity, fame, or pride fail to obtain final Liberation and remain in the Circle of Births. As he perseveres, the monk becomes free from bonds and passion, gains pure Lêsyâ and good qualities, suppresses and annihilates bad Karman, removes the thorn of sin, and wanders with a pure soul. Through austerities, silence, right doctrine, control, and religious practice, he ultimately obtains final Liberation.

Second Lecture, Called the Destruction of Karman. / First Chapter. .. Third Chapter.

Monks should control mind, speech, and body, give up possessions and relations, avoid quarrels, gossip, deceit, greed, pride, and wrath, and perform religious duties. They should endure pleasant and unpleasant conditions, collect pure alms, and seek the soul's benefit.

Third Lecture, Called the Knowledge of Troubles / First Chapter. .. Fourth Chapter.

Monks endure begging, weather, hunger, insults, injury, attacks, hair-plucking, and other hardships. They should renounce attachment, practise control, care for sick brothers, avoid sin, and wander until final liberation.

Fourth Lecture, Called Knowledge of Women. / First Chapter. .. Second Chapter.

A monk who has left worldly ties should live alone and wisely, abstain from sexual pleasures, and guard himself against women’s temptations. He should not look at women, walk with them, enter their apartments, or do work for others, and a pure monk should wander until final liberation.

Eighth Lecture, Called on Exertion.

A pious monk is free from bonds, has severed all fetters, annihilates bad Karman, and removes the thorn of sin. He should eat little, drink little, talk little, remain calm and free from greed, and wander while performing religious practices until liberation.

Ninth Lecture, Called the Law.

A monk should be without property and egoism, abandon wealth and worldly interests, treat living beings tenderly, and abstain from harmful acts, pleasures, improper speech, and worldly associations. He should endure pain and abuse without anger, serve a wise teacher, and practise what is right.

Tenth Lecture, Called Carefulness.

A monk should wander without forming resolutions, giving offence, attachment, property, stores, or desire for life or death. He should restrain his senses, endure hardships, accept only allowed food, and avoid causing or approving harmful works.

Eleventh Lecture, Called the Path.

The monk and great sage is asked to explain the best Path leading to liberation from all misery and how it should be described to gods or men.

Twelfth Lecture, Called the Creed.

They annihilate their works by abstention from works and do not commit sins after getting rid of the effects of greed. They avoid injury, practise control, and some become heroes through knowledge.

Thirteenth Lecture, Called the Real Truth.

Monks are instructed to follow their teachers' commands, control themselves, overcome pride and sensual attachment, and distinguish acceptable from unacceptable food. They should teach the Law with knowledge of others' dispositions, avoid injury, honour, fame, passion, and greed, and wander without desiring life or death.

Fourteenth Lecture, Called the Nirgrantha.

A monk should practise chastity, obey the teacher, master the conduct, avoid carelessness, and remain controlled. He should accept correction without anger, avoid harming or insulting others, teach the Law truthfully, and protect living beings.
