The Acts of the Apostles

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

The Acts of the Apostles is a narrative, addressed to Theophilus, of how the message about Jesus spreads from Jerusalem to Rome after his ascension. Its central figures are Peter, who leads the early chapters, and Paul (earlier Saul), who dominates the second half, with Barnabas, Philip, Stephen, James, Silas, and Timothy playing important supporting roles. After forty days of appearances, Jesus promises the apostles the Holy Spirit and power to be witnesses to the ends of the earth, then ascends. Matthias replaces Judas. At Pentecost the believers are filled with the Spirit and speak in many languages; Peter preaches and about three thousand join. The community shares goods, Peter and John heal a lame man, and the apostles face arrest, threats, and a beating from the council but keep teaching. Ananias and Sapphira die for deceit. Seven men, including Stephen, are chosen to serve; Stephen's speech before the council ends with his stoning while Saul looks on. Persecution scatters believers. Philip preaches in Samaria and baptizes an Ethiopian eunuch. Saul, on the road to Damascus, meets Jesus, is blinded, healed by Ananias, baptized, and begins preaching; Barnabas brings him to the apostles. Peter, after a vision, preaches to Cornelius's Gentile household, who receive the Spirit, and defends this in Jerusalem. Antioch becomes a centre where disciples are first called Christians. Herod kills James and jails Peter, who is freed by an angel; Herod is struck down. Barnabas and Saul, now called Paul, are sent out from Antioch, preaching in Cyprus, Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe, turning to the Gentiles amid opposition and stoning. A Jerusalem council, hearing Peter and James, decides Gentiles need not be circumcised. Paul and Barnabas part; Paul takes Silas, then Timothy, through Macedonia and Greece: Philippi (Lydia, the jailer), Thessalonica, Beroea, Athens (the Areopagus), Corinth, and Ephesus, where he teaches two years and a riot erupts over Artemis. He gives a farewell to Ephesian elders at Miletus. In Jerusalem Paul is seized in the temple, defends himself before the crowd and council, and, after a plot to kill him, is sent to Felix at Caesarea. He is held two years, then, under Festus, appeals to Caesar and speaks before Agrippa, who finds nothing deserving death. Sailing to Rome, he survives a storm and shipwreck and a viper bite on Malta. In Rome he testifies to Jewish leaders, declares salvation sent to the nations, and spends two years preaching boldly without hindrance.

The Acts of the Apostles / Chapter 1

The narrator addresses Theophilus and recalls that his first book covered what Jesus began to do and teach. Over forty days after his suffering Jesus appeared to the apostles he had chosen, spoke about God's Kingdom, and told them to wait in Jerusalem for baptism in the Holy Spirit; he then promised them power to be witnesses to the uttermost parts of the earth and was taken up in a cloud while two men in white told them he would return in the same way. The apostles went back to Jerusalem, gathered in the upper room with the women, Mary, and Jesus' brothers, and prayed. Peter, standing among about one hundred twenty disciples, explained that Judas, who guided those who took Jesus, had fallen and died, and that a replacement witness of the resurrection was needed. Two men, Joseph called Barsabbas and Matthias, were put forward, and after prayer the lot fell on Matthias, who was counted with the eleven apostles.

Chapter 2

On the day of Pentecost, the believers gathered in one place are filled with the Holy Spirit after a sound like a rushing wind and tongues like fire appear, and they begin speaking in other languages. Jews from many nations in Jerusalem hear them in their own languages; some are amazed while others mock them as drunk on new wine. Peter, standing with the eleven, answers that this fulfills Joel's prophecy and proclaims that Jesus of Nazareth, crucified by lawless men, was raised by God, exalted, and made both Lord and Christ. Cut to the heart, the listeners ask what to do, and Peter tells them to repent and be baptized for the forgiveness of sins, after which about three thousand are added. The believers then devote themselves to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, prayer, and sharing of goods, and the Lord adds to their number daily.

Chapter 3

Peter and John go up to the temple at the hour of prayer, where a man lame from birth is laid daily at the Beautiful gate to beg. Peter tells him he has no silver or gold, and in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth commands him to get up and walk; he takes the man's right hand, and the man is healed and enters the temple leaping and praising God. The amazed people run together to them at Solomon's porch, and Peter tells them the healing came by faith in Jesus' name, not by the apostles' own power. He says God glorified his Servant Jesus, whom the people delivered up and denied, and whom God raised from the dead. Peter calls them to repent and turn again so their sins may be blotted out and times of refreshing may come, citing Moses and the prophets, and says God sent Jesus first to them to bless them by turning each from wickedness.

Chapter 4

Peter and John, while speaking to the people, are seized by the priests, the captain of the temple, and the Sadducees, who are upset that they proclaim the resurrection in Jesus, and are held overnight while about five thousand men believe. The next morning the rulers, elders, and scribes, including Annas the high priest, Caiaphas, John, and Alexander, question them, and Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, says the crippled man was healed in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth and that there is salvation in no other name. Unable to deny the miracle, the council threatens Peter and John and orders them not to speak in Jesus' name, but they answer that they cannot help telling what they saw and heard, and are released. The believers pray to God, quoting David, and ask for boldness; the place is shaken and they are filled with the Holy Spirit. The chapter ends with the believers sharing all possessions, the apostles testifying to the resurrection, and Barnabas, a Levite from Cyprus, selling a field and laying the money at the apostles' feet.

Chapter 5

Ananias and his wife Sapphira sell a possession and keep back part of the price while laying the rest at the apostles' feet; Peter confronts each of them, and both fall dead and are buried by the young men, bringing great fear on the assembly. Signs and wonders follow through the apostles, believers multiply, and the sick are healed, even by Peter's shadow. The high priest and the Sadducees, jealous, imprison the apostles, but an angel of the Lord frees them at night and sends them to teach in the temple. Brought before the council, Peter and the apostles say they must obey God rather than men and witness to Jesus; the council wants to kill them until Gamaliel advises leaving them alone, so they are beaten, commanded not to speak in Jesus' name, and released. The apostles leave rejoicing and keep teaching Jesus as the Christ every day.

Chapter 6

As the number of disciples grows, the Hellenists complain that their widows are neglected in the daily service by the Hebrews. The twelve gather the disciples and have them choose seven men to oversee the work, so the twelve can keep to prayer and the ministry of the word. The multitude chooses Stephen, Philip, Prochorus, Nicanor, Timon, Parmenas, and Nicolaus, and the apostles pray and lay hands on them. The word of God spreads and many priests become obedient to the faith. Stephen performs wonders and signs, is disputed by men from several synagogues, and is seized after they induce men to accuse him of blasphemy; false witnesses accuse him before the council, whose members see his face like that of an angel.

Chapter 7

Answering the high priest's question, Stephen recounts Israel's history: God's call and promise to Abraham, Joseph being sold by his brothers and rising in Egypt, the family's move to Egypt, and the oppression there. He describes Moses, who was rejected by his own people, then sent by God from the burning bush to deliver them, yet the fathers rebelled by making a calf and serving other gods. Stephen notes the tabernacle, Solomon's house, and says the Most High does not dwell in temples made with hands. He then accuses his hearers of being stiff-necked, of resisting the Holy Spirit, and of betraying and murdering the Righteous One. Enraged when he says he sees Jesus standing at God's right hand, they drive him out of the city and stone him, and he dies praying for them while Saul looks on.

Chapter 8

A great persecution scatters the assembly in Jerusalem, with Saul ravaging it and dragging men and women to prison, while the scattered believers preach the word. Philip proclaims Christ in Samaria, where signs and healings bring great joy and many are baptized, including the sorcerer Simon. Peter and John come from Jerusalem, pray for the Samaritans, and lay hands on them so they receive the Holy Spirit; when Simon offers money for this power, Peter rebukes him and tells him to repent, and Simon asks for prayer. Directed by an angel and the Spirit, Philip meets an Ethiopian eunuch reading Isaiah on the road toward Gaza, explains the passage as being about Jesus, and baptizes him. The Spirit then catches Philip away, the eunuch goes on rejoicing, and Philip is found at Azotus and preaches in the cities until he reaches Caesarea.

Chapter 9

Saul, breathing threats against the Lord's disciples, sets out for Damascus with authority from the high priest to bind followers of the Way. On the road a light and the voice of Jesus stop him and leave him blind; Ananias, sent by the Lord, lays hands on him, he regains sight, is baptized, and begins proclaiming Jesus in the synagogues. The Jews plot to kill him, so his disciples lower him over the wall in a basket, and in Jerusalem Barnabas brings him to the apostles after the disciples fear him. When the Hellenists seek to kill him, the brothers send him off to Tarsus, and the assemblies enjoy peace. Peter then heals the paralyzed Aeneas at Lydda and raises Tabitha (Dorcas) at Joppa, where he stays with Simon the tanner.

Chapter 10

Cornelius, a devout centurion in Caesarea, is told by an angel in a vision to send men to Joppa to fetch Simon Peter. Meanwhile Peter, hungry on a housetop, sees a sheet full of animals descend three times and is told by a voice to kill and eat, and that what God has cleansed he must not call unclean. The Spirit tells Peter to go with the three men sent by Cornelius, and he travels to Caesarea with some brothers from Joppa. There Peter tells the gathered household that God shows no favoritism and preaches about Jesus Christ, and the Holy Spirit falls on all who hear, including Gentiles. Peter then commands that they be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ, and they ask him to stay some days.

Chapter 11

Peter returns to Jerusalem, where those of the circumcision criticize him for entering and eating with uncircumcised men. Peter explains in order his vision at Joppa, the Spirit's instruction to go with the three men from Caesarea, the angel's message to the man in Caesarea, and the Holy Spirit falling on the Gentiles. The Jerusalem believers fall silent and glorify God for granting the Gentiles repentance to life. Meanwhile, scattered believers preach in Antioch, where many Hellenists believe, and the Jerusalem assembly sends Barnabas, who brings Saul from Tarsus; the disciples are first called Christians there. Agabus predicts a great famine, and the Antioch disciples send relief to the Judean brothers through Barnabas and Saul.

Chapter 12

King Herod kills James, the brother of John, and arrests Peter during the days of unleavened bread, intending to bring him out to the people after the Passover. While the assembly prays constantly, an angel of the Lord frees Peter from the prison, and Peter goes to the house of Mary, the mother of John called Mark. The servant girl Rhoda recognizes his voice but the gathered believers do not believe her until they open the gate and see him. Peter tells them to report to James and the brothers, then leaves. Herod has the guards put to death, and at Caesarea an angel of the Lord strikes him down after the crowd calls his speech the voice of a god; the word of God grows, and Barnabas and Saul return to Jerusalem with John called Mark.

Chapter 13

In Chapter 13, the Holy Spirit directs the prophets and teachers at Antioch to separate Barnabas and Saul, who are sent out and sail to Cyprus with John as attendant. At Paphos, the sorcerer Elymas (Bar Jesus) opposes them before the proconsul Sergius Paulus, and Paul strikes him with blindness, after which the proconsul believes. John leaves them at Perga and returns to Jerusalem, while Paul and Barnabas go on to Antioch of Pisidia, where Paul preaches in the synagogue, tracing Israel's history to David and proclaiming Jesus' resurrection and forgiveness of sins. The next Sabbath almost the whole city gathers, the Jews contradict and blaspheme, and Paul and Barnabas turn to the Gentiles, many of whom believe. The Jews stir up a persecution that drives the two out, and they go to Iconium while the disciples are filled with joy and the Holy Spirit.

Chapter 14

In Iconium, Paul and Barnabas speak in the synagogue and many Jews and Greeks believe, but unbelieving Jews turn the Gentiles against them and the city divides. When a plot to stone them arises, they flee to Lystra and Derbe in Lycaonia and preach the Good News. At Lystra Paul heals a man lame from birth, and the crowd takes Barnabas and Paul for Jupiter and Mercury and a priest prepares to sacrifice to them, until the apostles tear their clothes and plead with the people to turn to the living God. Jews from Antioch and Iconium persuade the crowds, stone Paul and drag him out of the city, but he rises and goes on to Derbe. The two then revisit the cities, strengthen disciples, appoint elders, return to Antioch, and report that God had opened a door of faith to the nations.

Chapter 15

Men from Judea teach that Gentile believers must be circumcised to be saved, which causes discord with Paul and Barnabas in Antioch. Paul, Barnabas, and others are sent to Jerusalem, where some believing Pharisees insist on circumcision and keeping the law of Moses. Peter argues that God gave the Gentiles the Holy Spirit and saved them by grace, and James proposes that only a few things be asked of them: abstaining from idol pollution, sexual immorality, strangled meat, and blood. The apostles, elders, and assembly send a letter with Judas Barsabbas and Silas to Antioch, where it is read with rejoicing. Afterward Paul and Barnabas quarrel over taking Mark, and separate: Barnabas sails to Cyprus with Mark, while Paul takes Silas through Syria and Cilicia.

Chapter 16

Paul travels through Derbe and Lystra, where he circumcises Timothy, a disciple well spoken of by the brothers, and takes him along while delivering the Jerusalem decrees to the cities. The Holy Spirit forbids them to speak in Asia and disallows entry into Bithynia, and at Troas Paul sees a vision of a Macedonian man begging for help, so the company goes to Macedonia. At Philippi, Lydia, a seller of purple from Thyatira, listens, is baptized with her household, and hosts them. After Paul casts a spirit of divination out of a girl, her masters drag Paul and Silas before the magistrates, who have them beaten and jailed. An earthquake opens the prison, the jailer and his household believe and are baptized, and the next day Paul objects that he and Silas are Romans, so the frightened magistrates plead with them to leave the city, and they visit Lydia's house and the brothers before departing.

Chapter 17

Paul and Silas travel through Amphipolis and Apollonia to Thessalonica, where Paul reasons from the Scriptures in the synagogue for three Sabbaths and some Jews, many devout Greeks, and prominent women believe. The unpersuaded Jews stir up a mob, attack Jason's house, and drag Jason and other brothers before the city rulers, who take security and release them. Paul and Silas are sent by night to Beroea, where the Jews examine the Scriptures daily and many believe, until Jews from Thessalonica arrive and agitate the crowds. Paul is escorted to Athens while Silas and Timothy remain behind. In Athens, provoked by the idols, Paul debates in the synagogue and marketplace and is brought before the Areopagus by Epicurean and Stoic philosophers, where he speaks about the unknown God, repentance, and the resurrection, with mixed responses and a few believers including Dionysius and Damaris.

Chapter 18

Paul leaves Athens for Corinth, where he lives and works with the tent makers Aquila and Priscilla, who had left Rome under Claudius's order. He reasons in the synagogue, and when the Jews oppose him he turns to the Gentiles, staying in Justus's house; Crispus and many Corinthians believe, and the Lord encourages him in a vision. The Jews bring Paul before the proconsul Gallio, who refuses to judge a dispute about their law, drives them off, and ignores the beating of Sosthenes by the Greeks. Paul then sails with Priscilla and Aquila to Ephesus, leaves them there, goes on to Caesarea, Antioch, and Galatia and Phrygia. Meanwhile Apollos, an eloquent Alexandrian Jew, comes to Ephesus, is taught more accurately by Priscilla and Aquila, and goes on to Achaia, where he refutes the Jews from the Scriptures.

Chapter 19

Paul arrives at Ephesus and finds about twelve disciples who knew only John's baptism; after he explains and they are baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus, the Holy Spirit comes on them when he lays hands on them. Paul speaks in the synagogue for three months, then teaches daily in the school of Tyrannus for two years, while God works miracles through him. Seven sons of Sceva fail to exorcise an evil spirit using Jesus's name and flee beaten, which leads to fear, confessions, and the burning of magical books worth fifty thousand pieces of silver. The silversmith Demetrius stirs the craftsmen to anger over the threat to the trade in Artemis shrines, producing a riot in the theater where Gaius and Aristarchus are seized. The town clerk quiets the crowd, tells them to use the courts or regular assembly, warns of the danger of accusation over the riot, and dismisses the assembly.

Chapter 20

After the uproar, Paul left for Macedonia and Greece, spent three months there, and returned through Macedonia when a plot by Jews threatened him; a group of companions went ahead to Troas. At Troas, a young man named Eutychus fell from a third-floor window while Paul spoke late into the night, and Paul embraced him and declared his life was in him, so he was brought in alive. Paul then traveled by land and ship toward Miletus, bypassing Ephesus because he was hastening to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost. At Miletus he called the Ephesian elders and gave a farewell speech recalling his ministry, warning of coming trials and false teachers, and urging them to shepherd the flock. He knelt and prayed with them, and they wept and accompanied him to the ship, grieved that they would see his face no more.

Chapter 21

Paul and his companions sail from Cos and Rhodes to Patara, then Tyre, Ptolemais, and Caesarea, where disciples at Tyre and the prophet Agabus warn that Paul will be bound in Jerusalem. Paul insists he is ready to be bound and even die for the Lord Jesus, and the party goes up to Jerusalem. There James and the elders tell Paul that many believing Jews are zealous for the law and have heard he teaches Jews to forsake Moses, so they urge him to pay for and join four men in a vow and purification. While Paul is in the temple, Jews from Asia stir up the crowd, accusing him of teaching against the people, the law, and the temple and of bringing Greeks in, having seen Trophimus the Ephesian with him in the city. The crowd drags Paul out and tries to kill him, but the commanding officer arrives with soldiers, arrests and chains him, mistakes him for an Egyptian rebel, and finally permits him to address the people in Hebrew from the stairs.

Chapter 22

In Acts 22, Paul addresses a hostile Jerusalem crowd in Hebrew, describing his upbringing under Gamaliel, his former persecution of the Way, and his encounter with Jesus of Nazareth on the road to Damascus. He recounts being blinded, healed by Ananias, told to be baptized, and later commissioned in a temple trance to go to the Gentiles. The crowd erupts at the mention of the Gentiles and calls for his death. The commanding officer orders Paul scourged in the barracks, but Paul reveals he is a Roman citizen by birth, causing the examiners to withdraw and the officer to fear. The next day the officer releases Paul from his bonds and brings him before the chief priests and council.

Chapter 23

Paul addresses the council, is struck on the high priest Ananias's order, and rebukes him before apologizing on learning of his office. Paul then declares himself a Pharisee judged over the resurrection of the dead, dividing the Pharisees and Sadducees, and the commanding officer removes him to the barracks for safety. The Lord tells Paul he must testify in Rome, while more than forty Jews bind themselves under a curse to kill him and ask the chief priests and elders to help by requesting that he be brought down to the council. Paul's sister's son learns of the ambush and warns Paul and the commanding officer, who sends Paul by night under heavy military escort to the governor Felix in Caesarea with a letter stating that Paul faces no charge worthy of death or imprisonment. Felix learns Paul is from Cilicia, promises to hear him when his accusers arrive, and orders him kept in Herod's palace.

Chapter 24

Five days after Paul's transfer, the high priest Ananias, some elders, and the orator Tertullus come to the governor Felix and accuse Paul of being a plague, an instigator of insurrections among Jews, a ringleader of the Nazarenes, and of trying to profane the temple. Paul answers that he went to Jerusalem to worship only twelve days earlier, that no one found him disputing or stirring a crowd, and that he serves God according to the Way and hopes in the resurrection of the dead. Felix, who knows the Way well, defers the case until the commanding officer Lysias comes down, and keeps Paul in custody with some privileges. Felix and his wife Drusilla later hear Paul speak about faith in Christ Jesus, and Felix is terrified when Paul reasons about righteousness, self-control, and judgment to come. Felix hopes for a bribe and talks with Paul often, but after two years, when Porcius Festus succeeds him, he leaves Paul in bonds to gain favor with the Jews.

Chapter 25

Festus, newly arrived in his province, goes up to Jerusalem, where the high priest and leading Jews ask him to summon Paul there, plotting to kill him on the way. Festus instead holds the hearing at Caesarea, where the Jews bring grievous charges they cannot prove and Paul denies wrongdoing against the Jewish law, the temple, or Caesar. When Festus proposes a trial in Jerusalem, Paul appeals to Caesar, and Festus, after conferring with the council, agrees to send him. King Agrippa and Bernice then arrive, Festus explains the case to Agrippa, and Agrippa asks to hear Paul. Festus brings Paul before Agrippa and the assembled men, saying he found nothing worthy of death and needs something definite to write to the emperor.

Chapter 26

Paul, permitted by King Agrippa to speak, makes his defense, describing his early life as a Pharisee, his persecution of those who followed Jesus of Nazareth, and his encounter with a heavenly light and the voice of Jesus on the road to Damascus. He explains that he obeyed the vision by preaching repentance to Jews and Gentiles, and that the Jews seized him in the temple for it. Festus interrupts, calling Paul crazy, but Paul insists he speaks truth and appeals to Agrippa's belief in the prophets. Agrippa asks whether Paul is trying to make him a Christian, and Paul prays that all who hear him might become as he is, except for his bonds. Agrippa, Festus and Bernice withdraw and agree Paul has done nothing worthy of death or bonds, and Agrippa says he could have been freed had he not appealed to Caesar.

Chapter 27

Paul and other prisoners are handed to the centurion Julius and sail toward Italy, first on a ship of Adramyttium and then on a ship of Alexandria from Myra, with Aristarchus accompanying them. After difficult sailing along Crete, Paul warns that continuing will bring loss, but Julius heeds the master and owner, and the majority choose to sail on from Fair Havens toward Phoenix. The stormy wind Euroclydon drives the ship for many days, the crew jettisons cargo and tackle, and hope is lost until Paul reports an angel's promise that all aboard will live though the ship will be lost. On the fourteenth night, Paul stops the sailors' attempt to flee in the boat, urges everyone to eat, and the 276 people aboard are fed. The ship runs aground where two seas meet and breaks up, Julius prevents the soldiers from killing the prisoners in order to save Paul, and everyone reaches land safely by swimming or on planks.

Chapter 28

After escaping the sea, Paul and his companions learn they are on Malta, where the natives show them kindness, Paul survives a viper bite, and he heals the father of the chief man Publius and other sick islanders. After three months they sail in an Alexandrian ship via Syracuse, Rhegium and Puteoli to Rome, where brothers meet them on the way. In Rome Paul, guarded by a soldier, explains to the Jewish leaders why he is a prisoner and testifies about God's Kingdom and Jesus from the law and the prophets. Some believe and some do not, and Paul quotes Isaiah and declares that God's salvation is sent to the nations. He stays two years in his rented house, preaching boldly without hindrance.
