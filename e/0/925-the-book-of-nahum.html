The Book of Nahum

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

The Book of Nahum, in three chapters, is a prophetic oracle against Nineveh delivered through Nahum the Elkoshite. The central figures are the LORD, portrayed as a jealous, avenging yet slow-to-anger God who shelters those who trust him; Nineveh, the bloody city and its king of Assyria, who face judgment; and Judah, who receives good news. Chapter 1 establishes God's power over nature and his role as stronghold and avenger, promises to break the yoke from one addressed party, and pronounces doom on another, whose name, idols, and line will be cut off. It closes with a messenger announcing peace and telling Judah to keep its feasts and vows because the wicked one is cut off. Chapter 2 turns to the assault: an attacker with red shields and flashing chariots storms Nineveh, the river gates open, the palace dissolves, people flee, and the city is plundered and left empty. The lions' den is mourned, and the LORD of Armies declares he is against Nineveh. Chapter 3 pronounces woe on the city for lies, slaughter, prostitution, and witchcraft, promises public shame, and compares Nineveh to No-Amon, which fell despite its allies. Fortresses fall like ripe figs, fire and sword devour, and the king of Assyria suffers an incurable wound, welcomed by clapping because of his endless cruelty.

The Book of Nahum / Chapter 1

Chapter 1 of Nahum opens as a revelation about Nineveh, given through Nahum the Elkoshite. It portrays the LORD as a jealous, avenging God who is slow to anger yet great in power, whose presence makes the sea, rivers, mountains, and earth quake and wither. The LORD is described as a stronghold for those who take refuge in him, but he will make a full end of the place of his enemies with an overflowing flood and pursue them into darkness. The LORD addresses one party, promising to break the yoke and bonds laid on them, and pronounces doom on another: no descendants will bear its name, its idols will be cut off, and a grave will be made for it. The chapter ends with a messenger on the mountains announcing peace and telling Judah to keep its feasts and vows, because the wicked one is utterly cut off.

Chapter 2

Chapter 2 describes an attacker, called 'he who dashes in pieces', advancing against Nineveh, and the city is told to guard its fortress and strengthen itself. The attacking army is shown with red shields, scarlet-clad warriors, and flashing chariots that rage through the streets. Nineveh's defense collapses: the river gates open, the palace dissolves, and the people flee while the city is plundered of silver and gold and left empty, void, and waste. The text asks where the lions' den has gone, recalling how the lion once tore prey for his cubs and lionesses with no one to make them afraid. The LORD of Armies declares that he is against Nineveh, and says he will burn her chariots, cut the sword through her young lions, cut off her prey, and silence her messengers.

Chapter 3

Chapter 3 pronounces woe on the bloody city, Nineveh, describing its lies, robbery, chariots, slaughter, and heaps of corpses, and blaming its prostitution and witchcraft that sell nations and families. The LORD of Armies declares he is against the city and will expose its nakedness and shame, make it a spectacle, and leave it with no one to mourn or comfort it. Nineveh is compared to No-Amon, which had water, Cush, Egypt, Put, and Libya for defense yet was carried into captivity, its children dashed to pieces and its great men chained. Nineveh's fortresses are said to fall like ripe figs, its troops are women, its gates are open, and fire and sword will devour it despite preparations for siege. The chapter ends by addressing the king of Assyria, whose shepherds slumber and whose people are scattered, with a wound that cannot be healed and that all who hear of it greet by clapping because of his endless cruelty.
