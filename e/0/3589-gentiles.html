Abstract written by a model

In The Acts of the Apostles, the Gentiles receive God’s word, the Holy Spirit, baptism, and repentance to life, and the council decides not to burden them with circumcision. In The Acts of the Apostles, the Gentiles at Antioch gather to hear the message, rejoice, glorify God’s word, believe, and spread it. In Paul’s Letter to the Romans, the Gentiles are described as non-Jewish peoples who are justified through faith, grafted into Israel’s olive tree, warned not to boast, and shown praising God and owing material service to Jerusalem’s saints. In Paul’s Letter to the Galatians, the Gentiles are the people among whom Paul preaches, including Titus the Greek, and whom Peter once compelled to live as Jews. In Paul’s Letter to the Ephesians, the Gentiles are described as once separate from Christ but now fellow citizens with the saints and members of God’s household, though readers are told not to walk in their former futility.

Gentiles in The Acts of the Apostles

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where Gentiles first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Gentiles are non-Jewish people who come to receive God's word. In Caesarea, Cornelius's household hears Peter, receives the Holy Spirit, speaks in other languages, magnifies God, and is baptized in Jesus Christ's name. Jerusalem believers then conclude God has granted them repentance to life. Later, their turning to God brings the brothers great joy, and the council decides not to burden them with circumcision, asking only a few necessary things.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 10

Cornelius and the household gathered with him in Caesarea are Gentiles, whom Peter, a Jew, would normally consider unlawful to visit or join himself to. They listen to Peter's message, and the Holy Spirit is poured out on them, with the gift evidenced by their speaking in other languages and magnifying God. Peter then commands that they be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ.

Chapter 11

The Gentiles are reported to have received the word of God, and the Holy Spirit fell on them as it did on the apostles at the beginning. The Jerusalem believers conclude that God has granted them repentance to life.

Chapter 15

The Gentiles are said to have turned to God and received the Holy Spirit, and their conversion brings great joy to the brothers. The council decides not to trouble them with circumcision, asking only that they keep a few necessary things.

Gentiles in Paul’s Letter to the Romans

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where Gentiles first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Gentiles are the non-Jewish peoples, set alongside Jews throughout the records. They lack the law yet act by nature as a law to themselves, with the work of the law written in their hearts and conscience accusing or excusing them. Called Greeks in places, they are, with Jews, all under sin, but God is their God too and justifies the uncircumcised through faith. Though they did not pursue righteousness, they attained the righteousness of faith, and some were called as God's people. Through Israel's fall they received salvation and mercy, pictured as a wild olive grafted contrary to nature into the olive tree, sharing its root. They are warned not to boast, since they stand by faith and could be cut off. Their fullness must come in before Israel's hardening ends. Finally, they are shown praising God, hoping in Jesse's root, and, having shared the Jerusalem saints' spiritual things, owing them material service, which Macedonia and Achaia have given.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

5 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 2

Gentiles who don't have the law are said to do by nature the things of the law, and so are a law to themselves. They show the work of the law written in their hearts, with conscience and thoughts accusing or excusing them. God's name is blasphemed among them because of the Jew's disobedience, and the physically uncircumcised who fulfill the law will judge the lawbreaking Jew.

Chapter 3

Gentiles, called Greeks in places, are said along with Jews to be all under sin. God is their God also, and he justifies the uncircumcised through faith.

Chapter 9

The Gentiles, who did not follow after righteousness, attained righteousness, namely the righteousness that is of faith. God also called some of his people from among them, in line with Hosea's words that those who were not his people would be called his people and children of the living God.

Chapter 11

The Gentiles received salvation through Israel's fall, and are pictured as a wild olive grafted in among the branches, sharing the root and richness of the olive tree. They are warned not to boast or be conceited, because they stand by faith and will be cut off if they do not continue in God's goodness. Their fullness must come in before Israel's hardening ends, and they obtained mercy through Israel's disobedience.

Chapter 15

Scripture quotations say the Gentiles will praise God, rejoice with his people, and hope in the root of Jesse who arises to rule over them. Paul says their offering is made acceptable, sanctified by the Holy Spirit. Having shared in the spiritual things of the Jerusalem saints, they owe them service in material things, which Macedonia and Achaia have done.

Gentiles in Paul’s Letter to the Ephesians

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where Gentiles first appears

Abstract written by a model

The Gentiles, called "uncircumcision," are a people whose standing changes across the records. Chapter 2 describes them as once separate from Christ, alienated from Israel, strangers to the covenants, without hope and without God, but now made near by Christ's blood, fellow citizens with the saints and members of God's household. Chapter 3 declares them fellow heirs, members of the body and partakers of the promise through the Good News; Paul, a prisoner for them, preaches Christ's unsearchable riches to them. Chapter 4 contrasts this by describing their walk in futility, darkened understanding, hardened hearts, and lust, and readers are told not to walk as they do.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 2

The Gentiles, called "uncircumcision," were once separate from Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of Israel, strangers to the covenants of promise, without hope and without God in the world. Now they are made near in the blood of Christ and are no longer strangers and foreigners but fellow citizens with the saints and members of God's household.

Chapter 3

The Gentiles are declared to be fellow heirs, fellow members of the body, and fellow partakers of God's promise in Christ Jesus through the Good News. Paul is a prisoner on their behalf, and was given grace to preach the unsearchable riches of Christ to them. Paul says his troubles for them are their glory.

Chapter 4

The Gentiles are described as walking in the futility of their mind, darkened in understanding and alienated from the life of God through ignorance and hardness of heart. Having become callous, they gave themselves up to lust and uncleanness with greediness, and the readers are told not to walk as they do.
