Lao Tzŭ in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where Lao Tzŭ first appears

Abstract written by a model

Lao Tzŭ is presented as a great Prophet and Sage, older than Confucius and possibly interviewed by him, whose teachings center on TAO, virtue, naturalness, humility, and inaction. TAO is the clue to human and divine things, understood through the unity and regularity of creation rather than through fixed words, rules, or deliberate striving. He cultivates TAO and virtue while seeking concealment, withdrawing from view amid dynastic decay, yet he repeatedly instructs others, including Confucius, Nan Yung Ch'u, and Yang Tzŭ Chü. His teaching begins with returning good for evil and pursuing a higher life, then develops through criticism of energetic cleverness, artificial government, charity, duty, ceremonies, books, and rigid distinctions when treated as ultimate principles. He holds that rulers should influence invisibly, leave natural goodness uninterfered with, and approach government through inaction. He explains preservation of life as keeping all in ONE, knowing when to stop, avoiding care and excessive knowledge, and rejecting disturbance and artificiality. In later teachings he stresses the harmony arising from passive Heaven and active Earth, natural rather than cultivated virtue, and perfect goodness through recognizing creation’s unity. His practical moral posture values gentleness, humility, weakness, the lowest place, moderation, non-aggression, and sympathy with creation. He dies and is intensely mourned, while later records dispute the genuineness of the book associated with him and question how central Quietism and Inaction were to his undisputed teaching.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

14 sections; each heading opens the text

_Introduction._

Lao Tzŭ was a seventh-century B.C. Prophet who taught return of good for evil, a higher life, and the clue to human and divine things. He called this clue TAO and taught that doing nothing could allow all things to be done. His sayings influenced Chuang Tzŭ, although the existence and genuineness of the book later associated with him are disputed.

_Note on the Philosophy of Chaps._ i-vii.

Lao Tzŭ is described as older than Confucius and as having possibly met him. The note questions whether Quietism and the glorification of Inaction were as prominent in Lao Tzŭ’s undisputed teachings as they became in Chuang Tzŭ.

The triumph of Confucianism in the centuries which intervened between

Lao Tzŭ is portrayed as cultivating the TAO and virtue, seeking to remain concealed and unknown. His view of the TAO is associated with the hidden and inward, in contrast with Confucius' practical emphasis, though the unit suggests the two may originally have been philosophically close.

CHAPTER III.

Lao Tzŭ dies, causing people to mourn him intensely. Ch’in Shih argues that Lao Tzŭ’s words and tears had increased human emotion and forgotten the source from which life was received.

CHAPTER V.

Lao Tzŭ tells No-toes that Confucius could be freed from his fetters by being met with the continuity of life and death and the identity of can and cannot. No-toes replies that Confucius has been punished by God.

CHAPTER VII.

He says that energetic cleverness and deliberate pursuit make a ruler a mere handicraftsman. He teaches that a wise ruler's goodness influences the whole empire invisibly and is based upon the baseless.

CHAPTER XI.

Lao Tzŭ teaches that the natural goodness of the human heart should not be interfered with. He also states that governing the empire should be approached through inaction.

CHAPTER XII.

Lao Tzŭ is associated with the Record and with teachings about TAO, inaction, and the natural order. He tells Confucius that rigid rules and distinctions are merely handicraft skill and that true understanding cannot be fully expressed in words.

CHAPTER XIII.

Lao Tzŭ rejects Confucius’s doctrines of charity and duty to one's neighbour and urges following TAO and the regularity of the universe. He accepts classifications that accord with truth and teaches that TAO, virtue, charity, duty, ceremonies, music, books, and words must not be confused with essentials.

CHAPTER XIV.

Lao Tzŭ tells Confucius that TAO cannot be presented, imparted, or given unless there is suitable inner endowment and outward correctness. He criticizes charity, duty to one's neighbour, the Six Canons, and the administrations of ancient rulers as inadequate or harmful when treated as fixed doctrines.

CHAPTER XXI.

Lao Tzŭ tells Confucius that he was wandering in the unborn and explains the interaction of passive Heaven and active Earth as producing harmony and all things. He teaches that perfect goodness and happiness come from recognizing the unity of creation and that the virtue of the perfect man is natural rather than cultivated.

CHAPTER XXIII.

Lao Tzŭ questioned Nan Yung Ch'u before hearing his intended questions and recognized his confusion. He taught Nan Yung Ch'u that preserving life requires keeping all in ONE, knowing when to stop, attending to oneself, and remaining without care and excessive knowledge. He further explained the conditions of the perfect man, the nature of TAO, and the need to reject disturbances and artificiality.

CHAPTER XXVII.

Lao Tzŭ told Yang Tzŭ Chü that he seemed overbearing and taught that the truly pure behave as though sullied and those with abundant virtue behave as though they lack enough. His instruction changed Yang Tzŭ Chü’s spirit and conduct.

CHAPTER XXXIII.

Lao Tzŭ taught that strength should accept weakness, purity should endure disgrace, and people should follow rather than precede others. He emphasized depth, moderation, non-aggression, and liberal sympathy with creation, and the text calls him a true Sage of old.
