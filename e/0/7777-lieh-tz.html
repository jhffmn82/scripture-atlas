Lieh Tzŭ

person · 1 work · 13 quoted facts · 6 connections
Also called: Lieh Yü K'ou

Abstract written by a model

Lieh Tzŭ is an allegorical, possibly invented figure associated with TAO, whose historical existence is doubtful. He can ride the wind but remains dependent on it, so he has not reached complete freedom. After admiring Chi Han’s magic and thinking it superior to Hu Tzŭ’s TAO, Hu Tzŭ’s demonstrations show him his lack of real knowledge. He practices for three years, discards artificiality, and returns to natural laws. He reflects on life, death, and transformation after encountering an old skull; asks Kuan Yin about the perfect man’s invulnerability; and is shown through archery and fear on a precipice to lack TAO internally. Though said to have attained TAO, he lives poorly in Chêng and refuses food from Tzŭ Yang. Later he withdraws from Ch'i when his visible inner truth and influence attract attention, while Poh Hun Wu Jen warns that such influence disturbs his natural constitution.

Lieh Tzŭ is in one work on this site, Chuang Tzŭ. This is the abstract a model wrote of it from that work.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER I.

Lieh Tzŭ can ride upon the wind and travel wherever he wishes, but he remains dependent upon the wind. He therefore has not attained complete freedom.

CHAPTER VII.

He is initially infatuated with Chi Han's powers and thinks he knows something more perfect than Hu Tzŭ's TAO. After Hu Tzŭ's demonstrations, he becomes convinced that he lacks real knowledge, practices in earnest for three years, and reverts to the natural.

CHAPTER XVIII.

Lieh Tzŭ sees an old skull while eating by the roadside and speaks to it about the uncertainty of life and death. He describes transformations among plants, insects, animals, and man as part of the great Scheme.

CHAPTER XIX.

Lieh Tzŭ asks Kuan Yin how the perfect man can pass through solid bodies, fire, and great heights without harm. He receives an explanation that such feats arise from absolute purity rather than cunning.

CHAPTER XXI.

Lieh Yü K'ou demonstrates skilled archery under ordinary conditions by shooting while balancing a cup of water on his elbow. He becomes terrified on a precipice, and Po Hun Wu Jên concludes that his internal economy is defective and that he lacks TAO.

CHAPTER XXVIII.

Lieh Tzŭ lived in poverty despite having attained TAO and declined food sent by Tzŭ Yang. He refused because Tzŭ Yang acted on others' reports and might likewise act on reports that condemned him.

CHAPTER XXXII.

Lieh Yü K'ou turns back from Ch'i because five of ten restaurant keepers refuse payment, fearing that his unassimilated inner truth is visible externally and will attract rulers who may entrust him with government. His influence then draws many visitors, and Poh Hun Wu Jen warns that this influence disturbs his natural constitution. The text says his historical existence is doubtful and that he may have been an allegorical personage created by Chuang Tzŭ.
