begging-tour

Also called: begging-tour or in a residence or on a pilgrimage from village to village

Abstract written by a model

A begging-tour is the alms-collecting activity undertaken by a monk or nun, chiefly a regulated setting for seeking and receiving food, water, drink, raw substances, and other offerings. It begins with restrictions on companionship: the mendicant must not travel with certain persons or give them food. Food acceptability is central: offerings connected with entertainments, wedding breakfasts, funeral or family dinners, specified households, guests, sick persons, fire preparation, impurity, or doubt must be avoided. The tour may pass entertainments or householders only under prescribed conditions, with indifference to festival entertainment. Its route must avoid numerous living beings, seeds, sprouts, dew, water, mildew, dust, cobwebs, and obstructing people. Along the way, the monk or nun encounters houses, other alms recipients, obstacles, dangerous beings, animals, and householders; a byway is required when available, and the mendicant waits apart before entering a house already entered by another recipient. The tour concludes in regulated acceptance of portions, without preference, under rules governing sources, preparation, gestures, salt, and offerings.

Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra), Book II, translated by Hermann Jacobi / Second Book / First Part. / First Lecture, Call

During a begging-tour, a monk or nun should not travel together with a heretic, householder, or monk who does not avoid forbidden food. They should also not immediately or mediately give food to such persons.

Second Lesson.

The begging-tour is the setting in which the monk or nun seeks food. During it, the monk or nun must avoid food prepared for certain communal entertainments and must remain indifferent to festive entertainments.

Third Lesson.

During a begging-tour, a monk or nun should reject food and other items about which there are doubts or misgivings concerning acceptability. They should also reject food from specified royal, messenger, and relation-of-king households.

Fourth Lesson.

A begging-tour may include visits to entertainments or householders only under prescribed conditions. The monk or nun should avoid going when the route or gathering contains numerous living beings, seeds, sprouts, water, mildew, dust, cobwebs, or people that obstruct movement and study.

Fifth Lesson.

On a begging-tour, a monk or nun encounters householder's abodes, obstacles, dangerous beings, and other recipients of alms. The monk or nun must use a byway when one exists and must wait apart before entering when another recipient has already entered the house.

Sixth Lesson.

A begging-tour is the setting in which a monk or nun must follow rules concerning animals, householders, gestures, food, salt, and fire-prepared offerings. During it, the mendicant should avoid certain places and refuse offerings that are impure or unacceptable.

Seventh Lesson.

The begging-tour is the context in which a monk or nun receives food and water. During it, the monk or nun must follow restrictions concerning the sources and preparation of offerings.

Eighth Lesson.

The begging-tour is the context in which the rules for accepting food, drink, raw substances, and other offerings apply.

Ninth Lesson.

During a begging-tour, a monk or nun may encounter relations’ houses, food prepared for guests or sick persons, and food or drink of varying quality. The rules require avoiding certain houses and preparations and accepting received portions without preference.
