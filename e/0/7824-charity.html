charity

topic · 1 work · 9 quoted facts · 8 connections
Also called: Charity, Perfect charity, charity and duty to one's neighbour, duty to one's neighbour, true charity

Abstract written by a model

Charity is treated as a contested ethical practice rather than an essential moral quality. In its natural form it arises from TAO and springs from Virtue, including ordinary parental love; when intentionally cultivated, it is called artificial, accidental to the spiritual, and a means of reputation or applause. Some accounts make it part of princedom, capable of attracting and benefiting people but also of being used for advantage or imposed harmfully as universal doctrine. Confucius practices charity toward neighbours, teaches universal love without self, and regards charity with man as necessary for humility. Lao Tzŭ and Chuang Tzŭ question these teachings: perfect charity excludes individual love, universal love may conflict with eliminating self, and talk or preaching of charity distracts from original simplicity and changes human nature. Charity alone cannot prevent mental and bodily wear.

charity in Chuang Tzŭ

Chuang Tzŭ · the work’s page · read it where charity first appears

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER VIII.

Charity is portrayed as an artificial addition when intentionally cultivated for reputation or applause. The chapter argues that preaching charity changes human nature and is not part of the natural moral constitution.

CHAPTER X.

Charity and duty to one's neighbour are described as integral parts of princedom that a ruler can steal together with a State and the Sages' wisdom. The unit calls for them to be discarded so that the people's virtue may become one with God.

CHAPTER XIII.

Charity is presented by Confucius as rejoicing in all things and universal love without self. Lao Tzŭ treats it as an accidental of the spiritual and questions whether universal love and elimination of self contradict themselves.

CHAPTER XIV.

Chuang Tzŭ distinguishes ordinary charity, including the natural love between parents and offspring, from perfect charity, which does not admit of love for the individual. Lao Tzŭ later calls talk of charity a distraction from the world's original simplicity.

Kuan Chung being at the point of death, Duke Huan went to see him.

Charity can attract and benefit people, but it is often used for advantage rather than disinterestedly. Hsü Yu warns that imposing it as a universal doctrine can harm the empire.

CHAPTER XXXI.

Charity is part of the conduct Confucius practices and uses in trying to reform the masses. The old fisherman says that charity alone cannot prevent the wear of mind and tear of body, and Confucius later says that charity with man is necessary for humility before others.
