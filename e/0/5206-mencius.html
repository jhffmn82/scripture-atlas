Abstract written by a model

In Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang), Mencius advises rulers that true royal government depends on caring for the people through benevolent rule. In Mencius, Book II (Gong Sun Chou), Mencius teaches benevolence, righteousness, virtue, and government directed toward the people’s happiness. In Mencius, Book III (Teng Wen Gong), Mencius advocates benevolent government, secure livelihood, proper education, and steadfast adherence to principle. In Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou), Mencius teaches that benevolence, righteousness, propriety, sincerity, and self-examination guide government and personal conduct. In Mencius, Book V (Wan Zhang), Mencius explains filial piety, benevolence, Heaven’s mandate, friendship, office, propriety, and the conduct of scholars and ministers. In Mencius, Book VI (Gaozi), Mencius teaches that human nature and the mind incline toward goodness and that learning can preserve or recover it. In Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), Mencius teaches about human nature, Heaven, moral self-cultivation, righteousness, and proper government.

Mencius in Mencius, Book I (Liang Hui Wang)

Mencius · the work’s page · read it where Mencius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Mencius is a teacher, scholar, adviser, counsellor, and venerable Sir whose sole counsels concern benevolence and righteousness. He repeatedly advises rulers that true royal government depends on caring for the people: sharing pleasures rather than enjoying possessions alone, preventing hunger, lightening taxes and punishments, supporting farming and education, and giving people secure livelihoods. Beginning with King Hui of Liang, he rejects profit as the ruler’s central concern, criticizes famine amid abundance in the ruler’s household, and argues that even a small territory can attain royal dignity through benevolent government. He then judges rulers by their willingness to avoid killing and unite the kingdom under humane rule. With King Xuan of Qi, he develops these counsels through compassion, shared music and parks, proper treatment of states, worthy ministers, public judgment, and restraint in conquest. He explains that Zhou was cut off as a fellow who violated benevolence and righteousness, not as a sovereign put to death. In advice concerning Yan, he makes the people’s pleasure decisive and urges restoration, consultation, withdrawal, and benevolent government after harmful conduct. He also counsels Duke Mu and Duke Wen of Teng about famine, defense, threatened fortifications, doing good, and accepting Heaven’s ultimate determination. At the end, when a planned visit from Duke Ping of Lu is prevented, he concludes that Heaven determines whether he will find a suitable ruler.

Mencius in Mencius, Book II (Gong Sun Chou)

Mencius · the work’s page · read it where Mencius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Mencius is a man and high dignitary associated with Qi who teaches and advocates benevolence, righteousness, virtue, and government directed toward the people’s happiness. He says that human accord is stronger than advantages of time or situation, that virtue wins sincere submission while force does not, and that rulers should honor virtue, employ able people, and adopt policies that gain popular support. He holds that all people have a mind unable to bear others’ suffering and should develop their human principles. He attained an unperturbed mind at forty, understands words, skillfully nourishes a vast, flowing passion-nature, and seeks to emulate Confucius rather than the narrowness of Bo Yi or the deficient self-respect of Hui of Liu Xia. In Qi he is respected by the king, yet shows greatest respect by speaking to him about the ways of Yao and Shun. He acts independently because he holds no office requiring either action or advice, although he also serves as a high dignitary and undertakes a condolence mission to Teng. His conduct distinguishes appropriate acceptance of money for travel or protection from refusal when he has no need. He buries his mother in Lu, gives up office, declines a house, allowance, and 100,000 zhong, and rejects the appearance of seeking riches or honor. Dissatisfied with Qi’s ruler but not eager to depart, he hopes daily for the king’s change of mind because the king could enable happiness for Qi and the whole kingdom. He finally leaves Qi, dwells in Xiu, and remains longer than intended when troop collection makes departure improper, maintaining that he could bring tranquillity and good order if Heaven wished it.

Mencius, Book II (Gong Sun Chou) / Chapter 1

Mencius argues that raising Qi to royal dignity would be as easy as turning the hand. He explains that the conditions of the present time, including the people’s suffering and Qi’s resources, make benevolent government especially effective.

Chapter 2

Mencius says that he attained an unperturbed mind at forty and that he understands words and is skilled in nourishing his vast, flowing passion-nature. He explains the relation between the will and passion-nature, discusses righteousness and government, and says he wishes to learn to be like Confucius.

Chapter 3

Mencius distinguishes between using force to pretend to benevolence and using virtue to practise benevolence. He explains that virtue leads people to submit sincerely, while force produces submission without heartfelt loyalty.

Chapter 4

Mencius says that benevolence brings glory to a prince and its opposite brings disgrace. He advises a prince to esteem virtue, honor virtuous scholars, appoint worthy people to dignity, and give able people positions of trust.

Chapter 5

Mencius says that a ruler should honour men of talents and virtue, employ the able, and adopt five policies that win the support of the people.

Chapter 6

Mencius teaches that all men possess a mind that cannot bear to see the sufferings of others. He explains the four human principles and urges men to develop them fully.

Chapter 7

Mencius compares the arrow-maker with the maker of armour and says that choosing a profession requires great caution. He teaches that people who fail to practise benevolence should seek the cause of their failure in themselves.

Chapter 9

Mencius describes Bo Yi's refusal to serve or associate with people he disapproved of and Hui of Liu Xia's willingness to serve an impure prince while maintaining his principles. He says Bo Yi was narrow-minded and Hui of Liu Xia was wanting in self-respect, and that the superior man manifests neither quality.

Chapter 1

Mencius teaches that opportunities of time vouchsafed by Heaven, advantages of situation afforded by the Earth, and the union arising from the accord of Men differ in strength, with human accord being greatest. He concludes that the true ruler prefers not to fight but must overcome if he fights.

Chapter 2

Mencius declines to attend the king’s court because he says he is unwell, though he visits a member of the Dong Guo family the next day. He later explains to Jing Chou that speaking to the king about the ways of Yao and Shun is the greatest respect he can show him, and that a prince seeking great deeds should go to virtuous ministers rather than summon them.

Chapter 3

Mencius says he acted rightly in all three cases. He accepted money in Song for travelling expenses and in Xue to help procure arms, but refused it in Qi because he had no need for money.

Chapter 4

Mencius addresses Kong Ju Xin in Ping Lu about the deaths and displacement of the people during bad calamitous years and years of famine. He later tells the king that Kong Ju Xin is the only governor among five who knows his faults.

Chapter 5

Mencius questions Qi Wa about whether he has found anything to speak about after becoming chief criminal judge. He later explains that people responsible for an office or for giving opinions should leave when their duties cannot be fulfilled or their words are ignored, but that he may act freely because he holds no office and has no such duty.

Chapter 6

Mencius, occupying the position of a high dignitary in Qi, went on a mission of condolence to Teng. He did not discuss the business of the mission with Wang Huan because proper officers attended to it.

Chapter 7

Mencius went from Qi to Lu to bury his mother and stopped at Ying while returning to Qi. He defended using good wood for the coffin, saying that traditional practices satisfied natural feelings and that a superior man is not niggardly toward his parents.

Chapter 8

Mencius said that Yan might be smitten because Zi Kuai had no right to give it to another man and Zi Zhi had no right to receive it. He denied advising Qi to smite Yan, explaining that he had answered only the question of whether it might be smitten.

Chapter 9

Mencius said that Zhou Gong was an ancient sage and did not know Guan Shu would rebel. He taught that superior men of old reformed their errors, whereas superior men of the present persist in them and apologize for them.

Chapter 10

Mencius gave up his office and arranged to return to his native State. He said that he desired to visit the king, though he did not request permission to do so at a particular time. He declined the king's proposed house and allowance, saying that accepting 10,000 zhong would not be the conduct of one desiring riches.

Chapter 11

Mencius left Qi and spent the night in Zhou. He ignored a visitor's initial speech by sleeping, then explained that he was not being treated as Zi Si had been treated by the duke Mou.

Chapter 12

Mencius leaves Qi after not finding in the king a ruler to suit him, but says he did not desire to leave and hoped the king would change and recall him. He maintains that serving the king could bring happiness to the people of the whole kingdom, and he distinguishes his conduct from that of small-minded people who leave in anger after their remonstrances are rejected.

Chapter 13

Mencius left Qi. When Chong Yu questioned his dissatisfied appearance, Mencius explained that the expected time for a true royal sovereign had arrived and that he could bring tranquillity and good order to the kingdom if Heaven wished it.

Chapter 14

Mencius left Qi and dwelt in Xiu. He intended to leave after seeing the king in Chong, declined to receive salary, and later remained because orders for the collection of troops made it improper to ask permission to leave.

Mencius is a teacher, philosopher, and superior man in Zou who advocates benevolent government and holds principles he will not improperly bend. He teaches that human nature is good, repeatedly praises Yao and Shun, and presents Teng as capable of becoming a good State. His political teaching begins with personal example and proper filial conduct: rulers should fulfill funeral duties, seek remedies in themselves, and lead their inferiors. It develops into a program for the people’s secure livelihood, fair land arrangements, taxation and mutual aid, education, and the nine-squares system. He rejects Xu Xing’s universal self-sufficiency and market doctrine, defending exchange, specialized occupations, government, and the work of ancient sages. In correcting Yi Zhi, he argues that affection begins with parents and that burial arises from natural filial feeling. Mencius refuses to go to princes without invitation, explaining that office and support may properly be desired or received only on proper grounds, never through improper conduct. He defines a great man as one who maintains principles, serves the people when in office, and remains steady through riches, poverty, and power. He also argues that true royal government attracts people, that rulers’ attendants shape their virtue, and that recognized unrighteousness must end without delay. Although not fond of disputing, he says prevailing doctrines compel him to oppose Yang Zhu, Mo Di, and other perverse doctrines. He defends the former sages, seeks to rectify men’s hearts, and criticizes Chen Zhong for conduct inconsistent with his claimed purity.

Mencius in Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou)

Mencius · the work’s page · read it where Mencius first appears

Abstract written by a model

Mencius is a speaker and teacher who presents a connected teaching on government, conduct, and personal responsibility. He holds that virtue alone does not suffice for government: laws must be properly implemented, high stations belong to the benevolent, and rulers must be urged toward difficult achievements, good conduct, and the removal of perversity. Benevolence is the central condition of rule, enabling dynasties to gain the throne and preserving rulers, States, and the kingdom through gaining the people and their hearts. Righteousness, propriety, sincerity, respect, economy, and right principles also guide both rulers and individuals. The teaching moves from models supplied by sages and rulers such as Yao, Shun, king Wen, Bo Yi, and Tai Gong to practical accounts of ministers, princes, families, and the people. It insists that failure in love, government, or politeness calls for inward examination; that character is revealed by words and the pupil of the eye; and that people must follow duties that are near and easy, including loving parents and respecting elders. Mencius also addresses exceptions to ordinary rules, the proper education of sons, service to parents, rectifying a sovereign's mind, praise and reproach, and the evils of teaching others merely for self-interest. It closes by gathering benevolence, righteousness, wisdom, propriety, and music most fully into service to parents and obedience to elder brothers, while extending these principles to rulers, ministers, and public life.

Mencius says that the path of duty lies in what is near and that its work lies in what is easy. He teaches that loving parents and showing due respect to elders would bring tranquillity to the whole land.

Mencius explains the conditions by which those occupying inferior situations can obtain the confidence of the sovereign and succeed in governing the people. He teaches that sincerity is the way of Heaven and that complete sincerity moves others.

Mencius recounts Qiu's service to the head of the Ji family and examines how ministers who enrich rulers should be judged.

Chapter 15

Mencius teaches that the pupil of the eye reveals whether a man's breast is correct. He says that a man's words and pupil together disclose his character.

Chapter 16

Mencius teaches that the respectful do not despise others and the economical do not plunder them. Mencius says that the service of parents is the greatest service and the charge of oneself is the greatest charge. He teaches that preserving oneself enables service to parents and that these duties are roots of other services and charges.

Chapter 20

Mencius teaches that remonstrating with a sovereign about the mal-employment of ministers or blaming errors of government is insufficient. He says that only the great man can rectify what is wrong in the sovereign's mind.

Chapter 21

Mencius says that there are cases of praise which could not be expected, and of reproach when the parties have been seeking to be perfect.

Chapter 22

Mencius says that men's readiness with their tongues arises from not having been reproved.

Chapter 23

Mencius says that the evil of men is that they like to be teachers of others.

Mencius questioned Yue Zheng about his visit and arrival. He taught that a scholar's lodging-house must be arranged before he visits his elder.

Abstract written by a model

Mencius is a philosopher who teaches that human nature and the human mind are properly inclined to good, with feelings corresponding to benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and knowledge. Abstract written by a model

Mencius is a speaker and scholar whose teachings center on human nature, Heaven, shame, benevolence, righteousness, self-cultivation, and proper rule. He begins by connecting the full use of one’s mental constitution with knowing one’s nature and Heaven, while accepting what may rightly be called Heaven’s appointment. He teaches that all things are complete in us, that sincerity tested by self-examination is a great delight, and that reciprocity leads toward perfect virtue. Shame is indispensable: recognizing prior shamelessness can prevent its return. He contrasts habitual, unexamined conduct with the deliberate pursuit of the proper path. His account of character emphasizes innate moral capacities, intuitive knowledge, love for parents and elder brothers, and refusal to do or desire what righteousness forbids. Benevolence and righteousness distinguish humanity, while scholars should preserve their principles in either poverty or prosperity. He portrays superior people as independent of power, territory, population, and reputation, and as able to teach, counsel, transform society, and draw others toward the proper path. Mencius also sets standards for government. A true sovereign makes people deeply content; good government secures ease, preserves life, cultivates fields, lightens taxes, uses resources seasonably, and nourishes the old. He condemns unjust war, excessive taxation, false virtue, uncontrolled desires, and neglect of self-cultivation. Political action must remain subject to righteousness: even banishing a sovereign is usurpation unless it has the same purpose as Yi Yin. Across the chapters, his teaching moves from inner nature and moral discipline to the conduct of scholars, rulers, and society. It closes by presenting benevolence as extending care beyond what one initially values and by reaffirming the superior man’s responsibility to cultivate himself and guide others.

Part I / Chapter 1

Mencius teaches that exhausting one's mental constitution leads a person to know their nature and then Heaven.

Chapter 2

Mencius says that there is an appointment for everything and that a man should submissively receive what may be correctly ascribed to it. He distinguishes between deaths that may and may not be ascribed to Heaven's appointment.

Chapter 3

Mencius distinguishes between seeking that helps produce getting and seeking that does not.

Chapter 4

Mencius teaches that all things are already complete in us. He says that self-examined sincerity is the greatest delight and that vigorous effort at the law of reciprocity brings one closest to perfect virtue.

Chapter 5

Mencius says that acting without understanding, doing so habitually without examination, and pursuing the proper path without knowing its nature is the way of multitudes.

Chapter 6

Mencius says that a man may not be without shame. He teaches that feeling ashamed of having been without shame can prevent future occasions for shame.

Chapter 7

Mencius teaches that the sense of shame is of great importance and questions what a person without it shares with other men.

Chapter 8

Mencius says that the able and virtuous monarchs of antiquity loved virtue and forgot their power. He also says that the able and virtuous scholars of antiquity delighted in their own principles and were oblivious of the power of princes.

Chapter 9

Mencius advises Song Gou Jian about travelling to different courts and securing perfect satisfaction. He teaches that virtue, righteousness, and adherence to the proper path should guide a scholar in poverty or prosperity.

Chapter 10

Mencius says that the mass of men wait for a king Wen before receiving a rousing impulse, while scholars distinguished from the mass rouse themselves without a king Wan.

Chapter 11

Mencius says that a man who receives the families of Han and Wei yet does not become elated when looking upon himself is far beyond the mass of men.

Chapter 12

Mencius says that the people should be employed to secure their ease and put to death to preserve their lives. He says that the people will not murmur under either circumstance when those purposes are intended.

Chapter 13

Mencius contrasts the effects of a chief and a true sovereign on the people. He describes how the superior man transforms society through spiritual influence.

Chapter 14

Mencius compares the effects of kindly words, reputation for kindness, good government, and good instructions.

Chapter 15

Mencius defines intuitive ability and intuitive knowledge, and explains the natural love children have for their parents and elder brothers.

Chapter 16

Mencius describes Shun's life in the deep retired mountains and his powerful response to goodness.

Chapter 17

Mencius says that a man should not do what his own sense of righteousness tells him not to do or desire what it tells him not to desire. He teaches that acting this way is all the man has to do.

Chapter 18

Mencius says that men with intelligent virtue and prudence in affairs generally experience sickness and troubles. He explains that caution under peril and against calamity makes them distinguished for their intelligence.

Chapter 19

Mencius distinguishes persons who serve the prince, ministers who seek the tranquillity of the State, the people of Heaven, and great men by their aims and conduct.

Chapter 20

Mencius states that the superior man has three delights, and that ruling the kingdom is not one of them.

Chapter 21

Mencius explains that the superior man's highest enjoyment does not come from territory, population, or political influence. He teaches that the superior man's nature is fixed by Heaven and consists in benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and knowledge.

Chapter 22

Mencius explains why Bo Yi and Tai Gong sought king Wen and describes what it means for a ruler to nourish the old.

Chapter 23

Mencius says that fields should be well cultivated, taxes should be light, food resources should be used seasonably, and wealth should be spent only on prescribed ceremonies. He teaches that a sage should govern so that pulse and grain become as abundant as water and fire.

Chapter 24

Mencius teaches that broader understanding changes how Lu, the world, other waters, and other people’s words appear. He also explains how to contemplate water and compares the student’s progress to flowing water filling hollows.

Chapter 25

Mencius distinguishes disciples of Shun from disciples of Zhi by whether they earnestly practice virtue or pursue gain. He says that the interval between the thought of gain and the thought of virtue separates Shun from Zhi.

Chapter 26

Mencius compares the principles of Yang, Mo, and Zi Mo. He says that Zi Mo’s medium is nearer the right but becomes harmful when held without regard for circumstances.

Chapter 27

Mencius says that hunger and thirst distort people's taste and injure both the palate and men's minds. He teaches that preventing these conditions from harming the mind removes sorrow about not being equal to other men.

Chapter 28

Mencius says that Hui of Liu Xia would not have changed his firm purpose of life even for the three highest offices of State.

Chapter 29

Mencius compares a man with definite aims to be accomplished to someone digging a well. He teaches that stopping before reaching the spring means the effort is ultimately wasted.

Chapter 30

Mencius says that benevolence and righteousness were natural to Yao and Shun, were made their own by Tang and Wu, and were feigned by the five chiefs of the princes.

Chapter 31

Mencius says that worthy ministers may banish their sovereigns if they have the same purpose as Yi Yin. If they do not have that purpose, Mencius says the act would be usurpation.

Chapter 32

Mencius explains the benefits a superior man brings to a country through his counsel and instruction.

Chapter 33

Mencius replies that the unemployed scholar’s business is to exalt his aim by setting it on benevolence and righteousness.

Chapter 34

Mencius argues that refusing the kingdom of Qi when it is offered contrary to righteousness is only a small righteousness. He says that disowning parents and relatives and rejecting the relations of sovereign and minister, superiors and inferiors, are greater crimes.

Chapter 35

Mencius explains to Tao Ying that Gao Yao would apprehend Gu Sou if Gu Sou had murdered a man. He says Shun would not forbid this because Gao Yao had received the law from a proper source.

Chapter 36

Mencius went from Fan to Qi, saw the king of Qi's son at a distance, and discussed how position alters air and appearance. He also recounted the story of the prince of Lu at the Die Shi gate.

Chapter 37

Mencius says that feeding a scholar without loving him treats him as a pig, and loving him without respecting him keeps him as a domestic animal. He teaches that honouring and respecting precede any offering of gifts.

Chapter 38

Mencius teaches that bodily organs and their functions belong to Heaven-conferred nature, and that a man must be a sage to satisfy the design of his bodily organization.

Chapter 39

He said that merely urging moderation was insufficient and that the proper course was to teach filial piety and fraternal duty. He distinguished between someone prevented from completing mourning and someone who could complete it but neglected it.

Chapter 40

Mencius states that the superior man effects his teaching in five ways.

Chapter 41

Mencius says that a great artificer does not alter or do away with the marking-line for a stupid workman, and that Yi did not change his rule for drawing the bow for a stupid archer. He explains that the superior man draws the bow without discharging the arrow, appears to leap with it to the mark, and stands exactly in the middle of the path.

Chapter 42

Mencius explains that when right principles prevail throughout the kingdom, a person's principles appear along with the person. He also states that he has not heard of principles depending on other men for their manifestation.

Chapter 43

Mencius explains that he does not answer people who presume on nobility, talents, age, services performed for him, or old acquaintance. He says that two of these presumptions applied to Geng of Tang.

Chapter 44

Mencius teaches that stopping where stopping is not allowable leads to stopping in everything. He also says that shabby treatment of those who deserve good treatment leads to shabby treatment of all, and that precipitate advancement is followed by a speedy retreat.

Chapter 45

Mencius states distinctions among the superior man’s attitudes toward inferior creatures, people generally, and his parents.

Chapter 46

Mencius teaches that the wise should embrace all knowledge while focusing on what is of the greatest importance, and that the benevolent should embrace all in their love while cultivating earnest affection for the virtuous.
