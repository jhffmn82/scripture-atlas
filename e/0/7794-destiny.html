Destiny

topic · 1 work · 15 quoted facts · 5 connections
Also called: Fate, book of Fate, destiny, fate, operation of Destiny

Abstract written by a model

Destiny is a topic describing the governing force behind changing conditions and inevitable limits. It governs life and death, existence and non-existence, success and failure, poverty and wealth, good and evil, hunger and thirst, and warmth and cold. It is presented as an unavoidable law and a pre-arrangement with fixed limitations, causing events and, according to Tzŭ Sang, poverty. In the early chapters, Confucius advises leaving difficult outcomes to Destiny; safety comes from recognizing its rule over circumstances. The virtuous person quietly accepts what cannot be changed and moves without offending others. Life and death, including early death, old age, beginning, and end, follow a sequence beyond human interference. Fate against Confucius after futile struggle shows that failure may be fate while success is opportunity, giving the Sage courage. Later, Destiny’s limits are compared to those of form, and pursuit of gain is shown to bring loss. It ultimately remains uncertain because birth’s past and the hereafter are unknown.

Destiny is in one work on this site, Chuang Tzŭ. This is the abstract a model wrote of it from that work.

Destiny in Chuang Tzŭ

Narrative, section by section written by a model

7 sections; each heading opens the text

CHAPTER IV.

It is one of two sources of safety and governs circumstances such as a child's love for its parents. Confucius tells Tzŭ Kao to leave difficult outcomes to Destiny.

CHAPTER V.

Destiny governs changing conditions such as success and failure, poverty and wealth, and good and evil report. The virtuous person recognizes the inevitable, quietly acquiesces, and moves with the tide without offending others.

CHAPTER VI.

Life and death belong to destiny, whose sequence is as inevitable as day and night and beyond human interference. Destiny includes early death, old age, a beginning, and an end.

The walrus envies the centipede;

Fate is said by Confucius to be against him after he struggled against failure in vain. He says that knowing failure is fate and success is opportunity gives the Sage courage in great danger.

CHAPTER XVIII.

Destiny is described as a pre-arrangement that limits beings just as form has limitations. Confucius fears that Yen Yüan may fail to understand these limits when advising the prince of Ch'i.

CHAPTER XX.

Destiny is illustrated through the chain in which the cicada forgets itself, the mantis seizes the cicada, the strange bird seizes the mantis, and Chuang Tzŭ threatens the bird. The episode shows that loss follows the pursuit of gain.

CHAPTER XXVII.

Destiny cannot be affirmed or denied because the hereafter and what preceded birth are unknown. When things turn out as they ought, or otherwise, the agency may be supernatural.
