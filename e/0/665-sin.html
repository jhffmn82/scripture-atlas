Abstract written by a model

In The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach, or Ecclesiasticus, Chapter 21, sin is depicted as a snake whose teeth slay souls and as a two-edged sword whose stroke cannot be healed. In The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach, or Ecclesiasticus, Chapter 27, sin is pictured as a nail wedged between stones in commerce and as a lion lurking for evildoers, with many sinning for profit. In The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach, or Ecclesiasticus: Sin is treated as something one must not minimize by asking what happened after sinning, or compound by adding sin upon sins out of confidence in atonement. In The Book of Hosea: Sin is named as the reason Israel has fallen. Israel is to ask the LORD to forgive all their sins, and the LORD responds by promising to heal their waywardness and turn away his anger. In The Gospel According to John, sin enslaves those who commit it, and Jesus commands the accused woman to sin no more while warning unbelievers that they will die in their sins. In Paul’s Letter to the Romans, sin is presented as a universal enslaving power that entered through one man, brings death, and is overcome in believers who have died to sin. In Paul’s Letter to the Romans, sin is described as a power dwelling in the narrator’s flesh that produces coveting, deceives, kills, and holds him captive in conflict with his mind. In The Letter to the Hebrews: Sins are not taken away by the blood of bulls and goats, and the law's sacrifices only remind of them each year.

In The Letter from James, lust conceives and bears sin, and full-grown sin produces death; knowing good but failing to do it is also sin. In 1 John, sin is identified as lawlessness and unrighteousness, whose confession brings forgiveness, while sin leading to death is distinguished from sin not leading to death. In Surah 2: The Cow (Al-Baqara), sin surrounds wrongdoers and is linked to mischief, transgression, disobedience, unjust dealings, disbelief, and preferring worldly life over the Hereafter. In Surah 4: The Women (An-Nisaa): Sin is committed only against oneself. The records warn believers against injustice and constraint in marriage, urge kindness, and present marriage to believing maids for one who fears sin, while saying patience is better. In Surah 5: The Table (Al-Maaida), sin is a cause of punishment, while Allah remits sins for covenant keepers and relents when people repent and amend their conduct. In Surah 6: The Cattle (Al-An'aam), sin causes destruction, punishment, and loss, while those who repent after doing evil in ignorance and then do right receive Allah’s forgiveness. In Surah 29: The Spider (Al-Ankaboot): Disbelievers cannot bear the sins of those they mislead, although they claim they will do so. In The First Book of Nephi, His Reign and Ministry, sin prevents unclean people from dwelling with God, while the Lamb of God takes away the sins of the world. In The Second Book of Nephi: is_opposed_by writing [harshly] Nephi's writing speaks harshly against sin according to the plainness of the truth. In The Second Book of Nephi, sin is defined by law and opposition, making people guilty, unclean, spiritually dead, and separated from the Lord until they turn to God. In The Book of Jacob, the Brother of Nephi, sin is the people’s wrongdoing, including pride, persecution, fornication, and lasciviousness, for which teachers bear responsibility if they fail to warn them. In The Book of Enos: Enos struggles before God for a remission of his sins, and his guilt is swept away after God forgives him. In The Book of Mosiah, sin is defined as transgression, rebellion, wickedness, and failure to repent or obey God, bringing spiritual and communal consequences while repentance and faith enable salvation. In The Book of Alma, the Son of Alma, sin is portrayed as transgression and rebellion that bring condemnation and destruction unless people repent, while Christ atones for and removes it. In The Book of Alma, the Son of Alma, sin requires law, brings remorse and justice’s claims, and cannot be escaped through restoration to happiness; the speaker urges repentance and no further sin. In The Third Book of Nephi, the Son of Nephi, Who Was the Son of Helaman, sin is what Jesus Christ redeems people from, and repentance, baptism, humility, and belief bring remission. In The Book of Moroni: Those who do away with the things Moroni teaches and die do so in their sins and cannot be saved in the kingdom of God. In Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra), Book I, sin is harmful action toward living beings or through others, arising from ignorance and attachment and causing rebirth, pain, and suffering. In Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra), sin is said to multiply through entertainments, food, company, drunken sensuality, unsuitable food, and harmful speech, which monks and nuns must avoid. In Sutrakritanga (Sûtrakritâṅga), Book I, sin is wicked conduct or approval of wrongdoing that causes suffering, further births, and punishment, and must be abandoned and annihilated through proper conduct.

Abstract written by a model

Sin is action in the world that arises when one acts toward living bodies, causes or permits others to act, and includes injury, taking what is not given, and dealing in goods obtained through such acts. Abstract written by a model

Sin is a wicked deed that causes misery, suffering, further births, and punishment in the hells. It may be committed through one’s own activity, commission, or approval of another’s deed; sinful thoughts reveal an impure, careless mind. Attachment contributes to sin, which includes killing or injuring beings, false speech, taking what is not freely given, sexual pleasure, owning property, and failing to practise control. A sinner may conceal or deny conduct when reprimanded, committing further sin for reputation. Bad deeds lead to corresponding severe punishments. The teachings require abandoning sin, especially killing, and call on a corrected monk to promise not to repeat it, distinguish sin, and annihilate it through proper conduct.

Bad deeds, including killing or injuring beings, taking what is not freely given, and failing to practise control, lead sinners to the hells. Their consequences are suffered through punishments corresponding to the deeds.

A monk should promise not to commit the same sin again when corrected. He should declare the distinction of sin and seek the annihilation of sin through proper conduct.
