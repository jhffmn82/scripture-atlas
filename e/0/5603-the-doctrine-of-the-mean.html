Abstract written by a model

The Doctrine of the Mean presents a teaching on the Path of duty: Heaven gives humanity its Nature, and living in accord with it requires unceasing self-watchfulness. Its central ideal is the Mean, expressed through Equilibrium before feelings arise and Harmony when they act in due degree; their perfection brings order and flourishing to heaven and earth. Chung-ni and the Master contrast the superior man, who holds to the Mean with caution, sincerity, humility, and perseverance, with the mean man, who lacks caution and seeks notoriety. The Master laments that perfect virtue and the path of the Mean are rare, difficult to understand, easily exceeded or missed, and hard to sustain. Shun and Hui exemplify wise, firm adherence to it, while Tsze-lu’s question leads to an account of strong yet gentle and unchanging energy. The work then brings the Way into ordinary life: reciprocity, careful speech, self-rectification, acceptance of Heaven’s appointments, proper conduct in every station, and harmonious family relations. It portrays spiritual beings as subtly present in sacrifice, and presents filial piety, especially through Shun, King Wan, King Chi, King Wu, and the duke of Chau, as carrying forward forefathers’ purposes through rule, ceremony, mourning, and ancestral offerings. Government depends on proper persons, rulerly character, universal duties and virtues, preparation, and sincerity. Sincerity and intelligence entail one another; complete sincerity develops one’s own nature and those of other people, animals, and things, assists Heaven and Earth, transforms others, foreknows omens, and becomes ceaseless, vast, and Heaven-like. The Sage and ideal ruler preserve ceremony, study and practice the Mean, establish standards only with proper virtue and sovereignty, and become enduring models for the kingdom. The final chapters elevate Chung-ni, the sage ruler, and the wholly sincere person as Heaven-like figures, before returning to the superior man’s hidden virtue: sincere self-cultivation without display can move people toward virtue and happy tranquility.

The Doctrine of the Mean / Chapter 1

The Doctrine of the Mean explains that Heaven confers what is called the Nature, and accordance with the Nature is called the Path of duty. It teaches that the Path of duty cannot be left and that the superior man remains watchful over himself, especially when alone. It describes Equilibrium as the state before pleasure, anger, sorrow, or joy arise, and Harmony as the state when those feelings act in their due degree. When Equilibrium and Harmony exist in perfection, a happy order prevails throughout heaven and earth, and all things are nourished and flourish.

Chapter 2

Chung-ni explains that the superior man embodies the course of the Mean, while the mean man acts contrary to it. The superior man maintains the Mean because he is a superior man. The mean man acts contrary to the Mean because he is a mean man and has no caution.

Chapter 3

The Master says that perfect virtue is according to the Mean. He observes that such virtue has been rare among the people for a long time. The unit concerns the Master’s judgment about Perfect and the virtue according to the Mean.

Chapter 4

The Master explains why the path of the Mean is not followed or understood. He says that knowledgeable people and men of talents and virtue go beyond it, while stupid and worthless people do not reach it. He adds that although everyone eats and drinks, few can distinguish flavors.

Chapter 5

The Master remarks on the path of the Mean. He says that the path is untrodden. The unit concerns the Master's lament about the neglected path of the Mean.

Chapter 6

The Master describes Shun as greatly wise. Shun loved questioning others and studying their words, even when they were shallow. He concealed what was bad in their words and displayed what was good. He grasped both extremes, determined the Mean, and employed it in the government of the people.

Chapter 7

The Master says that men claim to be wise. Yet when they are driven forward and caught in a net, trap, or pitfall, they do not know how to escape. He also says that when men choose the course of the Mean, they cannot keep it for a full month.

Chapter 8

The Master describes the manner of Hui. Hui chose the Mean and held firmly to what was good. He kept what was good as if wearing it on his breast and did not lose it.

Chapter 9

The Master states that a kingdom, its states, and its families may be perfectly ruled. He says that dignities and emoluments may be declined. He also says that naked weapons may be trampled under the feet. Despite these achievements, he teaches that the course of the Mean cannot be attained to.

Chapter 10

Tsze-lu asks the Master about energy. The Master distinguishes the energy of southern regions, northern regions, and the energy one should cultivate oneself. Southern energy consists of forbearance, gentleness, and not taking revenge, while northern energy consists of readiness for death without regret. The superior man cultivates friendly harmony without weakness and remains firm and unchanging whether good or bad principles prevail in government.

Chapter 11

The Master describes what he does not do and what he cannot do. He does not live in obscurity while practicing wonders to gain honor in future ages. The good man tries to follow the right path but abandons it halfway, while the Master says he cannot stop in that way. The superior man follows the course of the Mean without regret even when unknown and unregarded, and only the sage is capable of this.

Chapter 12

The way of the superior man reaches wide and far while remaining secret. Common men and women may understand and practice parts of it, though even the sage cannot fully know or carry it out. Its greatness cannot be fully embraced, and its minuteness cannot be divided. It appears above and below, in Heaven and earth and in the everyday intercourse of common men and women.

Chapter 13

The Master explains that the path remains close to ordinary human consciousness and conduct. He teaches that the superior man governs people according to their nature, corrects what is wrong, and practices reciprocity by not doing to others what he would dislike for himself. He describes four standards of service and friendship that he has not yet attained. The superior man is marked by earnest practice, careful speech, and entire sincerity between words and actions.

Chapter 14

The unit concerns the superior man and how he conducts himself in every station and circumstance. He does what is proper to his position, remains himself, and neither despises inferiors nor seeks favor from superiors. He rectifies himself, seeks nothing from others, and does not murmur against Heaven or grumble against men. He waits quietly for the appointments of Heaven, and when he fails, he looks within himself for the cause.

Chapter 15

The chapter compares the way of the superior man to traveling a distance by first crossing the nearby space and to ascending a height by beginning on lower ground. It describes happy union with wife and children as musical harmony and concord among brethren as delightful and enduring harmony. The passage says that such harmony allows a family to be regulated and brings pleasure to a wife and children. The Master says that in such a state, parents have entire complacence.

Chapter 16

The Master explains how spiritual beings display their powers even though people cannot see or hear them. They enter into all things and influence people to fast, purify themselves, and dress richly for sacrifices. During worship, they seem to surround the worshippers above, to the right, and to the left. The passage teaches that the spirits’ subtle presence and the outgoings of sincerity cannot be repressed.

Chapter 17

The Master praises Shun as greatly filial and possessing the virtue of a sage. Shun had the throne, riches within the four seas, fame, and long life, and his descendants preserved sacrifices to him. Heaven is said to reward beings according to their qualities, nourishing what flourishes and overthrowing what is ready to fall. The unit concludes that a person of great virtue will surely receive the appointment of Heaven.

Chapter 18

The unit concerns King Wan, King Chi, King Wu, and the duke of Chau in the establishment and transmission of royal dignity. King Wan is praised as having no cause for grief because his father was King Chi and his son was King Wu. King Wu continued the enterprise of King T'ai, King Chi, and King Wan, gained possession of the kingdom, and established royal succession and ancestral sacrifices. The duke of Chau completed the virtuous course of Wan and Wu and set rules for titles, sacrifices, burials, and mourning.

Chapter 19

The Master explains the far-reaching filial piety of King Wu and the duke of Chau. Filial piety consists in carrying out the wishes of forefathers and carrying forward their undertakings through ancestral ceremonies, offerings, music, and reverence. These ceremonies distinguish rank, descent, talents, worth, and age while serving the dead as if they were alive. Understanding the sacrifices to Heaven and Earth and the sacrifices to ancestors makes governing a kingdom as easy as looking into one’s palm.

Chapter 20

The Master explains that government depends on proper men, whose qualities are developed through the ruler’s character, duty, benevolence, propriety, and knowledge of Heaven. He sets out the five universal duties, the three universal virtues, and singleness as the means of practicing them. He describes nine standard rules for governing the kingdom with its states and families, and teaches that preparation and sincerity are essential. The superior man pursues study, inquiry, reflection, discrimination, and earnest practice until weakness becomes strength and dullness becomes intelligence.

Chapter 21

The chapter examines the relationship between intelligence and sincerity. It states that sincerity arising from intelligence is attributed to nature, while intelligence arising from sincerity is attributed to instruction. It further asserts that sincerity entails intelligence, and intelligence entails sincerity.

Chapter 22

The unit concerns the most complete sincerity and its power to develop nature. A person who fully develops their own nature can likewise develop the nature of other men, animals, and things. By fully developing the natures of creatures and things, the person can assist the transforming and nourishing powers of Heaven and Earth and form a ternion with Heaven and Earth.

Chapter 23

The unit concerns the person who cultivates the shoots of goodness in him to the utmost. Through this cultivation, he attains sincerity, which becomes apparent, manifest, and brilliant. His brilliance affects others, causing them to change and be transformed. Only the person possessed of the most complete sincerity can transform under heaven.

Chapter 24

The chapter concerns the power of most entire sincerity to foreknow events. It says that nations and families about to flourish have happy omens, while those about to perish have unlucky omens. Calamity or happiness can be foreknown, especially by the individual possessed of the most complete sincerity, who is like a spirit.

Chapter 25

The unit concerns sincerity, self-completion, the way, and the superior man. Sincerity effects self-completion and provides the way by which man must direct himself. It is both the end and beginning of things, and without it nothing would exist. The superior man regards attaining sincerity as the most excellent thing, while the entirely sincere man completes himself as well as other men and things.

Chapter 26

The chapter explains that entire sincerity includes ceaselessness, which continues, evidences itself, reaches far, and becomes large, substantial, high, and brilliant. An individual possessing entire sincerity becomes the co-equal of Earth and Heaven and is made infinite. The way of Heaven and Earth is without doubleness, producing things unfathomably and displaying vast, enduring qualities through Heaven, Earth, mountains, and water. The chapter concludes that singleness is what makes Heaven Heaven and King Wan what he was, and that singleness likewise is unceasing.

Chapter 27

The chapter concerns the path proper to the Sage and its complete, nourishing greatness. It embraces the rules of ceremony and demeanor, but it can be fully practiced only by the proper man and through perfect virtue. The superior man studies, preserves, expands, and practices it while pursuing the course of the Mean. His conduct remains humble in high position, obedient in low position, and prudent under both good and bad government.

Chapter 28

The Master warns that ignorance, lack of rank, or living in the present while reverting to antiquity will bring calamity. Only the Son of Heaven may order ceremonies, fix measures, and determine written characters. Across the kingdom, carriages, writing, and conduct follow common standards, while proper virtue and occupying the throne determine who may establish ceremonies and music. The Master describes the ceremonies of Hsia, notes the continuing ceremonies of Yin in Sung, and follows the ceremonies of Chau now in use.

Chapter 29

The Ruler who attains sovereignty of the kingdom, possessing three important things, can govern with few errors. His institutions must be rooted in his own character and conduct, attested by the people, consistent with the three kings, Heaven and Earth, spiritual beings, and capable of awaiting a sage a hundred ages later. His movements, acts, and words become examples, laws, and lessons for the kingdom across ages. The people near him are never weary of him, while those far away long for him, bringing him lasting renown.

Chapter 30

Chung-ni handed down the doctrines of Yao and Shun and modeled the regulations of Wan and Wu. He harmonized with the times of Heaven and conformed to the water and land. He may be compared to Heaven and Earth, the four seasons, and the sun and moon in their sustaining, alternating, and successive activities. The harmonious nourishment and movement of all things display the greatness of heaven and earth.

Chapter 31

The chapter describes the ideal ruler as a sage possessing comprehensive virtues and abilities suited to governing. He is magnanimous, energetic, steadfast, reverent, discerning, and capable of maintaining the Mean. His virtues spread throughout the Middle Kingdom and beyond, causing the people to revere, believe, please, honor, and love him. Because of his vastness and depth, he is called the equal of Heaven.

Chapter 32

The chapter concerns the individual possessed of the most entire sincerity. Such an individual can adjust the great invariable relations of mankind, establish the great fundamental virtues of humanity, and know the transforming and nurturing operations of Heaven and Earth. The individual is described as earnest as man in his ideal, deep as an abyss, and vast as Heaven, and only one with exceptional apprehension, discernment, intelligence, knowledge, and Heavenly virtue can know him.

Chapter 33

The unit concerns the superior man’s concealed and sincere virtue, contrasted with the mean man’s pursuit of notoriety and ruin. The superior man examines his heart, remains reverent and truthful, and acts without display, anger, or reliance on rewards. His unseen self-cultivation stimulates the people to virtue and leads the world toward happy tranquility. The unit concludes that perfect virtue, like the doings of supreme Heaven, has neither sound nor smell.
