commandments, statutes, and ordinances in Deuteronomy

author unknown · the work’s page · read it where commandments, statutes, and ordinances first appears

Abstract written by a model

The commandments, statutes, and ordinances are the body of law that Moses teaches Israel on the LORD's behalf. They are unalterable: nothing may be added or removed. Keeping them counts as righteousness before the LORD and as Israel's wisdom and understanding in the sight of the peoples, who will see that no other great nation has such righteous law. Their purpose is Israel's good and preservation. The account unfolds in stages. In Chapter 4 Moses teaches them and forbids adding or subtracting. In Chapter 5 he calls Israel to hear them, and the LORD has Moses stay beside him to receive all of them for teaching. Chapter 6 says they are given so Israel may do them in the land, fear the LORD, and live long and well. They are to be taught diligently, bound on hand and forehead, and written on door posts and gates. Chapters 7, 8, and 11 make obedience the condition for the covenant's loving kindness and for blessings: life, multiplying, possessing the land, rain, fruitfulness, and victory. Forgetting the LORD or turning to other gods brings perishing and drought. Chapter 28 sets blessings for keeping them against curses for failing, with fearing the LORD's name as the aim. Chapter 30 declares the commandment not too hard or distant but very near, in the mouth and heart, centered on loving the LORD and walking in his ways. Chapter 31 closes with Moses writing the law in a book, giving it to the priests and elders, and ordering it read to all Israel every seven years at the feast of booths. The Levites place it beside the ark as a witness against Israel.

Abstract written by a model

The commandments of God are commandments contained in the plates of brass, delivered through records, taught by Mosiah and Abinadi, and to be observed throughout life. They require obedience in all things, including rejecting graven images and avoiding sin such as killing, adultery, stealing, false witness, and coveting. Their central promise is blessedness, prosperity, protection, salvation, and a part in the first resurrection for those who keep them; transgression brings rebellion, separation from the Spirit of the Lord, perishing, and exclusion from that resurrection. King Benjamin’s people covenant to obey them for the rest of their days, and the church of God is exhorted to keep them with long-suffering and much travail. Abinadi refuses to deny them even unto death, while the rebellious knowingly refuse them. The account ends with Alma and his companions exhorting the church to keep them, and Mosiah teaching that kings should establish and obey them, though unrighteous kings may trample them.
