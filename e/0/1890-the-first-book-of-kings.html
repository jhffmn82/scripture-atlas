The First Book of Kings

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

This document is the book of 1 Kings, tracing Israel's monarchy from David's death through Solomon's glory and the kingdom's division to the reign of Ahab and the prophet Elijah's ministry. It opens with a succession struggle: the aged David, after Nathan and Bathsheba intervene against Adonijah's self-proclaimed kingship, has Solomon anointed. Solomon then secures his throne, eliminating Adonijah, Joab and Shimei and removing Abiathar. At Gibeon he asks the LORD for an understanding heart, receives wisdom, riches and honor, and shows it in the judgment between two women claiming one child. Chapters 4 to 10 portray his reign: administrators, provisioning officers, wide dominion and peace. With Hiram of Tyre's timber and forced labor he builds the temple over seven years, then his own house, and the ark is installed amid the LORD's glory, followed by Solomon's prayer and a fourteen-day dedication feast. The LORD warns him to stay faithful; the queen of Sheba visits and his wealth grows immense. Chapter 11 turns the story: his many foreign wives lead him to worship other gods, the LORD announces the kingdom will be torn away, adversaries rise, and Ahijah tells Jeroboam he will receive ten tribes. Solomon dies, succeeded by Rehoboam. In chapter 12 Rehoboam rejects the people's plea to lighten the yoke, Israel rebels and makes Jeroboam king, and only Judah with Benjamin stays loyal. Jeroboam sets up golden calves at Bethel and Dan. A man of God confronts him, but is deceived by an old prophet and killed by a lion (ch. 13). Ahijah pronounces doom on Jeroboam's house when his son dies (ch. 14). Chapters 14 to 16 survey rulers of Judah and Israel in turn: Rehoboam, Abijam, and the reforming Asa in Judah; Nadab, Baasha, Elah, Zimri, Omri and finally Ahab in Israel, with dynasties ended by murder. Ahab, married to Jezebel, serves Baal and does more evil than any predecessor. Chapters 17 to 19 center on Elijah: he announces drought, is fed by ravens and a widow, and revives her son. On Mount Carmel the LORD's fire defeats the prophets of Baal and rain returns. Fleeing Jezebel, Elijah meets the LORD at Horeb in a still small voice and is told to anoint Hazael, Jehu and Elisha. The last chapters follow Ahab: he defeats Ben Hadad of Syria but spares him and is rebuked (ch. 20); he and Jezebel have Naboth killed for his vineyard, and Elijah foretells their fate (ch. 21). Ahab dies at Ramoth Gilead despite disguise, as Micaiah predicted, and the book ends with Jehoshaphat's reign and Ahaziah's evil rule over Israel (ch. 22).

Chapter 1

King David is old and cannot keep warm, so his servants bring Abishag the Shunammite to attend him. His son Adonijah declares himself king and holds a feast with Joab and Abiathar the priest, omitting Nathan, Benaiah, the mighty men, and Solomon. Nathan and Bathsheba each go to David, reminding him of his oath that Solomon would reign, and David orders Solomon anointed at Gihon by Zadok and Nathan. The people celebrate Solomon's kingship, Adonijah's guests scatter on hearing the news, and Adonijah clings to the altar's horns in fear. Solomon promises Adonijah safety if he proves worthy, then sends him home.

Chapter 2

As David nears death he charges his son Solomon to keep the LORD's law and to deal with Joab and Shimei while rewarding the sons of Barzillai. David dies after reigning forty years, and Solomon's kingdom is established. Adonijah asks Bathsheba to request Abishag as his wife, and Solomon takes this as a threat and has Benaiah kill him. Solomon banishes Abiathar from the priesthood, has Benaiah kill Joab at the altar, and installs Benaiah over the army and Zadok as priest. Shimei is confined to Jerusalem, leaves for Gath to retrieve runaway slaves, and is put to death by Benaiah on Solomon's order.

Chapter 3

Solomon allies with Pharaoh of Egypt by marrying his daughter, and the people still sacrifice at high places while Solomon, who loves the LORD, does the same at Gibeon. There the LORD appears to him in a dream and invites him to ask for something; Solomon requests an understanding heart to judge God's people. The LORD is pleased, grants wisdom plus riches and honor, and promises long life if Solomon obeys. Back in Jerusalem, Solomon offers sacrifices and holds a feast, then judges between two prostitutes who both claim a living child, ordering it divided by sword and discerning the true mother by her compassion. All Israel hears of the judgment and fears the king, seeing God's wisdom in him.

Chapter 4

Chapter 4 lists King Solomon's princes and officials, including Azariah, Zadok, Abiathar, Benaiah, Ahishar, and Adoniram, who held posts over priests, scribes, the army, the household, and forced labor. It names the twelve officers who each provisioned the king and his household for one month a year across Israel, including Ben Hur, Baana, Ahimaaz, and Geber. The chapter describes Solomon's wide dominion from the River to the border of Egypt, the peace and prosperity of Judah and Israel, his large daily provision, and his forty thousand stalls of horses and twelve thousand horsemen. It closes by praising the wisdom God gave Solomon, which surpassed that of the east and Egypt, including his three thousand proverbs and one thousand five songs, and says people of all nations came to hear him.

Chapter 5

Hiram, king of Tyre, sends servants to Solomon after hearing he has been anointed king in place of David, whom Hiram had always loved. Solomon tells Hiram that David could not build a house for the LORD's name because of surrounding wars, but that the LORD has now given him rest, so he intends to build it and asks for cedar cut in Lebanon. Hiram rejoices, blesses the LORD for giving David a wise son, and agrees to supply cedar and cypress timber, floating it by sea in rafts, while Solomon provides yearly wheat and oil for Hiram's household. The two make a treaty, and Solomon raises a levy of thirty thousand men, working in monthly shifts in Lebanon under Adoniram, along with seventy thousand burden bearers, eighty thousand stone cutters, and three thousand three hundred chief officers. Solomon's builders, Hiram's builders, and the Gebalites cut the large, costly stones and prepare timber and stones for the house's foundation.

Chapter 6

Chapter 6 describes King Solomon building the LORD's house in the fourth year of his reign, 480 years after Israel left Egypt. The text gives the dimensions of the house, porch, and side rooms, and notes that the stone was prepared at the quarry so no iron tool was heard on site. The LORD speaks to Solomon, promising to establish his word and dwell among Israel if Solomon keeps his statutes and commandments. The interior is lined with carved cedar and overlaid with gold, and an inner sanctuary is prepared for the ark of the LORD's covenant, containing two ten-cubit olive-wood cherubim overlaid with gold. The foundation was laid in the month Ziv of the fourth year and the house was finished in the month Bul of the eleventh year, a span of seven years.

Chapter 7

Chapter 7 describes Solomon's thirteen-year construction of his own house, including the House of the Forest of Lebanon, the hall of pillars, the porch of judgment, and a house for Pharaoh's daughter, all built of costly cut stone and cedar. Solomon then brings Hiram, a bronze worker from Tyre and son of a Naphtali widow, who makes the two pillars Jachin and Boaz, the molten sea on twelve oxen, ten bronze bases with ten basins, and the pots, shovels, and basins. The bronze is cast in the Jordan plain between Succoth and Zarethan and left unweighed because there was so much of it. Solomon also makes the gold furnishings of the LORD's house, including the altar, the table, lamp stands, and hinges. He finishes the work and places the items David his father had dedicated in the treasuries.

Chapter 8

King Solomon gathers Israel's elders and people at Jerusalem in the month Ethanim to bring the ark of the LORD's covenant into the newly built house, where it is placed in the most holy place beneath the cherubim's wings. A cloud fills the house with the LORD's glory so that the priests cannot minister. Solomon blesses the assembly, recounts the LORD's promise to David that his son would build the house, and offers a long prayer asking the LORD to hear Israel, the foreigner, and exiles who pray toward the house, and to forgive them. He then blesses the people, urging them to be wholly committed to the LORD. Solomon and all Israel offer vast sacrifices and hold a fourteen-day feast dedicating the house, after which the people go home joyful.

Chapter 9

After Solomon finishes building the LORD's house and the king's house, the LORD appears to him a second time and promises to establish his throne forever if he walks in integrity, but warns that Israel and the temple will be cast out if he or his children serve other gods. After twenty years of building, Solomon gives Hiram king of Tyre twenty Galilean cities in return for cedar, cypress and gold, but Hiram is displeased and calls them the land of Cabul. Solomon conscripts forced labor to build the LORD's house, his own house, Millo, the wall of Jerusalem, Hazor, Megiddo, Gezer and other cities, using the surviving non-Israelite peoples as bondservants while Israelites serve as soldiers and officers. Pharaoh's daughter moves to her own house, and Solomon offers sacrifices three times a year. Solomon also builds a fleet at Ezion Geber, and Hiram's sailors help bring back 420 talents of gold from Ophir.

Chapter 10

The queen of Sheba, having heard of Solomon's fame, travels to Jerusalem with a great caravan of spices, gold, and precious stones to test him with hard questions. Overwhelmed by his wisdom, house, table, and servants, she praises Solomon and the LORD his God, gives him gold, spices, and gems, and returns home after receiving all she desired. The chapter then details Solomon's immense wealth: 666 talents of gold yearly, golden shields and bucklers, an ivory and gold throne guarded by lions, gold drinking vessels, and the Tarshish fleet bringing gold, silver, ivory, apes, and peacocks. Solomon exceeds all earth's kings in riches and wisdom, receives yearly tribute from those who seek him, and amasses chariots and horsemen, importing horses and chariots from Egypt and exporting them to the Hittite and Syrian kings.

Chapter 11

King Solomon loved many foreign women, including Pharaoh's daughter, and had seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines who turned his heart to other gods. He built high places for Chemosh and Molech and worshiped Ashtoreth and Milcom, so the LORD announced he would tear the kingdom from Solomon's son, leaving one tribe for David's sake. The LORD raised up adversaries against Solomon: Hadad the Edomite, who had fled to Egypt as a child, and Rezon the son of Eliada, who reigned in Damascus. The prophet Ahijah the Shilonite tore a new garment into twelve pieces and told Jeroboam he would receive ten tribes; Solomon sought to kill Jeroboam, who fled to Shishak in Egypt. Solomon reigned forty years, died, and was succeeded by his son Rehoboam.

Chapter 12

Rehoboam goes to Shechem to be made king, where Jeroboam and Israel ask him to lighten the heavy yoke Solomon imposed. Rehoboam rejects the old men's advice to serve the people and follows the young men's advice to threaten harsher treatment, which the text says the LORD brought about to fulfill his word spoken by Ahijah. Israel rebels, stones Adoram to death, and makes Jeroboam king, leaving only Judah (with Benjamin) loyal to Rehoboam. Rehoboam assembles 180,000 warriors, but Shemaiah relays the LORD's command not to fight, and they return home. Jeroboam fortifies Shechem and Penuel, then fearing the people will return to Rehoboam, makes two golden calves for Bethel and Dan, appoints non-Levite priests, and institutes his own feast in the eighth month.

Chapter 13

A man of God from Judah comes to Bethel by the LORD's word and prophesies against the altar where King Jeroboam is burning incense, foretelling that a son of David's house named Josiah will sacrifice the high-place priests on it. Jeroboam's outstretched hand withers when he orders the man seized, and the altar splits as the sign foretold; the man of God intercedes and the hand is restored, but he refuses the king's invitation to eat because the LORD commanded him not to eat, drink, or return by the same way. An old prophet of Bethel pursues him and, lying that an angel told him to, persuades him to eat at his house. The LORD then pronounces judgment through the old prophet, and a lion kills the man of God on the road, yet does not eat the body or harm the donkey. The old prophet buries him in his own grave, asks to be buried beside him, and affirms the prophecy, while Jeroboam persists in making high-place priests, which brings his house to ruin.

Chapter 14

Jeroboam's son Abijah falls sick, and Jeroboam sends his disguised wife to the blind prophet Ahijah at Shiloh with bread, cakes, and honey. The LORD warns Ahijah, who exposes her and delivers a judgment: Jeroboam's house will be utterly swept away for idolatry, a new king will cut it off, and Israel will be uprooted and scattered beyond the River. The child dies as she reaches the threshold at Tirzah, and all Israel mourns and buries him, as foretold. Jeroboam reigns twenty-two years and is succeeded by his son Nadab. The chapter then turns to Rehoboam of Judah, whose people practice idolatry and sodomy, whose treasures Shishak king of Egypt takes in his fifth year, and who dies after seventeen years, succeeded by his son Abijam.

Chapter 15

Chapter 15 covers the reigns of Abijam and Asa over Judah and of Nadab and Baasha over Israel. Abijam reigned three years, walking in his father's sins, though the LORD preserved his line for David's sake. Asa reigned forty-one years and did right: he removed the sodomites, idols, and his mother Maacah's Asherah image, but he left the high places. When Baasha of Israel built Ramah against Judah, Asa sent the temple and royal treasures to Ben Hadad of Syria, who attacked northern Israelite cities, so Baasha stopped and Asa used the Ramah materials to build Geba and Mizpah. In Israel, Baasha killed Nadab son of Jeroboam at Gibbethon, wiped out Jeroboam's house as the LORD had spoken through Ahijah, and reigned twenty-four years in Tirzah doing evil.

Chapter 16

The LORD's word comes through Jehu the son of Hanani against Baasha of Israel for walking in Jeroboam's way, and after Baasha dies his son Elah reigns in Tirzah. Elah is killed while drunk by his chariot captain Zimri, who wipes out Baasha's house, then reigns only seven days before the army makes Omri king, and Zimri burns the king's house over himself when Tirzah is besieged. Israel is briefly split between followers of Tibni and Omri until Omri prevails; he buys the hill of Samaria from Shemer and builds the city. Omri's son Ahab becomes king, marries Jezebel daughter of Ethbaal, serves Baal, builds an altar and Asherah, and does more evil than any earlier king. The chapter closes with Hiel the Bethelite rebuilding Jericho at the cost of his sons, according to the LORD's word by Joshua.

Chapter 17

Elijah the Tishbite, a settler of Gilead, tells Ahab that there will be no dew or rain except by his word. The LORD sends him to hide by the brook Cherith, where ravens feed him, until the brook dries up. The LORD then sends him to Zarephath, where a widow gathering sticks feeds him from her last meal and oil, which then do not run out. When the widow's son becomes sick and stops breathing, Elijah carries him to his room, stretches himself on him three times, and prays, and the child revives. The widow then declares that Elijah is a man of God and that the LORD's word in his mouth is truth.

Chapter 18

In the third year of famine, the LORD tells Elijah to show himself to Ahab so that rain may come. Elijah meets Ahab's household head Obadiah, who fears the LORD and had hidden one hundred prophets from Jezebel, and persuades him to tell Ahab that Elijah is there. Elijah rebukes Ahab for following the Baals and has all Israel and the prophets of Baal and the Asherah gathered at Mount Carmel, where he proposes a contest in which the God who answers by fire is God. The prophets of Baal call on him from morning until evening with no answer, while Elijah rebuilds the LORD's altar, soaks the offering with water, and prays, and the LORD's fire consumes everything. The people declare the LORD is God, Elijah has the prophets of Baal killed at the brook Kishon, and a great rain comes, after which Elijah runs ahead of Ahab's chariot to Jezreel.

Chapter 19

Ahab reports to Jezebel what Elijah has done, and she sends a messenger vowing to kill Elijah by the next day. Elijah flees to Beersheba in Judah, leaves his servant, and goes a day into the wilderness, where he sits under a juniper tree and asks to die. An angel twice feeds him, and he travels forty days and nights in that strength to Horeb, God's Mountain. There the LORD questions him in a cave and shows him a wind, an earthquake, and a fire, none containing the LORD, followed by a still small voice; Elijah twice gives the same complaint. The LORD sends him to anoint Hazael, Jehu, and Elisha, says seven thousand in Israel have not bowed to Baal, and Elijah then finds Elisha plowing, throws his mantle on him, and Elisha makes a farewell feast with his oxen and follows him.

Chapter 20

Ben Hadad, king of Syria, besieges Samaria with thirty-two allied kings and demands Ahab's silver, gold, wives, and children; Ahab yields at first but refuses the second demand on the elders' advice. A prophet promises victory, and the young men of the princes of the provinces lead Israel out and rout the Syrians while Ben Hadad is drinking. The next year Ben Hadad attacks at Aphek, believing the LORD is only a god of the hills, and Israel kills one hundred thousand Syrians in one day. Ahab spares Ben Hadad and makes a covenant with him, and a prophet who disguises himself announces that Ahab's life and people will take the place of Ben Hadad's because he let go the man devoted to destruction. Ahab goes home sullen and angry.

Chapter 21

Ahab king of Samaria asks Naboth the Jezreelite to sell or exchange his vineyard next to the palace in Jezreel, but Naboth refuses because it is the inheritance of his fathers. Ahab sulks, and his wife Jezebel writes letters in his name to the elders and nobles of Naboth's city, instructing them to stage a fast and have two wicked fellows falsely accuse Naboth of cursing God and the king. The elders and nobles comply, Naboth is stoned to death, and Ahab goes to take possession of the vineyard. The LORD sends Elijah the Tishbite to confront Ahab, announcing that dogs will lick his blood, that his house will be swept away like those of Jeroboam and Baasha, and that dogs will eat Jezebel. When Ahab tears his clothes and humbles himself, the LORD says he will delay the evil until the days of Ahab's son.

Chapter 22

After three years of peace between Syria and Israel, Ahab king of Israel persuades Jehoshaphat king of Judah to join him in attacking Ramoth Gilead. Ahab's four hundred prophets, led by Zedekiah the son of Chenaanah, promise victory, but Micaiah the son of Imlah reveals that the LORD has put a lying spirit in their mouths and that Ahab will fall; Ahab imprisons him. Ahab disguises himself in battle, yet a random arrow wounds him and he dies at evening, and his blood is licked up by dogs as the LORD had said. The chapter then summarizes Jehoshaphat's reign over Judah, including his righteousness, his failed ships, and his death, and closes with Ahaziah son of Ahab ruling Israel in evil, serving Baal.
