fool in Proverbs

author unknown · 969 to 699 BC · the work’s page · read it where fool first appears

Abstract written by a model

The fool, portrayed in Chapter 17, is someone who cannot benefit from wisdom: he lacks understanding, so money cannot buy it, and rebuke affects him less than lashes affect the wise. His eyes wander without focus, he is unfit for excellent speech, and he grieves his parents. Encountering him in his folly is worse than meeting a robbed bear, yet silence makes him seem wise.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

Chapter 17

The fool is shown as unable to profit from wisdom: money in his hand cannot buy it, a rebuke affects him less than a hundred lashes affect the wise, and his eyes wander to the ends of the earth. He brings grief and bitterness to his parents, and meeting him in his folly is called worse than meeting a bear robbed of her cubs. When he keeps silent he is counted wise.

fool (the foolish) in Ecclesiastes or, The Preacher

author unknown · about 299 to 199 BC · the work’s page · read it where fool (the foolish) first appears

Abstract written by a model

The fool is a recurring figure of folly who walks in darkness and does evil without knowing it. Early on, wisdom excels folly, yet the fool shares the wise man's fate of being forgotten, and the narrator fears a fool may succeed him. Later, the fool's many words resemble a dream with many cares, and God takes no pleasure in fools who delay vows. Finally, their hearts dwell in the house of mirth, anger rests in their bosom, and their song is worse than the wise man's rebuke.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Chapter 2

Folly is something the narrator considers and tries to lay hold of, and the fool is said to walk in darkness. Wisdom excels it, yet the fool shares the wise man's fate and is equally forgotten. The narrator also worries that his successor may be a fool.

Chapter 5

Fools offer sacrifices without knowing they do evil, and a fool's speech comes with a multitude of words like a dream with many cares. God has no pleasure in fools who defer paying their vows.

Chapter 7

The heart of fools is in the house of mirth, and their laughter is compared to the crackling of thorns under a pot. Anger rests in the bosom of fools, and their song is worse to hear than the rebuke of the wise.

author unknown · about 299 to 199 BC · the work’s page · read it where fool first appears

Abstract written by a model

The fool is a figure of folly described in Chapter 10: his heart sits at his left hand and his understanding fails as he walks, so he announces his own foolishness. His speech begins in foolishness and ends in mischievous madness; he multiplies words, is swallowed by his own lips, and is wearied by labor.

fool in The Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach, or Ecclesiasticus

author unknown · about 179 BC · the work’s page · read it where fool first appears

Abstract written by a model

The fool is a figure lacking understanding, portrayed as burdensome: heavier than lead, sand, salt, or iron, hard to teach, and wearying through madness. His life is called worse than death, and he is mourned all his days. Chapter 22 closes by advising others not to talk with him, to beware, and to turn away; his fearful heart cannot withstand fear.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

1 section; each heading opens the text

Chapter 22

The fool is described as one who is hard to teach, like glued potsherds or a man roused from deep sleep, and as heavier than lead, sand, salt, or iron. Mourning for him lasts all the days of his life, and the reader is told not to talk much with him, to beware of him, and to turn away to find rest. His fearful heart, shaped by his own imagination, cannot stand against any fear, like fences on a high place in the wind.
