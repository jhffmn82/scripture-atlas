Abstract written by a model

Control is a religious practice taught by Gñâtrika as part of the highest good and valued by saints. Right conduct makes it a discipline of self-control, purity, nonviolence, detachment, restraint in speech and action, and endurance of calamity. It includes Guptis and requires renouncing property, hostility, sexual intercourse, killing, stealing, false or treacherous speech, and other transgressions. Though difficult for those attached to relations, women, pleasures, or worldly life, a wise man and monk should practise it through hardship, pain, eating, and while awaiting decease. It calls for guarding oneself, subduing the senses, abandoning undertakings and attachments, and overcoming aversion and sensual delight. Practised with sound judgment and without injury to living beings, control guards from the Circle of Births, destroys the thorn of Karman, helps remove sin, and has led some to liberation and others to become gods.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

8 sections; each heading opens the text

Second Lecture, Called the Destruction of Karman. / First Chapter. .. Third Chapter.

Right conduct consists of self-control, purity, nonviolence, detachment, restraint from speech and action, and endurance of calamities. It is taught by Gñâtrika and exemplified by disciplined monks.

Third Lecture, Called the Knowledge of Troubles / First Chapter. .. Fourth Chapter.

Control is difficult for those attached to relations, women, pleasures, or worldly life. A monk should practise it, abandon undertakings and attachments, and remain restrained through hardship.

Fifth Lecture, Called Description of the Hells. / First Chapter. .. Second Chapter.

Self-control is presented as a practice that a wise man should maintain while awaiting decease. It accompanies renunciation and knowledge of the world and the Circle of Births.

Seventh Lecture, Called Description of the Wicked.

A monk should practise control by eating, resort to control when suffering pain, and use it to subdue the foe at the head of the battle. Control supports the monk's effort to get rid of sin.

Eighth Lecture, Called on Exertion.

Control consists in not killing, stealing, speaking false or treacherous words, desiring transgressions, or failing to guard oneself. A man who guards his self and subdues his senses abhors all sins.

Tenth Lecture, Called Carefulness.

Control is to be delighted in and combined with sound judgment. A monk should overcome aversion to control and remain free from sensual delight.

Twelfth Lecture, Called the Creed.

Pious men practise it by restraining themselves and avoiding injury to living beings. Through it they become guarded and may become exempt from the Circle of Births.

Fifteenth Lecture, Called the Yamakas.

Control requires renouncing hostility in thoughts, words, and deeds, subduing the senses, remaining firm, and abstaining from sexual intercourse. It destroys the thorn of Karman; practising it, some have been liberated and others have become gods.
