householder

person · 1 work · 18 quoted facts · 6 connections
Also called: A layman, a householder, another man, householder's, layman, other, the giver, the layman

Abstract written by a model

A householder is a person with an abode whose household may be approached by a monk or nun while begging. The householder gives or prepares food for mendicants and others, sometimes out of respect for a Nirgrantha or for a fellow-ascetic, but such offerings may be unacceptable when bought, stolen, forcibly taken, prepared through injury to living beings, altered for the mendicant, given with certain hands, or prepared over fire. For a mendicant’s sake, the householder may alter small or large doors, reposition beds, cut grass, spread a couch, and use stools, benches, ladders, or a handmill to fetch food. These acts may involve falling, breaking earthen vessels, injuring earth-, fire-, or wind-bodies, or processing liquor by pressing, straining, or filtering. The records therefore present the householder as a provider whose assistance can also create impurity, injury, sin, and reasons for a well-controlled Nirgrantha to avoid a festival with a feast.

Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra), Book II, translated by Hermann Jacobi / Second Book / First Part. / First Lecture, Call

A householder may provide food, but a monk or nun should not accept food known to have been bought, stolen, forcibly taken, or prepared through sinful actions toward living beings. They should also avoid entering or leaving always liberal and always open houses for the sake of food or drink.

Second Lesson.

The layman might alter doors, reposition beds, cut or clip grass, and spread a couch in an upâsraya for the sake of a mendicant who lacks a bed. Such actions are presented as a reason for a well-controlled Nirgrantha not to resolve to attend a festival preceded or followed by a feast.

Sixth Lesson.

The householder is the person whose abode the monk or nun approaches while begging. A householder may give food, but food can become impure or unacceptable if the householder alters it for the mendicant, gives it with certain kinds of hands, or prepares it over fire.

Seventh Lesson.

A layman may fetch food from elevated places, granaries, vaults, or earthenware, and may cool hot food by blowing or fanning it. He may also fetch or alter water, but these actions can make offerings unacceptable or cause injury and sin.
