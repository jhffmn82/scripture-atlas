Abstract written by a model

In Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra), food is governed by rules concerning purity, preparation, receipt, consumption, disclosure, and distribution. In Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra), food is assessed for purity and acceptability in offerings and is accepted, refused, distributed, eaten, or discarded according to the stated rules. In Asa ki War (Asa di Var), food is made holy through the addition of ghi to holy corn, water, fire, and salt, then becomes impure upon entering a sinful body.

food in Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra)

Hermann Jacobi · the work’s page · read it where food first appears

Abstract written by a model

Food is governed by rules of purity, acceptance, preparation, receipt, consumption, disclosure, and distribution. It is impure and unacceptable when given from a hand, pot, spoon, or plate treated with cold or hot water, but pure and acceptable when a similarly soiled hand gives it to a similarly soiled or unsoiled recipient. Food prepared over fire, or certain grains or salts ground or pounded for a mendicant, should not be accepted. It should also not be accepted when prepared for a mendicant or another without permission, or placed before a relation’s house. Monk and nun must consume received sweet-smelling or bad-smelling food, consult before rejecting excess, disclose food, avoid concealment, and distribute food collected for many after consultation.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

3 sections; each heading opens the text

Sixth Lesson.

Food is impure and unacceptable when given from a hand or utensil that has been washed, wiped, wetted, moistened, dusted, or soiled with various substances, subject to the stated exception for similarly soiled recipients and hands. Food prepared over fire, and certain grains or salts ground or pounded for the mendicant, should not be accepted.

Ninth Lesson.

Food should not be accepted when it is âdhâkarmika, prepared for a mendicant or for another without the required permission, or placed before a relation’s house. A monk or nun should consume all received food, whether sweet-smelling or bad-smelling, and should consult fellow-ascetics before rejecting excess.

Tenth Lesson.

Food collected for many must be distributed only after consultation with an appropriate religious superior. A mendicant must disclose the food received, must not conceal agreeable food, and must not eat the good portion while bringing discoloured or tasteless food.

food in Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra)

Hermann Jacobi · the work’s page · read it where food first appears

Abstract written by a model

Food is assessed for purity and acceptability in offerings to monks or nuns. It is impure and unacceptable if affected or mixed with living beings, mildew, seeds or sprouts, water, dust, or other listed impurities; it is also unacceptable when fetched from above or placed on specified supports or bodies, including vegetable or animal matter. Herbs, grains, and flour may be accepted after their source of life is absent or they have been worked more than once. Food prepared by a giver for large gatherings, festivals, or communal entertainments is generally impure, while food prepared by another person may be acceptable and pure under the stated conditions. Food from Kshatriyas, kings, messengers, or relations of kings is impure and unacceptable. A monk or nun should refuse food if its status causes doubt. Food given for Sramanas or beggars must be silently accepted and impartially divided or eaten equally, without choosing desirable portions. Food may be discarded, distributed, eaten, deferred, or already removed; food cooled by a layman blowing or fanning for the mendicant should not be accepted.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

5 sections; each heading opens the text

Akaranga Sutra (Âkârâṅga Sûtra), Book II, translated by Hermann Jacobi / Second Book / First Part. / First Lecture, Call

Food affected by living beings, mildew, seeds, sprouts, water, dust, or other listed impurities is impure and unacceptable. Herbs, grains, and flour may be accepted when they no longer contain their source of life or have been worked more than once.

Second Lesson.

Food and related offerings prepared for large gatherings, festivals, or communal occasions are generally impure and unacceptable when prepared by the giver. They may be accepted when prepared by another person, or when offered after the assembled people have received their due share, and they are also acceptable from unblamed families.

Third Lesson.

A monk or nun on a begging-tour should not accept food about whose acceptability or unacceptability the mind has doubts or misgivings. Food from the houses of Kshatriyas, kings, messengers, and relations of kings is impure and unacceptable.

Fifth Lesson.

Food may be thrown away, thrown down, taken away, distributed, eaten, put off, or already eaten or removed. Food given for all must be silently accepted and then impartially divided or eaten equally, without selecting desirable portions.

Seventh Lesson.

Food placed on posts, pillars, beams, scaffolds, lofts, platforms, roofs, earthenware, earth-body, wind-body, fire-body, vegetable matter, or animal matter should not be accepted. Food cooled by blowing or fanning is also unacceptable if the layman performs that action for the mendicant.
