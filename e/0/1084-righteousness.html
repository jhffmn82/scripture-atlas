righteousness

topic · 12 works · 21 quoted facts · 25 connections
Also called: Righteousness, The work of righteousness, properly, right, right principle, righteous, the determinations of righteousness, the effect of righteousness, your righteousness

Abstract written by a model

In Psalms, righteousness is personified as kissing peace, looking down from heaven, and going before the LORD. In The Book of Isaiah, righteousness is the foundation of the king’s reign, whose work is peace and whose effect is lasting quietness and confidence. In The Gospel According to Matthew, righteousness is desired and rewarded, brings blessing amid persecution, and must exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees for entry into the Kingdom of Heaven. In The Analects of Confucius, Book XIII: Tsze-Lu, a superior’s love of righteousness makes the people submit to his example. In The Analects of Confucius, Book XV (Wei Ling Kung), righteousness is essential to the superior man in everything, though it is not the subject of the people’s conversation. In The Analects of Confucius, Book XVI (Ke She), righteousness is practiced to carry out principles. In The Analects of Confucius, Book XVII (Yang Ho), righteousness is held by the superior man as most important, while valour without it leads to insubordination or robbery. In The Analects of Confucius, Book XIX: Tsze-Chang, the scholar trained for public duty thinks of righteousness when confronted with an opportunity for gain.

and 5 more works

In Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou), righteousness makes a prince’s acts righteous and bears its richest fruit in obeying elder brothers. In Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou), righteousness is man’s straight path and a principle requiring superiors to observe laws, officers to serve their prince, and people to pursue difficult achievements and oppose perversity. In Mencius, Book V (Wan Zhang), righteousness is the inviolable way governing conduct, gifts, offices, personal purity, and proper reception of benefits. In Mencius, Book VI (Gaozi), righteousness arises from shame and dislike, guides conduct, belongs to Heaven’s nobility, and is preferred to life when preservation conflicts with it. In Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin), righteousness is a natural quality of the superior man and the path of life, expressed through sincere conduct, respect, and rejection of wrongful gain.

righteousness in Mencius, Book IV (Li Lou)

Mencius · about 299 BC · the work’s page · read it where righteousness first appears

Abstract written by a model

Righteousness is man’s straight path and a principle for superior and great men. It requires superiors to observe laws and officers to serve their prince, urging difficult achievements, setting good before the sovereign, and repressing perversities. It is followed by Shun; those who reject it, deny it, or claim they cannot pursue it depart from the proper path.

righteousness in Mencius, Book V (Wan Zhang)

Mencius · about 300 BC · the work’s page · read it where righteousness first appears

Abstract written by a model

Righteousness is a way and inviolable standard governing conduct. It requires adherence to principles prescribed by Yao and Shun, preserving personal purity, and refusing offices, gifts, lodging, or pay obtained contrary to it. It guides judgments about whether gifts and offices are properly received or held, while distinguishing refined judgment from simply calling all wrongful takers robbers. It unfolds through Yi Yin and Confucius, then concludes that righteousness is the way, with propriety as its door.

Righteousness requires adherence to the principles prescribed by Yao and Shun. Yi Yin would not accept office or gifts contrary to righteousness, and his different course from other sages was judged by the shared aim of keeping his person pure.

righteousness in Mencius, Book VII (Jin Xin)

Mencius · about 399 to 200 BC · the work’s page · read it where righteousness first appears

Abstract written by a model

Righteousness is a natural quality of the superior man, rooted in the heart and expressed throughout the body. It opposes taking what one has no right to and is presented as the path of life. A poor scholar must not abandon it, since it supports perfect satisfaction, and it works through respect for elders. It begins in natural feelings that refuse wrongdoing, then unfolds by extending those feelings into sincere speech and action, proper conduct, and rejection of gain by guile. Declining a dish of rice, a plate of soup, or the kingdom of Qi may show righteousness, though these are small forms beside other moral obligations.
