Abstract written by a model

This document is the book of Baruch together with the Letter of Jeremiah, a set of exilic writings addressed to Jews taken captive in Babylon. In Chapter 1, Baruch, son of Nerias, writes a book in Babylon in the fifth year after the Chaldeans burned Jerusalem and reads it to King Jechonias and the exiles by the river Sud. The people weep, fast, pray, and send money to Joakim the high priest and the priests in Jerusalem for offerings and for prayers for Nabuchodonosor and his son Baltasar. They ask that the book be read in the Lord's house as a confession that righteousness belongs to the Lord and shame to Judah, because the people, kings, priests, and prophets disobeyed him from the exodus onward. Chapter 2 continues the confession: the Lord has fulfilled his threats, including the horrors written in Moses' law. The people ask for mercy for his own sake, not their fathers' righteousness, and recall that refusing to serve Babylon brought desolation. They recall Moses' word that the stiff-necked people would be scattered but, repenting in captivity, would be restored to the land promised to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob under an everlasting covenant. Chapter 3 shifts from prayer to a meditation on wisdom. Israel forsook wisdom's fountain; rulers, wealthy men, giants, and foreign peoples never found her way, and only God, who knows all things, found her. He gave her to Jacob and Israel, and she appeared on earth and lived with men. Chapter 4 identifies wisdom with the book of God's commandments, the enduring law that gives life to those who keep it. Personified Jerusalem, a desolate widow, says her children were handed to a foreign nation for sacrificing to demons, then urges them to take courage and trust God's salvation. She promises doom for their oppressors and sees her sons returning. Chapter 5 turns to joyful consolation: Jerusalem is told to put off mourning and put on God's glory and righteousness, to be named 'Righteous Peace, Godly Glory,' and to see her children gathered from west to east, carried on a royal throne, with mountains made low and trees giving shade as God leads Israel in mercy and righteousness. The final piece, the Letter of Jeremiah, is sent to those about to be taken captive to Babylon. Jeremy says the captivity is punishment for sin, lasting seven generations, after which God will bring them out peacefully. He warns them not to fear or copy the silver, gold, and wooden idols they will see, mocking them at length as powerless and exploited by their priests, contrasts them with the sun, moon, stars, and elements that obey God, and concludes that the just man without idols is better.

Baruch / Chapter 1

Baruch, son of Nerias, wrote a book in Babylon in the fifth year, at the time the Chaldeans took and burned Jerusalem, and read it aloud to Jechonias king of Judah and to the assembled exiles by the river Sud. The people wept, fasted, prayed, and collected money according to each one's ability, sending it to Joakim the high priest and the priests in Jerusalem. The money was to buy burnt offerings, sin offerings, and incense, and the exiles asked for prayers for the lives of Nabuchodonosor king of Babylon and his son Baltasar, and for themselves. They instructed that the book be read in the house of the Lord on feast days and assembly days as a confession that righteousness belongs to the Lord and confusion of face to Judah. The confession recounts that the people, kings, princes, priests, prophets, and fathers disobeyed the Lord from the exodus from Egypt onward, ignored the prophets, and so suffered plagues and the curse declared through Moses.

Chapter 2

In this chapter the speaker, praying on behalf of the people, confesses that the Lord has fulfilled the word he pronounced against Israel, its judges, kings, princes, and the men of Israel and Judah, bringing great plagues including cannibalism of sons and daughters, as written in the law of Moses. The people admit they sinned by not listening to the Lord's voice, and they ask him to turn away his wrath, hear their prayer, and deliver them for his own sake, appealing not to the righteousness of their fathers and kings but to his mercy. They recall the prophets' command to serve the king of Babylon and remain in the land, and acknowledge that refusing led to desolation, the silencing of the voices of mirth and the bridegroom and bride, and the casting out of the bones of their kings and fathers. The chapter closes by recalling the Lord's word through Moses that the stiff-necked people would be reduced to a small number among the nations, yet in captivity would take it to heart, repent, and be brought back to the land promised to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob under an everlasting covenant.

Chapter 3

In Chapter 3, the afflicted people of Israel cry out to the Lord Almighty, the God of Israel, confessing that they and their fathers have sinned and asking for mercy rather than remembered iniquity. They lament that they are in captivity among enemies, a reproach and a curse, because they forsook the fountain of wisdom. Israel is urged to learn where wisdom, strength, understanding, life and peace are found. The text says the rulers, silver-hoarders, giants, and foreign peoples such as the sons of Agar and the merchants of Merran and Teman never found wisdom's way and perished in their foolishness. Only God, who knows all things, found wisdom; he gave it to Jacob and Israel, and afterward she appeared on earth and lived with men.

Chapter 4

Chapter 4 opens by declaring the book of God's commandments to be the enduring law, giving life to those who hold it and death to those who leave it. It urges Jacob and Israel to take hold of it and not give their glory to another. Personified Jerusalem, speaking as a desolate widow, explains that her children were delivered to a shameless, foreign-tongued nation because they provoked God with sacrifices to demons and abandoned his law. She then urges the children to take courage, cry to God, and seek him ten times more, trusting that he will bring them salvation and joy. The chapter ends by promising doom for the cities that afflicted and enslaved Israel's children, and by calling Jerusalem to look east and see her sons returning, gathered from east to west at the word of the Holy One.

Chapter 5

Chapter 5 addresses Jerusalem, telling her to remove her mourning and affliction and put on the beauty of God's glory, the robe of righteousness, and a diadem. God will show her splendor everywhere under heaven and will call her by the name "Righteous Peace, Godly Glory". Jerusalem is told to stand on the height and see her children gathered from west to east at the word of the Holy One, rejoicing that God remembered them. Whereas they left on foot as captives of their enemies, God now brings them back carried on high on a royal throne. God has ordered mountains and hills made low and valleys filled so Israel may walk safely, and the woods and sweet smelling trees shade Israel, as God leads Israel with joy in the light of his glory, with mercy and righteousness.

The Letter of Jeremiah

The Letter of Jeremiah is a letter Jeremy sent to those about to be led captive to Babylon by Nabuchodonosor, king of the Babylonians, as God commanded. He tells them the captivity is punishment for their sins, that it will last seven generations, and that God will afterward bring them out peacefully. He warns them not to fear or imitate the gods of silver, gold, and wood they will see carried on shoulders in Babylon. He mocks these idols at length: they cannot speak, see, save themselves from rust, thieves, war, or fire, or help the weak, while their priests steal from them and sell their offerings. He contrasts them with the sun, moon, stars, lightning, wind, clouds, and fire, which obey God, and concludes that the just man without idols is better because he will be far from reproach.
