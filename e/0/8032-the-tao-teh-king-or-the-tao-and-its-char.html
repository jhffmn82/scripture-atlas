The Tao Teh King, or The Tao and Its Characteristics

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

This document presents the Tao as the nameless, enduring origin and mother of heaven, earth, and all things: unseen, unfathomable, empty yet useful, and acting without possessiveness or striving. It repeatedly contrasts named existence with the deeper, unnameable source, and teaches that mystery, vacancy, stillness, humility, softness, and simplicity reveal or accord with the Tao. Its central human figure is the sage, whose conduct mirrors the Tao. The sage governs without purposeful action, speaks little, claims neither ownership nor reward, avoids display and self-assertion, and completes work so unobtrusively that people believe they acted of themselves. The text favors water, valleys, infancy, emptiness, and low positions as images of excellence: the soft and weak overcome the hard and strong; the low receive what flows toward them; the unfilled space makes things useful. The work moves from cosmology and personal cultivation into government, social decline, conflict, and communal life. It warns that desire, sensory excess, learning used for cleverness, wealth, fame, harsh laws, taxation, ambitious rulers, weapons, and forceful attempts to seize or hold a kingdom create disorder, poverty, theft, war, and decline. Benevolence, righteousness, propriety, filial conduct, and loyal ministers are often portrayed as later responses to the loss of the Great Tao rather than its original condition. For rulers, the text advocates restraint, moderation, economy, gentleness, humility, and non-contention. A state is best governed by allowing people to become simple, settled, and self-correcting; a great state should abase itself like a low stream. War is an evil necessity, and victory should never be celebrated. The text urges beginning with small things, preventing disorder before it appears, withdrawing after achievement, knowing oneself, recognizing ignorance, contentment, and knowing when to stop. It concludes with ideals of reconciliation, a small self-sufficient state, sincere rather than fine speech, generosity without accumulation, and a sage who gives without striving. Throughout, the Tao’s way is presented as impartial, inexhaustible, paradoxical, and enduring.

Ch. 1. 1. The Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and

The unit distinguishes the enduring Tao and enduring name from forms that can be trodden or named. The Tao is described as the Originator of heaven and earth when conceived without a name and as the Mother of all things when conceived with a name. It teaches that freedom from desire reveals the deep mystery, while desire reveals only the outer fringe, and that the two aspects together are called the Mystery, the gate of all that is subtle and wonderful.

2. 1. All in the world know the beauty of the beautiful, and in doing

The unit explains how opposites and contrasts give rise to one another, including existence and non-existence, difficulty and ease, and length and shortness. It states that the sage manages affairs without doing anything and conveys instructions without speech. All things arise, grow, undergo their processes, and complete their work without claiming ownership or expecting reward. The work is done invisibly, and this is why the power does not cease.

3. 1. Not to value and employ men of superior ability is the way to

The unit concerns how the sage should govern the people. It says that not valuing and employing men of superior ability prevents rivalry, while not displaying desirable or difficult-to-procure things prevents theft and disorder. The sage empties the people's minds, fills their bellies, weakens their wills, and strengthens their bones. He keeps them without knowledge and desires, restrains those with knowledge from acting on it, and thereby establishes universal good order.

4. 1. The Tao is (like) the emptiness of a vessel; and in our

The unit describes the Tao as like the emptiness of a vessel, warning people to guard against fullness when employing it. It presents the Tao as deep, unfathomable, pure, and still, possibly the Honoured Ancestor of all things. It advises people to blunt sharp points, unravel complications, temper brightness, and agree with others’ obscurity. The Tao’s origin is unknown, though it may have existed before God.

5. 1. Heaven and earth do not act from (the impulse of) any wish to be

Heaven and earth do not act from a wish to be benevolent and deal with all things as dogs of grass are dealt with. The sages likewise do not act from a wish to be benevolent and deal with the people as dogs of grass are dealt with. The space between heaven and earth is compared to a bellows that is emptied without losing its power and produces more air when moved again. Much speech leads to swift exhaustion, so the inner being should be guarded and kept free.

6.

The unit concerns the valley spirit and the female mystery. It says that the valley spirit does not die and remains unchanged. The female mystery is identified with the gate from which beings first issued forth, called the root from which heaven and earth grew. Its power remains long and unbroken when used gently and without pain.

7. 1. Heaven is long-enduring and earth continues long. The reason

The unit concerns Heaven, earth, and the sage. Heaven and earth endure for a long time because they do not live for themselves. The sage places his own person last and treats it as foreign, yet his person is preserved and he attains his private ends. The text presents the lack of personal and private ends as the reason such ends are realised.

8. 1. The highest excellence is like (that of) water. The excellence

The unit describes the highest excellence through the example of water, which benefits all things and occupies the low place without striving. It also identifies excellence in residence, mind, associations, government, affairs, and movement according to suitability, stillness, virtue, good order, ability, and timeliness. The highest excellence does not wrangle about a low position, so no one finds fault with it.

9. 1. It is better to leave a vessel unfilled, than to attempt to

The unit concerns the proper limits of use, possession, achievement, and recognition. It says that an unfilled vessel is better than one carried when full, and that repeatedly feeling a sharpened point causes it to lose its sharpness. Gold, jade, wealth, and honours cannot be kept safely when they fill a hall or produce arrogance. After work is completed and a name becomes distinguished, withdrawing into obscurity is presented as the way of Heaven.

10. 1. When the intelligent and animal souls are held together in one

The unit concerns the union of the intelligent and animal souls, disciplined attention to the vital breath, and the cultivation of a flawless state. It discusses loving the people and ruling the state without purposeful action, while intelligence may appear as without knowledge. It describes The Tao as producing and nourishing all things without claiming, boasting, or controlling them, calling this The mysterious Quality.

11. The thirty spokes unite in the one nave; but it is on the empty

The passage concerns the wheel, vessels, and an apartment, explaining how each depends on empty space for its use. Thirty spokes unite in the wheel’s one nave, while the empty space for the axle makes the wheel useful. Clay vessels and an apartment formed by doors and windows likewise depend on their empty hollowness or interior space, so what exists serves adaptation and what is absent serves usefulness.

12.

The passage describes how sensory pleasures can overwhelm perception and alter human conduct. Colors, musical notes, flavors, chariot racing, hunting, and rare or strange objects are presented as distractions that deprive the senses and make the mind mad. It teaches that the sage satisfies the craving of the belly rather than the insatiable longing of the eyes. The sage puts aside the latter desire and prefers the former.

13. 1. Favour and disgrace would seem equally to be feared; honour and

The unit concerns favour, disgrace, honour, great calamity, the body, and the administration of the kingdom. Favour and disgrace are equally feared because gaining favour creates apprehension of losing it, while losing it leads to fear of greater calamity. Honour and great calamity are personal conditions because the body makes a person liable to great calamity. An administrator who honours the kingdom as his own person and loves it as his own person may be entrusted with governing it.

14. 1. We look at it, and we do not see it, and we name it 'the

The text describes the Tao through three qualities: it is not seen, not heard, and not grasped. These qualities cannot be described separately, so they are blended into The One. The Tao is neither bright nor obscure, acts ceaselessly, cannot be named, and returns to nothing. It is encountered without seeing its front or back, and knowing the Tao of old helps direct the present and unravel its clue.

15. 1. The skilful masters (of the Tao) in old times, with a subtle

The unit concerns the skilful masters (of the Tao) in old times and their subtle comprehension of the Tao's mysteries. They appeared cautious, irresolute, grave, evanescent, unpretentious, vacant, and dull. It teaches that muddy water becomes clear through stillness, while the condition of rest arises when movement continues. Those who preserve this method of the Tao do not wish to be full of themselves, and therefore can seem worn rather than new and complete.

16. 1. The (state of) vacancy should be brought to the utmost degree,

The unit concerns the cultivation of the state of vacancy and stillness, and the return of things to their root after their activity. It explains that this return is a fulfillment whose report is the unchanging rule. Knowing that rule produces capacity, forbearance, community of feeling, kingliness of character, likeness to heaven, and possession of the Tao. Possession of the Tao enables long endurance and freedom from bodily decay.

17. 1. In the highest antiquity, (the people) did not know that there

The unit concerns the changing relationship between the people and their rulers across successive ages. The people first did not know their rulers, then loved and praised them, then feared them, and finally despised them. It says that when faith in the Tao was deficient in the rulers, a want of faith in them followed among the people. The earliest rulers acted with reticence, completed their work successfully, and left the people saying that they were as they were of themselves.

18. 1. When the Great Tao (Way or Method) ceased to be observed,

The unit describes what happened when the Great Tao (Way or Method) ceased to be observed. Benevolence and righteousness came into vogue, followed by wisdom and shrewdness, and great hypocrisy ensued. When harmony no longer prevailed throughout the six kinships, filial sons appeared; when the states and clans fell into disorder, loyal ministers appeared.

19. 1. If we could renounce our sageness and discard our wisdom, it

The unit concerns three proposals for governing people: renouncing sageness and wisdom, renouncing benevolence and righteousness, and renouncing artful contrivances and scheming for gain. It says that renouncing sageness and wisdom would benefit the people, while renouncing benevolence and righteousness would restore filial and kindly behavior. It also says that abandoning artful contrivances and scheming for gain would eliminate thieves and robbers. The three methods are associated with olden ways that failed in elegance, while simple and plain courses would avoid selfish ends and many lusts.

20.

The unit contrasts renouncing learning with the apparent troubles and distinctions that arise among people. It presents the speaker as unlike the multitude of men, who seem satisfied, capable, and well supplied, while the speaker appears listless, confused, homeless, and lacking a sphere of action. The speaker nevertheless values the nursing-mother, the Tao. The text also says that what all men fear is feared, and that questions asking to be discussed have a wide and endless range.

21.

The unit concerns Tao as the source of the grandest forms of active force. Tao eludes sight and touch, yet contains the forms, semblances, and essences of all things. Its essences enfold the truth of what will later be seen, and Tao's name passes not away. The text attributes the enduring beauty and lack of decay in existing things to the nature of the Tao.

22. 1. The partial becomes complete; the crooked, straight; the empty,

The unit explains that what is partial, crooked, empty, or worn out can become complete, straight, full, or new. It concerns a person with few or many desires and the sage, who embraces humility and avoids self-display, self-assertion, self-boasting, and self-complacency. The sage thereby gains recognition and superiority, and no one in the world can strive with him. It teaches that the saying about the partial becoming complete comprehends all real completion.

23. 1. Abstaining from speech marks him who is obeying the spontaneity

The unit concerns abstaining from speech, the spontaneity of nature, and the Tao. It says that Heaven and Earth cannot sustain violent or sudden actions for long, and therefore man cannot either. A person making the Tao his business agrees with people according to whether they pursue the Tao, pursue its manifestation, or fail in both, and such agreement brings happiness of attaining to the Tao; insufficient faith causes a lack of faith in him.

24. He who stands on his tiptoes does not stand firm; he who stretches

The unit concerns a person who displays himself, asserts his own views, vaunts himself, or is self-conceited. It says that such a person does not shine, is not distinguished, does not have his merit acknowledged, and is not allowed superiority. It compares these conditions, from the standpoint of the Tao, to remnants of food or a tumour on the body, which all dislike. Those who pursue the course of the Tao therefore do not adopt or allow them.

25. 1. There was something undefined and complete, coming into

The passage describes something undefined and complete that existed before Heaven and Earth and is regarded as the Mother of all things. It names this thing the Tao and calls it The Great, saying that it passes on, becomes remote, and returns. Tao, Heaven, Earth, and the sage king are identified as the four great things in the universe. Man follows the law of the Earth, the Earth follows Heaven, Heaven follows the Tao, and the Tao follows its own nature.

26. 1. Gravity is the root of lightness; stillness, the ruler of

The passage teaches that gravity is the root of lightness and stillness rules movement. A wise prince traveling all day stays near his baggage waggons and remains indifferent to brilliant prospects. The lord of a myriad chariots must not act lightly before the kingdom, because doing so loses his root of gravity, while active movement causes him to lose his throne.

27. 1. The skilful traveller leaves no traces of his wheels or

The unit describes the skilful traveller, speaker, reckoner, closer, and binder as acting without leaving traces, faults, tallies, bolts, bars, strings, or knots. It says that the sage skilfully saves every man and thing, casting none away, which is called “Hiding the light of his procedure.” It presents the man of skill as a master to the person without skill, and the person without skill as helper of the man of skill’s reputation; this relationship is called “The utmost degree of mystery.”

28.

The unit describes three ways of preserving constant excellence: knowing strength while maintaining feebleness, knowing whiteness while remaining in black's shade, and knowing glory while accepting disgrace. Each way leads to simplicity, humility, or the state of the simple infant, with return to man's first state. It then states that the unwrought material, when divided, forms vessels, and that the sage, when employed, becomes the Head of all the Officers and governs without violent measures.

29. 1. If any one should wish to get the kingdom for himself, and to

The unit concerns a person who wishes to gain the kingdom through active deeds. It teaches that the kingdom is spirit-like, cannot be obtained by forceful action, and is destroyed by trying to win it or lost by trying to hold it. It describes the course and nature of things as reversing what was ahead and turning strength into weakness, after which the sage rejects excessive effort, extravagance, and easy indulgence.

30. 1. He who would assist a lord of men in harmony with the Tao will

The unit concerns He who would assist a lord of men in harmony with the Tao and how he should act in the kingdom. He should not assert mastery by force of arms, because such action brings a proper return, and wherever a host is stationed, briars and thorns spring up. A skilful commander strikes a decisive blow and stops, guarding against vanity, boastfulness, arrogance, and the desire for mastery. Things that reach strong maturity become old, and what is not in accordance with the Tao soon comes to an end.

31. 1. Now arms, however beautiful, are instruments of evil omen,

The unit concerns arms, those who have the Tao, and the superior man’s attitude toward war and victory. Arms are described as instruments of evil omen that the superior man uses only when necessity compels him. Victory by force is undesirable because delighting in the slaughter of men prevents a person from getting his will in the kingdom. The unit also explains how left and right positions relate to festivity, mourning, military command, and rites of mourning.

32. 1. The Tao, considered as unchanging, has no name.

The unit concerns the Tao and its relation to the whole world. The Tao, in its unchanging primordial simplicity, has no name, but when it acts it acquires a name that allows people to know where to rest and avoid failure and error. If a feudal prince or king could guard and hold it, all would spontaneously submit to him. Heaven and Earth, under its guidance, unite and send down sweet dew without human direction, and the Tao relates to the world as great rivers and seas relate to valley streams.

33. 1. He who knows other men is discerning; he who knows himself is

The unit contrasts knowledge of other men with knowledge of oneself, calling the former discernment and the latter intelligence. It says that overcoming others is strength, while overcoming oneself is might. It describes satisfaction with one's lot as wealth and continued energetic action as evidence of a firm will. It adds that fulfilling the requirements of one's position leads to continuance, while dying without perishing constitutes longevity.

34. 1. All-pervading is the Great Tao! It may be found on the left

The unit concerns the Great Tao, all things, and the sage. The Great Tao is everywhere, produces all things without claiming authority, and presides over their return to their root. All things depend on it, obey it, and do not know that it governs their actions. The sage accomplishes great achievements by not making himself great.

35. 1. To him who holds in his hands the Great Image (of the invisible

The unit concerns him who holds the Great Image of the invisible Tao in his hands. The whole world repairs itself around him, and men resort to him without hurt, finding rest, peace, and ease. Music and dainties can make a passing guest stop briefly, but the Tao seems insipid, without flavour, and not worth looking at or listening to. Despite this appearance, the use of the Tao is inexhaustible.

36. 1. When one is about to take an inspiration, he is sure to make a

The unit describes a procedure called 'Hiding the light (of his procedure).' It explains that weakening follows strengthening, overthrowing follows raising up, and despoiling follows making gifts. It states that the soft overcomes the hard and the weak overcomes the strong. It also says that fishes should not be taken from the deep and that instruments for the profit of a state should not be shown to the people.

37. 1. The Tao in its regular course does nothing (for the sake of

The unit concerns the Tao, princes and kings, and the effects of maintaining the Tao. The Tao does nothing for the sake of doing it, and therefore there is nothing it does not do. If princes and kings maintained the Tao, all things would transform themselves through them. The speaker says that if this transformation became an object of desire, the desire would be expressed through nameless simplicity, which is free from external aims and allows all things to proceed as they will.

PART II. / 38. 1. (Those who) possessed in highest degree the attributes (of the

The unit concerns those who possess the attributes of the Tao, benevolence, righteousness, and propriety, and the Great man. It contrasts the highest and lower degrees of possessing these qualities, especially whether they act without purpose or constantly seek to display and carry them out. It describes propriety as an attenuated form of leal-heartedness and good faith and as the beginning of disorder, while swift apprehension is a flower of the Tao and the beginning of stupidity. The Great man abides by what is solid and chooses the fruit over the flower.

39. 1. The things which from of old have got the One (the Tao) are--

The unit concerns the One (the Tao) and the things that receive their qualities from it. Heaven becomes bright and pure, Earth firm and sure, spirits powerful, valleys full, and creatures alive through the One (Tao). Princes and kings receive from it the model they give to all, but without moral sway they decay. The text teaches that dignity and loftiness depend on humble foundations, which princes and kings acknowledge by calling themselves lowly names.

40.

The unit describes the movement and workings of the Tao. It says the Tao proceeds through contraries, with weakness marking the course of its mighty deeds. All things under heaven sprang from the Tao as existing and named. Existence sprang from the Tao as non-existent and not named.

41. 1. Scholars of the highest class, when they hear about the Tao,

The unit concerns the Tao and how different classes of scholars respond when they hear about it. Scholars of the highest class earnestly practice the Tao, while middle-class scholars alternate between keeping and losing it, and lowest-class scholars laugh at it. The text describes the Tao through paradoxes, portraying it as hidden, nameless, difficult to perceive, and associated with humility, simplicity, and incompleteness. Despite being hidden and nameless, the Tao skilfully imparts what all things need and brings them to completion.

42. 1. The Tao produced One; One produced Two; Two produced Three;

The Tao produces One, One produces Two, and Two produces Three, while Three produces All things. All things leave behind Obscurity, move toward Brightness, and are harmonised by the Breath of Vacancy. Men dislike being orphans, having little virtue, or being carriages without naves, although kings and princes use these designations for themselves. The text teaches that some things are increased by being diminished, that violent and strong do not die their natural death, and that this is the basis of the teaching.

43. 1. The softest thing in the world dashes against and overcomes the

The softest thing in the world dashes against and overcomes the hardest. That which has no substantial existence enters where there is no crevice. The speaker says this shows the advantage of doing nothing with a purpose. Few in the world attain to the teaching without words and the advantage arising from non-action.

44.

The unit compares fame, life, and wealth, asking which a person should value most. It argues that keeping life while giving up fame and wealth avoids greater sorrow and pain. It teaches that pursuing fame or large stores means rejecting greater riches, while contentment and knowing when to stop protect a person from shame, blame, and danger.

45.

The unit states that a person who considers great achievements poor will preserve vigour for a long time. It presents treating fullness as emptiness, ongoing exhaustion as unable to stop the flow, and straightness, great art, and eloquence as their apparent opposites. It also says that constant action overcomes cold, while stillness overcomes heat. Purity and stillness provide the correct law for everything under heaven.

46. 1. When the Tao prevails in the world, they send back their swift

The unit concerns the Tao's influence in the world and the conduct that follows when it prevails or is disregarded. When the Tao prevails, swift horses are sent back to draw dung-carts. When the Tao is disregarded, war-horses breed in the border lands. The text condemns ambition, discontent with one's lot, and the wish to be getting, and teaches that the sufficiency of contentment is enduring and unchanging.

47. 1. Without going outside his door, one understands (all that takes

The unit concerns understanding the world and the Tao of Heaven without leaving one's door or looking out from one's window. It says that the farther one goes out from himself, the less he knows. The sages gained knowledge without travelling, named things without seeing them, and accomplished their ends without intending to do so.

48. 1. He who devotes himself to learning (seeks) from day to day to

The unit contrasts learning with devotion to the Tao. He who devotes himself to learning seeks from day to day to increase his knowledge, while he who devotes himself to the Tao seeks to diminish his doing. Doing is diminished repeatedly until non-action is reached, at which point nothing remains undone. All under heaven is obtained by he who gives himself no trouble with that end.

49. 1. The sage has no invariable mind of his own; he makes the mind

The unit concerns the sage and how he relates to the people. The sage adopts the people’s mind as his own and treats both the good and not good, and the sincere and not sincere, with corresponding goodness and sincerity so that all become good and sincere. Though he appears indecisive and indifferent, the people watch him closely, and he treats them all as his children.

50. 1. Men come forth and live; they enter (again) and die.

The unit concerns Men and the different ways they relate to life and death. It states that some become ministers of life, some ministers of death, and some seek life while moving toward death through excessive efforts to perpetuate life. It also describes he who is skilful in managing life as able to travel among rhinoceros and tiger and enter a host without being harmed, because he has no place of death in him.

51. 1. All things are produced by the Tao, and nourished by its

This unit concerns the Tao and all things. The Tao produces, nourishes, forms, completes, matures, maintains, and overspreads all things. All things honour the Tao and exalt its outflowing operation through a spontaneous tribute. The Tao does not claim possession, vaunt its ability, or exercise control over what it brings to maturity.

52. 1. (The Tao) which originated all under the sky is to be

The Tao originated all under the sky and is considered the mother of them all. When the mother is found, one knows what her children should be, and guarding the qualities of the mother keeps one free from peril. Keeping the mouth closed and the portals shut brings exemption from laborious exertion, while promoting affairs through breath brings no safety. Perceiving what is small gives clear-sightedness, and guarding what is soft and tender gives strength; using light well and reverting to its bright source wards off blight and hides the unchanging from men's sight.

53. 1. If I were suddenly to become known, and (put into a position

The unit concerns governing according to the Great Tao and the dangers of boastful display. It says the Great Tao is level and easy, but people prefer byways. It describes princes who maintain their courts while neglecting fields and granaries, indulge in luxury, and accumulate wealth, calling them robbers and boasters contrary to the Tao.

54.

This unit concerns Tao and its effects when cultivated within a person, family, neighbourhood, state, and kingdom. Tao is compared to skilful planting and careful cultivation that cannot be uprooted or taken away. When Tao prevails, families gain riches, neighbourhoods thrive, states find good fortune, and people prosper throughout the kingdom. The text says this effect is confirmed by observing different cases under the sky.

55. 1. He who has in himself abundantly the attributes (of the Tao) is

He who has in himself abundantly the attributes of the Tao is like an infant. The infant is protected from poisonous insects, fierce beasts, and birds of prey, and its weak bones, soft sinews, firm grasp, excited virile member, and unhoarse crying show the perfection of its physical essence and harmony. Knowing this harmony reveals the unchanging Tao, while all life-increasing arts turn to evil. When things become strong, they become old and soon end because this is contrary to the Tao.

56. 1. He who knows (the Tao) does not (care to) speak (about it); he

The unit concerns the Tao and the person who knows it. A knower of the Tao does not speak about it, keeps his mouth shut, closes the portals of his nostrils, and harmonizes himself with obscurity. This state of blunting sharp points, unraveling complications, attempering brightness, and agreeing with obscurity is called the Mysterious Agreement, and the person who embodies it is beyond ordinary distinctions and is the noblest man under heaven.

57. 1. A state may be ruled by (measures of) correction; weapons of

The unit concerns how a state and kingdom are governed. It says that correction and weapons may rule a state, and crafty dexterity may be used in war, but a kingdom becomes one's own through freedom from action and purpose. It explains that prohibitive enactments, implements for profit, crafty acts, and legislation bring poverty, disorder, strange contrivances, and thieves and robbers. A sage teaches that doing nothing of purpose, keeping still, avoiding trouble, and showing no ambition allow the people to become correct, rich, and primitively simple.

58.

The unit concerns government, correction, and the sage’s conduct. It says that government which meddles in everything often works ill, while seemingly unwise government may best supply goodness to the people. It explains that correction can turn into distortion and good into evil, amid persistent public delusion. The sage acts like a harmless square or corner: straightforward without license and bright without dazzling.

59. 1. For regulating the human (in our constitution) and rendering

The unit concerns moderation in regulating the human (in our constitution) and rendering proper service to the heavenly. Moderation effects an early return to man's normal state, which is described as the repeated accumulation of the attributes of the Tao. That repeated accumulation brings the subjugation of every obstacle to such return, whose limit is unknown. One who possesses the mother of the state may continue long, with deep roots and firm flower stalks securing enduring life.

60. 1. Governing a great state is like cooking small fish.

The unit compares governing a great state to cooking small fish. It says the kingdom should be governed according to the Tao so that the manes of the departed do not manifest or employ their spiritual energy to hurt men. Neither the manes nor the ruling sage injuriously affect each other, and their good influences converge in the virtue of the Tao.

61. 1. What makes a great state is its being (like) a low-lying,

The unit explains that a great state resembles a low-lying, down-flowing stream toward which the small states tend. It compares this principle to the female overcoming the male through stillness, which is treated as a kind of abasement. A great state gains small states by condescending to them, while small states win over a great state by abasing themselves. The great state seeks to unite and nourish people, while a small state seeks acceptance and service, and the great state must learn to abase itself.

62.

This unit concerns Tao and its honored place among all things. Its admirable words can purchase honour, and its admirable deeds can raise their performer above others, while it also guards bad men and effaces their ill. The sovereign, as the Son of Heaven, appoints three ducal ministers, yet an offering of great rank would be less valuable than a lesson of Tao. The ancients prized Tao because it could be obtained by seeking and could allow the guilty to escape their guilt, which is why all under heaven consider it most valuable.

63. 1. (It is the way of the Tao) to act without (thinking of) acting;

This unit concerns the way of the Tao and the conduct of the sage. The way of the Tao is to act without thinking of acting, conduct affairs without trouble, treat the small as great and a few as many, and recompense injury with kindness. The sage anticipates difficulty while things are easy and accomplishes great things by acting while they are still small. The unit teaches that difficult and great things arise from earlier easy and small states, and that the sage avoids difficulty by recognizing it even in what seems easy.

64. 1. That which is at rest is easily kept hold of; before a thing

The unit explains that things are easiest to control before they appear, become disorderly, or approach failure. It teaches that great structures and journeys begin from small beginnings. It says that acting with an ulterior purpose causes harm and that people often ruin affairs when they are near success. The sage avoids such action, desires what other men do not, learns what they do not, and supports the natural development of all things.

65. 1. The ancients who showed their skill in practising the Tao did

The ancients who practised the Tao sought to make the people simple and ignorant rather than enlighten them. The difficulty in governing the people is said to arise from their having much knowledge. Governing a state by wisdom is described as harmful, while not doing so is described as beneficial. The governor who understands these principles follows the model and rule of mysterious excellence, which leads people toward conformity with him.

66. 1. That whereby the rivers and seas are able to receive the homage

The unit explains that rivers and seas receive the homage and tribute of the valley streams because they remain lower than them. It presents the sage (ruler) as following the same principle by placing himself below men in words and behind them in person. Although he is above and before them, men do not feel burdened or injured, and because he does not strive, no one can strive with him.

67. 1. All the world says that, while my Tao is great, it yet appears

The world says that the speaker’s Tao appears inferior to other systems, but its greatness is what makes it seem so. The speaker holds three precious things: gentleness, economy, and shrinking from taking precedence of others. These qualities enable boldness, liberality, and highest honour, while abandoning them leads to death; gentleness also brings victory in battle and Heaven’s protection.

69. 1. A master of the art of war has said, 'I do not dare to be the

A master of the art of war advises acting as the guest rather than the host, preferring defense and withdrawal to initiating or advancing in conflict. This approach is described as preparing to fight and advancing against an enemy even when no visible weapons or enemy are present. The text states that lightly engaging in war is the greatest calamity because it risks losing the precious quality of gentleness, and that when weapons are crossed, the one who deplores the situation conquers.

70. 1. My words are very easy to know, and very easy to practise; but

The speaker says that the speaker's words are easy to understand and practise, yet no one can both understand and practise them. The words contain an originating and all-comprehending principle and an authoritative law, but men do not know these and therefore do not know the speaker. Few people know the speaker, and the sage is described as wearing a poor garment of hair cloth while carrying jade in the bosom.

71. 1. To know and yet (think) we do not know is the highest

The passage concerns knowing, the mistaken belief that one knows, and the disease arising from that mistake. It teaches that knowing while recognizing what one does not know is the highest attainment. Being pained by having the disease preserves a person from it. The sage understands the pain inseparable from the disease and therefore does not have it.

72. 1. When the people do not fear what they ought to fear, that which

The unit concerns the people, their fears, their ordinary life, and the sage. It warns that when people do not fear what they ought to fear, their great dread will come upon them. It advises them not to indulge thoughtlessly in ordinary life or act as though they are weary of what that life depends on. The sage knows these things, loves without appearing to value himself, and chooses the former alternative over the latter.

73. 1. He whose boldness appears in his daring (to do wrong, in

The unit contrasts a man who dares to do wrong in defiance of the laws with one who does not dare: the former is put to death, while the latter lives on. It questions why one outcome appears advantageous and the other injurious, since Heaven’s anger makes the cause difficult to discern. Heaven overcomes without striving, obtains replies without speaking, and draws people without calling. Its net is wide-meshed yet lets nothing escape.

74. 1. The people do not fear death; to what purpose is it to (try to)

The people do not fear death. The passage questions the purpose of frightening them with death and suggests that if they feared it, wrongdoing might be prevented through execution. It says that One presides over the infliction of death. It warns that anyone who inflicts death in place of One is like a person hewing wood instead of a great carpenter and may injure himself.

75. 1. The people suffer from famine because of the multitude of taxes

The unit concerns the people and the conditions affecting their lives. The people suffer famine because of the multitude of taxes consumed by their superiors. They are difficult to govern because of the excessive agency of their superiors. They make light of dying because of the greatness of their labours in seeking the means of living, and leaving living altogether out of view is said to be better than setting a high value on it.

76. 1. Man at his birth is supple and weak; at his death, firm and

The unit concerns Man, all things, trees, and plants in relation to life and death. Man is supple and weak at birth but firm and strong at death. Trees and plants are soft and brittle in early growth but dry and withered at death. Firmness and strength accompany death, while softness and weakness accompany life; what is firm and strong is below, and what is soft and weak is above.

77. 1. May not the Way (or Tao) of Heaven be compared to the (method

The unit compares the Way (or Tao) of Heaven to the method of bending a bow, in which what is high is brought low and what is low is raised. The Way (or Tao) of Heaven diminishes superabundance and supplements deficiency, unlike the way of man, which takes from those who have not enough to add to its own superabundance. The text asks who can use personal superabundance to serve all under heaven and answers that only one in possession of the Tao can do so. The ruling sage acts without claiming results, achieves merit without resting arrogantly in it, and does not display superiority.

78. 1. There is nothing in the world more soft and weak than water,

The unit concerns water, which is described as the softest and weakest thing yet able to overcome firm and strong things. It states that people know the soft overcomes the hard and the weak overcomes the strong, but cannot put this into practice. A sage teaches that accepting the state's reproach and bearing men's direful woes lead to being regarded as a ruler. The unit concludes that strictly true words seem paradoxical.

79. 1. When a reconciliation is effected (between two parties) after a

The unit concerns reconciliation between two parties after great animosity and the grudge that may remain in the mind of the one who was wrong. It explains that the sage keeps the left-hand portion of the engagement record and does not insist on its speedy fulfilment by the other party. He who has the attributes of the Tao regards only the conditions of the engagement, while he who lacks those attributes regards only conditions favourable to himself. The Way of Heaven has no partiality of love and is always on the side of the good man.

80. 1. In a little state with a small population, I would so order it,

The text describes how a ruler would organize a little state with a small population. The people would have no need to employ exceptional abilities, travel by boats or carriages, or use weapons. They would return to knotted cords, value simple food, clothing, dwellings, and ways, and remain isolated from a neighbouring state. Although the neighbouring state would be close enough for its fowls and dogs to be heard, the people would have no intercourse with it even in old age or at death.

81. 1. Sincere words are not fine; fine words are not sincere. Those

The unit contrasts sincere words with fine words and describes those skilled in the Tao as non-disputatious, while those who dispute are not skilled in it. It says that those who know the Tao are not extensively learned, and those who are extensively learned do not know it. The sage does not accumulate for himself; by expending and giving to others, he possesses and has more himself. The Way of Heaven injures not, and the sage does not strive in his doing.
