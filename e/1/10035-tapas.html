Abstract written by a model

Tapas are austerities practised by monks after being decided upon, particularly when avoiding neglect of self-control. They are twofold, external and internal, and each kind is sixfold. They matter because, together with control, they bring purity, destroy Karman, and are one of four causes of final deliverance. Monks consider their austerities after night duties, undertake them, and praise perfected saints. The records illustrate severe penance through Mrigâputra’s vows, meditation, purification, and monthly fast, and through Garga’s departure from laziness to lead a virtuous wandering life.

Narrative, section by section written by a model

6 sections; each heading opens the text

Nineteenth Lecture.

Mrigâputra observes the five great vows, five Samitis, and three Guptis, and practises mental and bodily penance. He meditates, purifies himself, and breaks his fast only once every month.

Twenty-Second Lecture.

Râgîmatî maintains self-control and her vows when Rathanêmi tempts her. Her words lead Rathanêmi to return to the Law, after which he protects his thoughts, words, and acts and subdues his senses.

Twenty-Sixth Lecture.

At the end of the night duties, a monk considers which austerities he will undertake. He then practises the austerities he has decided upon and praises the perfected saints.

Twenty-Seventh Lecture.

Garga decides to leave the lazy donkeys and practise severe austerities. He then wanders about on the earth, leading a virtuous life.

Twenty-Eighth Lecture.

Austerities are one of the four causes of final deliverance and are twofold, external and internal, each being sixfold. Through austerities one reaches purity and destroys Karman together with control.

Thirty-First Lecture.

Self-control should be practised, while neglect of self-control should be avoided.
