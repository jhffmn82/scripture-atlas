The Book of Mosiah

document · 1 work · 0 quoted facts · 0 connections

Abstract written by a model

These records summarize the Book of Mosiah, beginning with King Benjamin preparing his son Mosiah to rule. Benjamin teaches his people that they owe everything to God, must serve one another, keep the commandments, repent, care for the needy, and receive salvation through Jesus Christ. An angel’s message foretells Christ’s ministry, suffering, death, resurrection, and atonement. The people experience a mighty change, covenant to obey God, take upon themselves the name of Christ, and Mosiah becomes king after Benjamin dies. Mosiah sends Ammon to learn the fate of Zeniff’s people in Lehi-Nephi. There Ammon finds King Limhi’s people oppressed by the Lamanites. Their history recounts Zeniff’s settlement, early prosperity, wars, and the later wicked reign of Noah. Noah rejects the prophet Abinadi, whose teachings condemn disobedience and proclaim that God will come among humanity, suffer, redeem his people, overcome death, and judge all according to their works. Alma accepts Abinadi’s words; Abinadi is burned to death, while Alma forms a church among believers. Noah’s people suffer invasion, bondage, and internal conflict. Limhi succeeds Noah, and, with Ammon and Gideon’s counsel, leads his people in a secret escape to Zarahemla. Alma’s community at Helam is likewise enslaved under Amulon and the former priests of Noah, but God strengthens and delivers them to Zarahemla. Mosiah reunites these groups; Alma baptizes Limhi’s people and organizes united churches. The final events address challenges within the church: unbelief among the rising generation, sin, discipline, and persecution. Alma receives authority to forgive repentant people and exclude those who refuse repentance. Alma the younger and the sons of Mosiah initially oppose the church but repent after an angel’s appearance and become missionaries. Mosiah’s sons leave to preach among the Lamanites, while Mosiah translates ancient records and entrusts them to Alma. Finally, because his sons refuse kingship and Mosiah warns of its dangers, the people replace kings with elected judges, making Alma the first chief judge and ending the reign of the kings.

The Book of Mosiah / Chapter 1

King Benjamin teaches his sons, including Mosiah, Helorum, and Helaman, the language of their fathers, the records, and the commandments of God. He gives Mosiah charge to proclaim that the kingdom will be conferred upon him and that he will be a king and ruler over the people. King Benjamin also instructs Mosiah concerning the records, the sword of Laban, and the ball or director, and Mosiah proclaims the gathering of the people at the temple. The Lord has preserved the people when they keep the commandments, but transgression can cause them to become weak like the Lamanites.

Chapter 2

The people gather at the temple to hear king Benjamin, who speaks from a tower because the multitude is too great for the temple. King Benjamin teaches that serving others is serving God, that all people are indebted to God, and that keeping the commandments brings prosperity and blessings. He announces that Mosiah is now king and warns the people against obeying the evil spirit, transgressing, and dying unrepentant. He describes the guilt and punishment of those who rebel against God and the never-ending happiness of those who remain faithful.

Chapter 3

The angel tells the people that the Lord Omnipotent will come among them as Jesus Christ, perform miracles, suffer, die, and rise again. Jesus Christ’s blood atones for the sins of those who died without knowing God’s will, while salvation comes only through repentance and faith in his name. The people, prophets, and law of Moses testify of Christ’s coming, but the natural man remains an enemy to God unless he yields to the Holy Spirit. At the judgment, people are judged according to their works, and the wicked are consigned to misery, torment, and damnation.

Chapter 4

King Benjamin teaches the people about salvation through the atonement of Jesus Christ, repentance, faith, and obedience to the commandments of God. The people receive a remission of their sins and peace of conscience through their faith in Jesus Christ. He teaches that all people depend upon God and must care for the poor, beggar, and needy by sharing their substance wisely and in order. He warns that those who fail to watch their thoughts, words, deeds, and obedience to God must perish.

Chapter 5

King Benjamin’s people affirm that they believe his words because the Spirit of the Lord Omnipotent has wrought a mighty change in their hearts, removing their disposition to do evil and giving them a desire to do good. They enter into a covenant with God to do his will and obey his commandments throughout their lives. King Benjamin teaches that through this covenant they become children of Christ, take upon themselves the name of Christ, and may receive salvation and eternal life if they remain steadfast and abound in good works.

Chapter 6

King Benjamin finishes speaking to the people, takes the names of those who entered into a covenant with God, and consecrates his son Mosiah as ruler and king. The people covenant to keep God's commandments and take upon themselves the name of Christ, except for little children. Mosiah begins to reign, appoints priests to teach the people, and tills the earth so he will not become burdensome to them. King Benjamin lives three years after these events and then dies.

Chapter 7

King Mosiah sends Ammon and fifteen other strong men from the land of Zarahemla to inquire about the people who went to dwell in the land of Lehi-Nephi. Ammon and three companions are captured near the land of Nephi and brought before Limhi, who learns that their brethren in Zarahemla are alive. Limhi explains that his people are in bondage to the Lamanites and pay half of their produce, flocks, herds, and possessions as tribute. He teaches that their iniquities and transgressions brought affliction upon them, but promises that God will deliver them if they turn to him and serve him diligently.

Chapter 8

King Limhi tells his people about their brethren in the land of Zarahemla, and Ammon recounts what happened from Zeniff’s departure until Ammon’s arrival. Limhi’s people present plates, breastplates, and swords from a discovered land filled with bones and ruins, but no one can interpret the plates. Ammon explains that the king of the people in the land of Zarahemla is a seer who can translate ancient records through interpreters, a gift from God. Limhi rejoices because the records may reveal the identity and destruction of the people who made them.

Chapter 9

Zeniff leads a group from Zarahemla into the wilderness and obtains permission from king Laman to possess the land of Lehi-Nephi and the land of Shilom. Zeniff’s people build, repair walls, cultivate crops, and prosper, but king Laman intends to bring them into bondage. After twelve years, king Laman stirs up the Lamanites, who attack Zeniff’s people and take their flocks and crops. Zeniff arms his people, prays to God, and leads them to defeat the Lamanites, although 279 of his people are killed.

Chapter 10

Zeniff and his people establish and possess their land in peace for twenty-two years, preparing weapons, guarding the land, farming, and making cloth. After king Laman dies, his son stirs the Lamanites to war, but Zeniff’s spies discover their preparations and Zeniff organizes his people for battle. Zeniff teaches his people about the Lamanites’ traditions and urges them to trust in the Lord, enabling them to drive the Lamanites from the land with a great slaughter. Being old, Zeniff confers the kingdom upon one of his sons.

Chapter 11

Zeniff confers the kingdom upon his son Noah, who reigns wickedly, taxes the people heavily, supports priests and lavish building, and leads the people into sin, idolatry, and violence. The Lamanites attack Noah's people, and Noah's armies drive them back in a victory that causes pride and delight in blood. Abinadi prophesies that the Lord will punish the people with affliction and bondage unless they repent and turn to the Lord. Noah and the people harden their hearts against Abinadi and the word of the Lord, and Noah seeks Abinadi's life.

Chapter 12

Abinadi returns among the people in disguise and prophesies that the Lord will punish them for their iniquities and abominations unless they repent. The people seize Abinadi and bring him before king Noah, who imprisons him and has the priests question him. Abinadi rebukes the priests for failing to understand and keep the law of Moses, and he teaches that obedience to the commandments of God leads to salvation.

Chapter 13

Abinadi continues his message to king Noah and the people of king Noah after they cannot kill him because the Spirit of the Lord is upon him. He recites the commandments of God and condemns the priests for teaching iniquity instead of obedience. He explains that the law of Moses was given to a stiffnecked people as a strict law of performances and ordinances, but that salvation does not come by the law alone. He teaches that the atonement, the redemption of God, and the coming Messiah are necessary, and that the Messiah will bring to pass the resurrection of the dead.

Chapter 14

The unit concerns Isaiah’s prophecy about a suffering servant of the Lord. The servant is despised, rejected, wounded, bruised, oppressed, and led to death for the transgressions, iniquities, and sins of many. He bears griefs and sorrows, makes intercession for transgressors, and justifies many through his knowledge. After offering his soul for sin, he sees his seed, prolongs his days, and receives a portion with the great.

Chapter 15

Abinadi teaches that God shall come down among the children of men as the Son of God and the Father, suffer, die, and redeem his people. The prophets and all who believe their words and look forward to redemption are called the seed of the Lord and heirs of the kingdom of God. The Lord breaks the bands of death, brings the resurrection of the dead, and grants eternal life to those who have part in the first resurrection. Salvation shall be declared to every nation, kindred, tongue, and people, and the Lord shall bring again Zion and redeem Jerusalem.

Chapter 16

Abinadi teaches that all nations will see the salvation of the Lord and confess that God's judgments are just. He explains that all mankind became carnal, sensual, and devilish through the fall, but God redeemed his people from their lost and fallen state. Christ's coming into the world, his rising from the dead, and the resurrection overcome the grave and the sting of death. The dead will be judged according to their works, receiving either endless life and happiness or endless damnation. Abinadi teaches that redemption comes through Christ the Lord and calls people to repent while the arms of mercy are extended.

Chapter 17

Abinadi is brought before king Noah after being imprisoned and accused, and he refuses to recall his testimony that God himself should come down among the children of men. Alma believes Abinadi, pleads for him, is cast out, and records Abinadi’s words while hiding. King Noah delivers Abinadi to the priests, who bind and scourge him with faggots until he dies by fire. Before his death, Abinadi prophesies affliction and death by fire for the seed of those who destroy God’s people because they believe in the salvation of the Lord their God.

Chapter 18

Alma repented, taught Abinadi’s words privately, and gathered believers at Mormon to teach repentance, redemption, and faith in the Lord. He baptized about two hundred and four people, organized them as the church of God or church of Christ, and ordained priests to teach and serve. The people covenanted to serve God, keep his commandments, support one another, and stand as witnesses of God. After the king accused Alma of stirring rebellion, the king’s army pursued Alma and the people, who departed into the wilderness with their families.

Chapter 19

The army of the king returns after searching unsuccessfully for the people of the Lord, but division and contention arise among the remaining people. Gideon attacks king Noah, who escapes when the Lamanites invade from the land of Shemlon and leads the people into the wilderness. The Lamanites capture many of king Noah’s people, spare them after their fair daughters plead for them, and impose tribute while requiring king Noah’s surrender. After king Noah is killed and his priests flee, Limhi becomes king, establishes peace, and rules for two years under Lamanite guards and tribute.

Chapter 20

In Shemlon, the priests of king Noah abduct twenty-four daughters of the Lamanites while they are gathered to sing and dance. The Lamanites blame the people of Limhi and attack them, but the people of Limhi defend themselves and capture the wounded king of the Lamanites. Limhi learns that the priests committed the abduction, and Gideon urges him to explain this to the king rather than punish his people. The king of the Lamanites is pacified, and both peoples meet without arms and return peacefully to their own lands.

Chapter 21

Limhi and the people of Limhi return to the city of Nephi but suffer oppression, afflictions, failed battles, and bondage under the Lamanites. After humbling themselves and crying to God, they begin to prosper, and the Lord softens the Lamanites’ hearts so their burdens are eased, though they are not yet delivered. Ammon and his brethren arrive from Zarahemla, and Limhi learns that king Mosiah can interpret the record brought from a destroyed land covered with dry bones. Limhi and many of his people covenant with God and desire baptism, but Ammon declines to perform it because he considers himself unworthy and lacks authority.

Chapter 22

Ammon and king Limhi consult the people of Limhi about escaping bondage, but they find no way to fight the numerous Lamanites. Gideon proposes sending wine to the Lamanites so their guards will become drunk, allowing the people of Limhi to escape through a secret pass by night. The people of Limhi depart with their families, flocks, herds, provisions, and precious things, travel through the wilderness around the land of Shilom, and reach the land of Zarahemla. Mosiah receives them as subjects, while the Lamanites pursue them but become lost in the wilderness.

Chapter 23

Alma leads his people into the wilderness, where they establish the land and city of Helam and prosper. Alma teaches them to reject kingship, love one another, and accept only just teachers and ministers. The Lord later softens the Lamanites’ hearts, but the Lamanites take possession of Helam and place Alma’s people under guards. Amulon and the priests of king Noah join the Lamanites and receive authority over people in Helam.

Chapter 24

Amulon gains favor with king Laman and appoints the brethren of Amulon as teachers over the Lamanites, but they teach worldly skills rather than the things of God. Amulon exercises authority over Alma and his people, persecutes them, and places them under harsh tasks and guards. God lightens their burdens, promises deliverance, and leads Alma and his people out of bondage into the wilderness. After twelve days, Alma and his people arrive in the land of Zarahemla, where Mosiah receives them with joy.

Chapter 25

King Mosiah gathered the people of Nephi and the people of Zarahemla and caused the records of Zeniff and the account of Alma and his brethren to be read. The people felt joy for those delivered from bondage, sorrow for those slain by the Lamanites, gratitude for God's goodness and power, and anguish for the Lamanites. Alma preached repentance and faith on the Lord, baptized king Limhi and his people, and established churches throughout the land of Zarahemla. The churches were united as the church of God, and the Lord poured out his Spirit upon the people.

Chapter 26

The rising generation does not believe the traditions of their fathers, including teachings about the resurrection and the coming of Christ, and many refuse baptism and church membership. Dissensions and flattering words lead some church members into sin, so Alma brings them before King Mosiah and then inquires of the Lord about how to judge them. The Lord confirms Alma’s authority, explains that the church is his, and commands that those who repent and confess their sins be forgiven and received, while those who will not repent are excluded. Alma records and applies the Lord’s commandments, after which the church has peace, prospers, and continues teaching and baptizing.

Chapter 27

Persecutions by unbelievers caused the church of God to complain, leading king Mosiah to prohibit persecution and command equality, humility, and self-support. Alma (son) and the sons of Mosiah secretly sought to destroy the church, but the angel of the Lord appeared, rebuked Alma, and called him to repentance. After fasting and prayer, Alma recovered his strength, declared that he had been born of God and redeemed, and began preaching the word of God with the sons of Mosiah. They traveled throughout the land, repaired injuries done to the church, and brought many people to the knowledge of the truth and their Redeemer.

Chapter 28

The sons of Mosiah ask king Mosiah to let them go to the land of Nephi to preach the word of God among the Lamanites, and the Lord approves their request. They hope to bring the Lamanites to knowledge of the Lord, cure their hatred toward the Nephites, and help establish peace. King Mosiah translates the plates of gold with the interpreters, learning the history of the destroyed people and causing the people to mourn and gain knowledge. He then gives the records and interpreters to Alma the younger and commands him to preserve them and keep a record of the people.

Chapter 29

Mosiah asks the people whether they want a king, but after Aaron and Mosiah’s other sons decline the kingdom, he sends a written message explaining the dangers of kingship. The people accept Mosiah’s proposal to choose judges, and Alma becomes the first chief judge while peace continues through the land. Mosiah and Alma’s father die, and the reign of the kings ends as the reign of the judges begins among the Nephites.
